## 1. Coopetition

### 1.1 What is claimed

Coopetition names the simultaneous pursuit of cooperation and competition. **[D]** B1: "This phenomenon, known as coopetition, captures the simultaneous pursuit of cooperation and competition between organisations." **[D, definitional]** The most-quoted definition in the field is Bengtsson & Kock's: "a dynamic and paradoxical relationship, which arises when two companies cooperate in some areas (such as strategic alliances), but simultaneously compete in other areas" — but note carefully: **I did not retrieve Bengtsson & Kock (2000)**. That wording reaches me as a quotation inside B2, i.e. **[D secondhand]**. Its origin claim is itself secondhand: B1 attributes the concept's introduction to Brandenburger & Nalebuff (1996) **[C of B1, not verified]**.

The field's current claim, at review level, is that coopetition is manageable rather than resolvable: tensions are reframed as "productive inputs for learning, capability development, and adaptability" **[D]** and "sustainable coopetition" as "a dynamic capability grounded in continuous recalibration of cooperation–competition balances" **[D; C of B1]**.

### 1.2 What evidence, at what level

- **Review/synthesis, no effect sizes [D].** B1 analyses 2,104 articles (1996–2024) by bibliometrics and LDA topic modelling over abstracts. It reports topic shares, themes and trajectories. It reports **no** pooled effect, no stability rate, no failure rate.
- **Single study, qualitative [D].** B2: 102 recorded meetings, 82 hours, 17 coded conversations, one mid-sized US nonprofit. It documents *how employees talk* the tension into being manageable: moral reframing ("It's not about [Riverbend Alliance]. It's about the clients we serve"), admiration-and-replication, and boundary-setting.
- **The performance claim is secondhand [C].** B1 reports that "Bouncken et al. (2015) found that firms engaged in coopetition achieved higher levels of innovation and market success compared to those that relied solely on competition or cooperation." I did not retrieve Bouncken et al. This is the kind of sentence a downstream report is most likely to misquote as "research shows coopetition improves outcomes." **[I]** It is one sentence, sourced to one study I have not read, inside a review that explicitly declines to report effect sizes.

### 1.3 Strongest counter-evidence

Three, in ascending order of force.

1. **Stability is not the field's conclusion — it is the field's problem.** **[D]** B1 §5.4.4: "Our review argues that stability in relationships can be a risk rather than a solution." **[D]** B1: "sustaining this balance is difficult, as the same relationships that enable value creation also contain inherent risks of conflict, tensions, opportunism, and resource leakage." **[D]** B1 §5.4.3: "not all tensions are equally productive."
2. **The empirical record on coopetition is a record of management practices, not of outcomes.** **[I]** Both B1 and B2 study how actors *handle* the tension; neither studies whether the collaborations survive, deliver, or dissolve. A study that cites them for "coopetition works" is citing process evidence for an outcome claim.
3. **The asymmetric-partner question is not answered.** **[U]** I searched B1 for asymmetry: one occurrence, naming "power asymmetries" as a modifier of relational composition, in a sentence my extraction truncated. No retrieved source measures whether coopetition between a 145-hectare estate and a 100 m² volunteer garden is more fragile than between equals. **[U] "Coopetition is more fragile between unequal partners" is not established in the sources I retrieved.** The nearest support is indirect and comes from another literature (B3 on power imbalance, §4 below) which is about *tie formation*, not tie durability.

### 1.4 Does the literature support "collaborating with a competitor for the same resources is normal"?

**Partly, and only at the level of the nonprofit field, not the firm.** **[D]** B2's abstract states exactly our situation as a sector-level norm: "Nonprofit organizations are expected to collaborate with peers to advance social good while simultaneously competing for scarce resources such as funding, volunteers, and legitimacy." That is the single most directly usable sentence I retrieved. **[I]** It supports the *vocabulary* of our study and the claim that the tension is a recognised, ordinary feature of the field — not that any particular pairing in Eindhoven is stable, and not that any of them experiences it as coopetition rather than as indifference or avoidance.

**Claimed conditions (as the literature states them) [D; C]:**
- separation by domain, time or organisation — cooperation in one activity, competition in another (B2 reporting Hoffmann et al. 2018; B2 reporting Bunger et al. 2014);
- trust and relational governance, paired with the warning that trust and tension co-evolve rather than trade off (B1);
- explicit boundary rules about "when, where, and with whom" each is appropriate (B2's third theme);
- preference for competing with non-collaborators: "it was better to compete with non-collaborators" **[D]** — the case's own ordering, not a tested proposition.

**[I]** Note what these conditions have in common: every one of them is a *practice* that an organisation can adopt. None is a structural predictor. That makes the list a good source of design questions for Eindhoven and a bad source of expectations about which pairs will hold.

### 1.5 Transfer test — seven nodes, unequal, one city

**Licenses.** (i) Using "coopetition" as a name for a pattern the study may *observe* — a pair that shares a volunteer pool and competes for the same municipal grant — provided the study reports the observation, not the label, first. (ii) A checklist of the four claimed conditions (domain separation, trust, boundary rules, preference-ordering among partners) as questions to ask of each pair. (iii) The warning that collaboration and competition are not opposites to be traded off, so an initiative that both cooperates and competes is not being incoherent.

**Does NOT license.** (i) Any expectation that collaborating-with-a-competitor is *stable* in this network: the strongest synthesis in the field says stability can be the risk **[D]**. (ii) Any transfer of the firm-level performance claims (innovation, market success) to voluntary gardens whose "performance" is neither sales nor patents — and those claims are secondhand anyway. (iii) Any claim that unequal size makes coopetition more fragile: **[U]** not established. (iv) Any use of the concept to *explain* an observed conflict: coopetition is a description of a dyadic pattern, and the literature's own evidence is about how actors talk about it.

**Severity check.** Is this more than vocabulary? Marginally. It supplies one substantive, non-obvious proposition: that a partner you compete with is the *preferred* counterparty over a stranger (B2's giving hierarchy) — a genuine hypothesis-generator for a network where the closed food forest and the volunteer gardens share a volunteer base. It supplies no prediction about which of the seven pairs will hold.

**Verdict: usable as framing, and as a hypothesis-generator — not as a source of predictions about stability or outcomes at this scale.**

## 2. Organisational ecology / niche theory and resource partitioning

### 2.1 What is claimed

Three nested claims. **(a) Niche and competition.** An organisation's *realized niche* is the set of resources it actually taps; its *fundamental niche* is what it could tap absent competitors; "Two organizations compete if and only if their fundamental niches 'overlap'" **[D, B5 reconstructing Hannan & Freeman]**. **(b) Density dependence.** At carrying capacity, perturbations of competition are dampened and "competition returns to an equilibrium state" **[D, B5]**; founding and mortality rates respond to population density **[C, B5 background]**. **(c) Resource partitioning.** "Increasing concentration enhances the life chances of specialist organizations" **[D, B5 quoting Carroll & Hannan 1995 p. 217]**; the mechanism is that scale economies leave a few generalists at the centre, and "This lessened crowding of generalists and their move to the center opens up small pockets of resources on the periphery of the market, and it is here that specialist forms usually appear and thrive" **[D]**.

So the theory's answer to "specialise or displace?" is: **both, in sequence, and conditionally on concentration.** When the market is not concentrated, "specialist organizational forms will not do as well" **[D]**.

### 2.2 What evidence, at what level

- **Review/reconstruction [D].** B5 is a formal-logic reconstruction of resource partitioning from Carroll & Hannan (1995 pp. 215–221) — 92 sentences reduced to 7 core sentences, then axiomatised and machine-checked for consistency and soundness. It is strong on the theory's *internal* structure.
- **The empirical list is secondhand [C].** B5 enumerates corroboration across nine industries — "car industry (Hannan et al. 1995), banking … newspapers (Carroll 1985) … auditing (Boone, Bröcheler, and Carroll 2000) … the American feature film industry (Mezias and Mezias 2000)". **I verified none of these studies.** **[U]** at primary level.
- **No meta-analysis retrieved [U].** I did not find, in the timebox, a quantitative synthesis of partitioning results, nor any study applying partitioning to a nonprofit or green-space population.

**A finding worth flagging to the Lead [C of B5, [I] as to consequence].** B5 argues that the theory's *actual* driver is economies of scale, not concentration: "not concentration but economies of scale cause resource partitioning and the proliferation of specialists." **[I]** If that is right, the theory's core mechanism is a cost structure that voluntary gardens and a care estate do not share, which weakens the analogy before it starts.

### 2.3 Strongest counter-evidence

1. **The theory assumes inertia — the opposite of what a small network of adaptive, volunteer-run organisations does.** **[D]** B5: "organizations are seen as inert … Generalists do not become specialists or vice versa." **[I]** The seven Eindhoven nodes are precisely the kind of organisations the theory says are *not* the units it models: they change scope, merge identities (Voedselbos 2.0), and close. Applying partitioning here imports an assumption the case violates.
2. **The theory needs a population, a resource base with a measurable centre and periphery, and concentration. None of these exist here.** **[I]** Resource partitioning is a claim about *vital rates in populations* — foundings and failures across dozens or hundreds of organisations competing for one measurable resource base. Seven nodes, each with a different funder, a different legal form and a partly different resource (municipal ground vs. care contracts vs. grant money vs. volunteers), are not a population. **[D]** B5 records that concentration is "usually defined as the ratio of the aggregate size of the (3 or 4) largest firms to that of all firms in the population" — a statistic that is undefined for n = 7 with incommensurable "sizes" (hectares vs. staff vs. volunteers).
3. **Partitioning predicts coexistence only where generalists have vacated the periphery by moving to the centre.** **[I]** In Eindhoven the largest nodes have *not* vacated the periphery: the municipal estate and the care estate both run public-facing, volunteer-involving, nature-and-wellbeing programming — which is where the small gardens live. That is niche overlap, not partitioning. The theory, read honestly, predicts competition for exactly the resources our study suspects are contested.

### 2.4 Transfer test — seven nodes, unequal, one city

**Licenses.** (i) Two genuinely useful analytic distinctions: *realized vs. fundamental niche*, and niche *overlap* as the definition of competition **[D]**. These let the study say precisely what it means by "compete" — the same resource, actually tapped. (ii) The insight that a big generalist's move to the centre creates space at the periphery **[D]** — as a *question*: has any Eindhoven node vacated a niche, and did a small initiative move into it? (iii) The density intuition that scarcity plus crowding raises mortality **[C]** — consistent with the food forest's closure, but not derived from it.

**Does NOT license.** (i) Any prediction that the seven will *specialise into complementarity*. The theory's mechanism is selection on inert organisations, not strategic differentiation by reflexive ones; it predicts differential survival, not a division of labour arrived at by choice. (ii) Any use of "niche" as a metaphor for "what an organisation is good at". In the theory a niche is a resource set with an overlap measure; using the word without the measure is decoration. (iii) Any claim that "ecology predicts coexistence among unequal organisations" — the retrieved text says the opposite for the unconcentrated case. (iv) Any density-dependence estimate: **[U]** no retrieved source gives a functional form, parameters, or an application to this kind of population.

**Severity check.** This concept, used carefully, gives the study its sharpest vocabulary (overlap, realized vs. fundamental niche) and its most uncomfortable implication (the theory predicts competition where we hope for complementarity). Used carelessly — "organisational ecology tells us diverse niches coexist" — it is false to the source.

**Verdict: usable as framing for the definition of competition, and as a falsification prompt; NOT usable as a prediction that unequal organisations in a scarce niche will specialise.**

## 3. Nonprofit competition for volunteers, donors and legitimacy

### 3.1 What is claimed

Nonprofits compete for the same finite resources and are simultaneously rewarded for collaborating. **[D]** B2's abstract names the resource set almost exactly as this study does: "competing for scarce resources such as funding, volunteers, and legitimacy." **[D]** B2: "nonprofits compete for many of the same finite resources, including grants, donations, volunteers, media attention, and even clients", and "the same organizations must differentiate themselves to secure grants, donations, clients, and media attention."

On **legitimacy and "who speaks for the field"**, the retrieved evidence is thinner and mostly secondhand:
- **[D]** funders make collaboration itself a legitimacy marker: "Funders encourage coalitions, information sharing, and joint initiatives, framing collaboration as a marker of legitimacy."
- **[C of Barman 2002, reported by B2, unverified]** "nonprofits facing competition often differentiate themselves from peers to justify their claim to resources."
- **[C of Sharp 2018, reported by B2, unverified]** nonprofits are "averse to openly acknowledging competition, as doing so invites existential questions about their organizational identity and purpose."
- **[C of Gazley & Guo 2020, reported by B2, unverified]** "collaboration is often assumed to be inherently beneficial, despite evidence that it can strain resources or fail to produce desired outcomes."

**Organisational-category and field research — the literature that would actually support a claim that "a dominant organisation's categorisation determines what counts" — was NOT retrieved [U].** I did not obtain Pontikes, Hsu, Hannan-on-categories, or any DiMaggio-Powell field paper. **[U]** The proposition "the largest node's framing of 'green Eindhoven' determines what counts as green Eindhoven" has **no retrieved support** in this line. It is a plausible hypothesis, not a literature finding.

### 3.2 What evidence, at what level

- **Single study, qualitative [D].** B2 (as §1.2). Its evidential value for competition is illustrative: it shows employees of *one* organisation treating named peers as competitors for donors, and treating collaborators as shielded from competition.
- **Reviews, secondhand [C].** The sector-level generalisations in B2's literature review (Irvin 2010; Barman 2002; Gazley 2018; Paarlberg & Hwang 2017; Arenas et al. 2021) are real claims with real citations, but I retrieved none of the underlying papers. **[I]** Every one of the sentences our study would most like to use — "nonprofits compete for volunteers", "competition is reframed as community benefit" — currently rests on one qualitative case plus its author's summaries of others.
- **Meta-analysis on nonprofit competition or volunteer labour supply: not retrieved [U].** I found no meta-analysis, and no review specifically of volunteer-labour competition, within the timebox. **[U]** Whether competition for volunteers *harms or helps* outcomes is not established by anything I retrieved.

### 3.3 Strongest counter-evidence

1. **The literature's own warning against the "collaboration is good" premise [C].** Gazley & Guo, via B2: collaboration "can strain resources or fail to produce desired outcomes", and managers "may see pressure to collaborate as a burden instead of a potential synergy". **[I]** This is the sharpest available corrective to a study whose frame is complementarity: the field's own reviewers do not treat collaboration as a good in itself.
2. **Competition may be productive, not corrosive [I, from D].** B2 reports admiration-and-replication: employees adopting a rival's practices after praising them. **[I]** On this evidence, competition for recognition operates partly as a channel for practice diffusion. A study that frames competition only as a threat to complementarity would be flattening its own evidence base.
3. **The unit is wrong for "who speaks for the field" [I].** B2 studies intra-organisational talk, not field-level contests over category. Using it to claim anything about a city-level contest over "speaking for green Eindhoven" is a level jump with no retrieved bridge.

### 3.4 Transfer test — seven nodes, unequal, one city

**Licenses.** (i) Treating "competition for grants, volunteers, visitors, media attention and legitimacy" as a documented *sector* pattern rather than a suspicion the study invented **[D]**. (ii) The specific, checkable hypothesis that a partner is a *preferred* counterparty and a non-partner is fair game (B2's giving hierarchy **[D]**) — testable across the seven by asking who shares volunteers with whom. (iii) The legitimacy-as-funder-constructed point: if the municipal funder rewards collaboration, then collaboration is partly compliance, not affinity **[D; I]**.

**Does NOT license.** (i) Any claim that competition among the seven *harms* outcomes — **[U]**, no retrieved study measures this. (ii) Any claim about field-level category control or "who speaks for green Eindhoven" — **[U]**, the category/field literature was not retrieved and B2 cannot carry it. (iii) Any inference that an initiative that did not collaborate was unwilling: the same literature says competition is *disavowed* in public talk **[C]**, so non-collaboration is as likely to be prudence or capacity as preference **[I]** — see §6.

**Verdict: usable as framing for the resource list and for the legitimacy-as-compliance point; NOT usable for any claim about who legitimately "speaks for" the field, and not usable for harm-or-help outcomes.**

## 4. Resource dependence theory

### 4.1 What is claimed

Pfeffer & Salancik's central proposition, as stated by B3: "in order to reduce uncertainty in the flow of needed resources from the environment, organizations will try to manage their resource dependencies with a variety of tactics, such as coopting or absorbing the sources of dependence into the organizational structure" **[D]**. Dependence is defined relationally: actor *i* depends on *j* "in proportion to i's need for resources which j can provide, and (2) in inverse proportion to the ability of other actors to provide the same resources to i" **[D]**. Power "is a property of the social relation; it is not an attribute of the actor" (Emerson, quoted in B3) **[D]**.

The peer-reviewed review's abstract describes the field as organised around "the five options that Pfeffer and Salancik propose firms can enact to minimize environmental dependences: (a) mergers/vertical integration, (b) joint ventures and other interorganizational relationships, (c) boards of directors, (d) political action, and (e) executive succession" **[D, abstract-level only]**.

**The refinement that matters most for an unequal network [D].** B3 argues the original theory conflated two dimensions and that they have **opposite** effects: "Under conditions of power imbalance, the dependent organization will be willing, but not able to take action to reduce constraint"; "higher levels of an organization's asymmetric dependence on other organizations will actually reduce that organization's ability to take action to manage environmental dependencies" **[D; C of B3]**. Mechanism: "power imbalance reduces the frequency of exchange among social actors by hindering conflict resolution", plus legitimacy-and-fairness disputes over the division of payoffs **[D]**.

### 4.2 What evidence, at what level

- **Review/synthesis, abstract only [D].** B4 confirms the theory's scope and its five-response repertoire. Nothing beyond the abstract is attributed to it.
- **Single study [D].** B3's test: US inter-industry mergers and acquisitions, 1985–2000, 1,857 deals, industry-level input-output dependence measures; interdependence predicts M&A, power imbalance obstructs it **[C; I did not re-analyse the models]**.
- **The critique is at review level and is unusually blunt [D].** B3: RDT "stands in organizational discourse as an appealing general metaphor, rather than a foundation for testable empirical research"; "empirical support for the key prediction stemming from the theory is surprisingly limited".

### 4.3 Strongest counter-evidence

1. **The theory's own reviewers say its empirical support is thin [D].** This is the strongest objection available, and it comes from inside the tradition. **[I]** A study that borrows RDT's prestige to explain a seven-node network is borrowing from a theory its own adherents describe as a metaphor awaiting tests.
2. **The evidence is about mergers between US public companies [D].** **[I]** M&A is the paradigm case of "constraint absorption" — an option unavailable to a volunteer garden and undesirable for a municipal estate. The dependent variable does not exist in our setting, so the finding (power imbalance obstructs tie formation) transfers only as an analogy.
3. **The counter-prediction is real [I].** RDT's *original* formulation predicts that dependence drives tie-building; B3 says that prediction is why the empirical record is muddled. **[I]** So the honest statement is: RDT has two rival predictions about unequal dyads, and the study must choose and say so.

### 4.4 Transfer test — seven nodes, unequal, one city

**Licenses.** (i) A precise, non-obvious prediction our case can actually test: **the node that owns nothing — no land title, no payroll, ground and money both from the municipality — is predicted to be *willing but not able* to restructure its dependence**, i.e. to seek agreements, co-opt the funder, and still not change the dependency. **[D]** for the mechanism, **[I]** for the application. (ii) The claim that power is relational, not an attribute: the small gardens are not "weak"; they are weak *in relation to* specific resource holders, and may hold resources (volunteer hours, legitimacy, a story the funder wants) that the estates need **[D]** for the principle, **[I]** for the instantiation. (iii) A reason to expect *fewer* or *shallower* ties across the size gradient than a complementarity framing assumes **[D/I]** — which is a falsifiable expectation, not a decoration.

**Does NOT license.** (i) Any claim that the study has "applied resource dependence theory". With n = 7 and one city, this is a plausibility argument, not a test; the theory's own literature couldn't test it at n = 1,857 industries. (ii) Any inference that a dependent organisation's *strategy* reveals its *interests* — the theory explicitly separates motivation from ability **[D]**, which is precisely why behaviour cannot be read as preference (§6). (iii) Any quantitative statement about dependence: measuring "resource criticality" and "availability of alternatives" for seven organisations would require data the study does not have **[U]**.

**Severity check.** This is the one concept that yields a genuine, falsifiable, case-specific expectation. It is also the concept most vulnerable to being used as a label. The discipline required: state the prediction (willing-but-not-able), state what would disconfirm it (the landless node successfully restructures its dependence, e.g. by securing a lease or an independent funding stream), and do not cite RDT for anything else.

**Verdict: usable as framing and as the study's single best hypothesis-generator — with an explicit caveat that it is an analogy from M&A research, and that the theory's own reviewers call its empirical base thin.**

## 6. Stated versus revealed preference, and the inference from behaviour to interest

### 6.1 What is claimed

The distinction is between preferences as "states of mind" and choices as "actions", with a live dispute over whether choice can stand in for preference **[D]**. B6: "Mentalists, in contrast, insist that choices and preferences are entities of quite different categories: Preferences are states of mind whereas choices are actions." The revealed-preference programme "has been used to define preference in terms of choice" **[D]**, and behaviourists "reject the interpretation of preferences as causes of choice, instead insisting that they only capture choice patterns" **[D]**.

### 6.2 What evidence, at what level

**Review/synthesis of a conceptual literature — not empirical evidence [D].** Two retrieved arguments do the work our study needs:

1. **The inference problem is structural, not incidental.** Preferences exist over options one cannot choose: "the apparent existence of preferences over alternatives that one cannot choose between … This contradicts the claim that preferences exclusively transpire from choices" **[D]**. Hence observed choice under a restricted option set does not identify preference over the full set.
2. **Choice depends on the option set.** B6, quoting Sen: property α "has been contested with reference to cases when alternatives are preferred for their position in an alternative set, when the set of alternatives itself constitutes important information about the alternative chosen, or when certain alternatives provide the chooser with the freedom to reject them" **[D]**. Also: "total preferences are constructed at the moment of elicitation, and thus influenced by contexts and framings … not encoded in pre-existing partial preferences" **[D]**.

### 6.3 The warning our study actually needs

**[D] and decisive:** B6 observes that the mentalist camp "distinguish[es] between those agents who indeed have preferences as states of minds — e.g. humans, and maybe higher animals — and those agents who do not — e.g. **machines, plants or institutions**."

**[I]** Read plainly, the source our study would cite for "stated vs. revealed preference" treats **institutions as precisely the kind of entity to which preferences are not attributed**. That is a sharper and more uncomfortable point than "behaviour can reflect constraint": it is that attributing an interest or a want to an *initiative* — "Voedselbos really wants visibility", "the estate wants footfall" — is a category move requiring justification, not a description. Any "revealed priority" in this study is a property of a decision made by particular people under particular constraints, reported as such.

### 6.4 Strongest counter-evidence

**[I]** The counter-argument is that the study does not need mental states at all: it can describe *priorities revealed by allocation of scarce resources* (staff time, land, grant applications) without claiming anyone's preference. That is defensible — but only if the study then says "allocated to", not "wanted". The moment the text says an initiative "prioritised X over Y" it has made a preference claim, and B6's objections apply. **[D]** for the objections; **[I]** for the application.

### 6.5 Transfer test — seven nodes, unequal, one city

**Licenses.** (i) A disciplined two-column discipline for every claim: *what was said* (statements, plans, applications) versus *what was done* (staff time, land use, grant applications, closures). (ii) An explicit rule that behaviour under constraint is not evidence of preference, with the food forest's closure as the paradigm: closing is compatible with many preference orderings over an unconstrained option set, including "wanted to continue but could not". (iii) The constructivist point that a stated priority elicited in an interview is partly produced by the interview.

**Does NOT license.** (i) Any inference "they behaved this way, therefore they really want this" — **[D]** that inference is exactly what the retrieved source problematises. (ii) Treating a stated priority as the "real" one and behaviour as distortion, which simply inverts the error. (iii) Attributing preferences to organisations without naming the deciding humans **[D/I]**.

**Verdict: usable as framing — and it should be treated as a **prohibition**, not a lens. It is the most load-bearing item in this line because it constrains what every other concept may be used to say.**

## 7. Cross-cutting: what the whole set licenses, and what it does not

**Licenses (accumulated).**
1. A precise vocabulary: *niche overlap* (competition defined as tapping the same resource), *realized vs. fundamental niche*, *power imbalance vs. interdependence*, *coopetition*, *constraint absorption* **[D]**.
2. One substantive prediction worth testing: under power imbalance the dependent party is "willing, but not able" **[D]** — applied to the landless node.
3. One substantive corrective: collaboration is not a good in itself and stability can be the risk **[D]**.
4. One prohibition: do not read behaviour as preference, and do not attribute preferences to organisations **[D]**.
5. A reason to expect the network to be *less* complementary than the framing hopes: partitioning predicts competition where generalists have not vacated the periphery, and asymmetric dependence obstructs tie formation **[D/I]**.

**Does NOT license (the list a reviewer should check the final report against).**
