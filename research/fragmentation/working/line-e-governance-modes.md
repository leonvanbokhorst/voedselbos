## 1. Scope and brief

The Lead asked for one bounded line of work: make the contest between a "stronger centre" (a board that sets strategy plus an execution organisation that delivers) and a "missing middle" / polycentric reading **empirically serious**, and test whether shared accountability and contribution-based recognition can coexist with organisational autonomy and financial sustainability. Three sub-questions were authorised: **A** the strongest empirical case for centralisation on its own merits; **B** polycentricity as a genuine rival, including the critical literature; **C** contribution accounting, accountability regimes, recognition without money, and the financial sustainability of small organisations under collaborative requirements.

**Structural warning, stated before the findings.** This literature does not contain the test the regional debate is implicitly asking for. There is **no retrieved study** that compares a strategy-setting board plus an execution organisation against a polycentric arrangement on shared outcomes, in health promotion or anywhere else. What exists is: (i) comparative case evidence about *which governance arrangements coordinate* (mostly judged by informant perception, not outcome data); (ii) a large, methodologically uneven literature on *consolidating delivery organisations*, which is a different intervention from creating a coordinating centre; (iii) a quasi-experimental health-services literature on *service reconfiguration*, which is a different intervention again; and (iv) a normative/comparative literature on polycentricity whose own proponents describe its advantages as posited rather than demonstrated (E37).

The consequence for the parent report is blunt: **a verdict of the form "centralisation works" or "polycentricity works" is not available from this evidence base.** What is available is a set of *discriminating conditions* under which each has been observed to deliver or fail, and these conditions are about **functions** (who holds binding authority over resources; how complete the implementation is; how much coordination burden the arrangement imposes on the smallest participants) rather than about **forms** (whether a board exists). That is the finding I would defend, and §4 converts it into observations that could settle the question in one region.

**Transfer caveat for the whole line.** Almost none of this literature is Dutch and almost none is health promotion. What transfers is a *mechanism about the form of governance*: for example, that a coordinating body which sets plans but does not allocate budgets fails to bind its members (A5). What does not transfer is *magnitude* and *institutional context*: hospital merger cost figures, English NHS target regimes, Danish municipal sizes and Canadian regionalised mental-health systems all sit inside specific funding and accountability architectures. Per-source transfer notes are in §2; a consolidated warning is in §6.2.

**Exclusions observed.** No fieldwork, no stakeholder contact, no Dutch sources, no claim about which arrangement Eindhoven–De Kempen should adopt. I do not adjudicate the region; I supply the literature and the discriminating observations.

## 3. Appraisal

### 3.1 Family A — centralisation as a genuine rival

**Point A1 — The typology that the debate is borrowing from is not itself an empirical finding.** The Provan & Kenis (2008) article could not be read at any level (A1; see §6.1). What can be documented is that the same research programme treats mode-appropriateness as contingent (A2: form, voluntary-vs-mandated inception, developmental stage determine which criteria apply) and that insiders now describe the empirical literature applying the typology as suffering "persistent conceptual ambiguity" and a "lack of discernment between governance and management" (A3, preprint). The whole-network empirical literature is described by its own reviewers as "modest" (A4). So the typology supplies vocabulary, not a tested ranking. **Anything in the parent report that reads "research shows NAOs coordinate better" would be unsupported.**

**Point A2 — The strongest positive case for consolidated authority is a conditional one.** A5 is the single most directly relevant study retrieved: ten Canadian mental health networks, three governance forms, 96 informants. Corporate structure supported by regionalisation "offered the most direct means" to inter-organisational collaboration (E4). But the mechanism the authors identify is not the *existence* of a centre: it is the **alignment of budget authority with planning authority**. Where the province held the budgets and the network held the plan, "networks lacked a key governance lever" and hospitals "were not held accountable" (E5–E6). That is the most important sentence in this line for the regional proposal: **a strategy board without resource authority is a documented failure mode, not a weak version of a working model.** [D for A5's finding; [I] for the application to Eindhoven–De Kempen]

**Point A3 — There is one positive NAO outcome study, and it is real but narrow.** A7 (synthetic control, Chinese interjurisdictional rivers) reports a 36% pollution reduction after a provincially mandated NAO was created, and the authors themselves call it "preliminary" (E11). It shows an NAO *can* produce a measured shared outcome where the mandate gives it authority over the parties. It does not show that a centre without a mandate does so, and pollution is not participation, reach or health.

**Point A4 — Mandated centres are bargained, not designed.** A8 shows NAO size and remit follow power dependencies, including dependence on external non-members (E12). The centre is an actor in the system, not a neutral fixer of it. This matters because the regional proposal presents the board as an instrument; the evidence presents it as a settlement.

**Point A5 — The NAO failure mode is documented, though thinly.** A6 finds "strong inertial tendencies … especially network administrative organization models" (E8); its authors' literature summary names the risk that members "may not feel accountable for network choices" (E9, their summary, not their finding); and their discussion names a cost the proposal would create — the coordination required between the new centre and the existing regional authority (E10). Two Italian cases; informant perception; not an outcome test.

**Point A6 — Consolidating delivery organisations does not deliver the promised gains, and it costs autonomy and access.** This is the best-evidenced block in family A. A9 (102 English hospital mergers): no gains beyond reduced activity, financial performance down, waiting times up (E13–E14). A10 (nine NHS trust mergers): savings undershot the target by a third, "loss of managerial focus", smaller partners' staff reporting "a decrease in the autonomy of services and local decision making", and external agencies reduced to dealing with middle management (E15–E18). A11's realist synthesis adds the mechanism: mandated collaboration shifts the basis of cooperation from trust to contract and produces "collaborative inertia" (E19–E20). *Level discipline: these are organisation-level findings about merger. They are evidence that the consolidation of delivery units carries these costs; they are not evidence that a coordinating board carries them. The mechanism plausibly transfers — proximity of decision-makers to delivery, and partners' loss of a direct line — but the magnitude does not.*

**Point A7 — Economies of scale in authority have a democratic cost, but the cost is contingent.** A12 gives a causal, sizeable negative effect of jurisdiction size on citizens' internal political efficacy (E21). A13 finds no equivalent trust effect in Norway and attributes the Danish–Norwegian difference to implementation (E22). So "bigger unit ⇒ weaker local voice" is a documented risk, not a law; it is conditional on how the reform is made.

**Point A8 — Where centralisation demonstrably improved outcomes, the active ingredient was completeness and adherence, not the centre.** A14 is the strongest positive centralisation evidence retrieved: London's full-coverage hyperacute model reduced 90-day mortality; Greater Manchester's partial model did not (E23–E24). The authors' own explanation is "the lower level of adherence in Greater Manchester" and differential access (E25). A15 shows later Manchester gains concentrated among patients actually treated at the central unit (E26). *Level discipline: this is service reconfiguration, not governance. It establishes that centralisation can work at scale when it binds the pathway; it does not establish that a board produces coordination.*

**Point A9 — "The coordination body becomes another island" is documented as a pattern, not as a measured causal claim.** A16 (47 countries) reports platforms that "operate in isolation" and 34% of states running multiple fragmented mechanisms with minimal linkage (E27–E28) — no counterfactual, donor architecture, and internally inconsistent counts. A11 documents collaborative inertia (E20). B5 (§3.2) supplies the sharpest version: a new collaborative venue associated with *less* cooperation elsewhere (E40). No retrieved study measures a coordination layer *causing* worse coordination than its absence. **That specific claim remains [U].**

**Verdict, family A — contested; three sub-verdicts.**

- **Established [D]:** merging delivery organisations produces no reliable coordination, cost or quality gain and imposes access, autonomy and management-distance costs (A9, A10, A11).
- **Established [D], one comparative study:** consolidated authority coordinates better than shared governance *when it also holds the budget*; a planning-only body "lacked a key governance lever" (A5).
- **Documented [D] at service level:** centralisation with binding standards and complete implementation improves outcomes at scale (A14, A15); incomplete implementation produces no measurable gain.
- **Not established [U]:** that NAO or lead-organisation mode outperforms participant governance on outcomes. No clean comparative outcome test was retrieved, and the typology's own proponents question how modes are classified.
- **Not established [U]:** that a coordination body adds a layer without adding coordination, as a causal finding.

### 3.2 Family B — polycentricity as a genuine rival

**Point B1 — The founder of the concept treated its benefits as an open empirical question.** Ostrom's lecture defines polycentricity and immediately says whether the centres form an interdependent system "is an empirical question in particular cases" (E33); it reports the 1960s–70s US evidence that duplication "rarely occurred" and that "complexity is not the same as chaos" (E34–E35); and it closes by asking "how diverse polycentric institutions help or hinder" a list of outcomes (E36). *So "Ostrom showed polycentricity works" is a misreading; Ostrom showed that one specific charge — that plurality is chaotic and inefficient — did not hold in US metropolitan water and police services.*

**Point B2 — The advantages are posited, not demonstrated.** Carlisle & Gruby state it directly: "systematic development of polycentricity, including its posited advantages, is lacking" (E37). Anyone citing adaptive capacity, fit or redundancy as findings is citing the programme, not its results.

**Point B3 — The critical empirical literature documents four distinct failure modes.**

- *Crowding out.* B5: more cooperation in a collaborative venue is associated with less cooperation in other venues (E40). This is the strongest single piece of counter-evidence in the line, because it is the exact opposite of the intended effect.
- *Necessity without sufficiency.* B6: overlapping authority is "necessary—but not sufficient" for collective action (E41).
- *Power capture.* B7: higher-level actors take over or dismantle lower-level centres; cross-level linkages become "deliberately nonfunctional" (E42). B3 adds that many polycentric systems are "struggling" and that power is undertheorised (E38).
- *Masking.* B4: structural density and stability can coexist with declining effectiveness (E39); the richer the structure, the harder failure is to see.

**Point B4 — In health systems, the distributed-autonomy claim has not been shown.** B8 — the closest analogue to "distributed autonomy with shared rules" — finds "little rigorous evidence", mixed results, and only 10 of 16 studies at relatively low risk of bias (E43–E44). This is a systematic review of devolution in low- and middle-income countries, so the transfer to a Dutch regional setting is a transfer of *absence of evidence*, not of a negative finding.

**Point B5 — What the evidence does support on the polycentric side is conditional and managerial, not structural.** A18 (137 cases) identifies the conditions and the "small wins" trust cycle (E30); A19 finds network management and trust both predict outcomes, trust most (E31); A20 finds sustained collaborations were those where management "streamlined the coordination burden" (E32). None of these is evidence for *more nodes*; all are evidence for *better-managed relations between whatever nodes exist*. That is a material distinction the parent report should keep.

**Verdict, family B — contested; superiority not established either way.**

- **Established [D]:** polycentricity's posited advantages are not systematically developed, let alone demonstrated (B2); Ostrom's empirical base was US metropolitan services and she framed the question as open (B1).
- **Established [D]:** specific failure modes — crowding out across venues (B5), necessary-but-not-sufficient overlap (B6), power capture (B7, B3), masking of decline (B4).
- **Established [D]:** in health systems, the effect of devolution on performance and outcomes is not established (B8).
- **Established [D]:** the conditions associated with collaborative success are relational and managerial — trust, small wins, coordination-burden relief (A18, A19, A20).
- **Not established [U]:** that a polycentric arrangement produces better reach or outcomes than a single centre, in health promotion or in high-income health systems. **No retrieved study tests this head-to-head.**

### 3.3 Family C — can accountability and autonomy be reconciled?

**Point C1 — Accountability without consequences is not accountability.** Bovens's definition requires an obligation to explain, a forum that can judge, and consequences (E45). This is analytically decisive for the region's dilemma: a "light" accountability that asks initiatives to describe their contribution but attaches no consequence is not a weaker form of accountability — it is recognition, a different thing. Conversely, any arrangement in which a centre can act on the basis of an account is, by definition, a transfer of discretion. **The question is therefore not "can we have accountability without losing autonomy" but "which decisions does the centre get to make, and what is the smallest set of accounts needed for those decisions".** [I, from E45]

**Point C2 — Bovens also supplies the escape route, and it is a real one.** His three evaluative perspectives — democratic, constitutional and **learning** — generate *different* deficits (E46). An accountability design optimised for learning (what happened, what should change) is not the same design as one optimised for control (did you deliver what you promised). The literature retrieved supports the existence of the distinction; it does **not** contain a study showing that a learning-oriented regime has been operated at regional scale with small grantees. That is a design proposal, not a finding. [D for the distinction; [U] for the demonstration]

**Point C3 — Contribution analysis is a coherent method whose adoption is thinner than its citation count suggests.** The method is well specified (C3, C4: contributory causes "neither necessary nor sufficient", E48) and has been applied in results-based-management settings where a single organisation must show its contribution inside a partnership (C5, E50). But C5 states there have been "few examples … in practice" (E49); C6 documents application challenges (E51); C7 shows the methodological questions are still open (E52). **No retrieved source shows funders accepting a contribution claim in place of attribution.** [U] — this is the single largest evidential gap in family C, because the region's whole accountability design depends on it.

**Point C4 — Accountability regimes reliably change what is measured and reported, and less reliably what is done.** Two independent literatures converge:

- *Targets:* C8 documents measurement problems and gaming in the English public health target regime (E53).
- *Accreditation/recognition:* C10 (Cochrane, 2 studies, very low certainty) finds a 30-percentage-point gain in documented compliance and a **2.4% median gain in quality indicators** (E55), with only one of nine intervention hospitals actually achieving accreditation (E56). C11 finds "inconsistent findings … scant evidence" across 21 studies (E57). C12 finds voluntary healthy-outlet schemes certify about two-thirds of participants, with restaurants (the most autonomous, least supported outlets) faring worst (E58). C13 explains the mechanism: measures produce reactivity — "efforts to improve ranking factors without improving the characteristics the factors are designed to measure" (E59).
- *Reading across levels:* C10–C12 are organisation-level accreditation studies; the transfer to a regional recognition scheme is a transfer of *mechanism* (recognition attaches effort to documentation), not of magnitude. A voluntary regional award is far weaker than statutory hospital accreditation, and should be expected to produce less of everything.

**Point C5 — Multi-principal accountability is named more than measured.** C9's systematic review finds the accountability-overload field "still in its early stages, lacking consensus on terminology and a universal definition" (E54). The canonical framing article (C2) could not be retrieved and **is not used**. So the honest statement is: overload is a plausible, widely asserted mechanism with a thin empirical base — not an established effect. [D for the state of the field; [U] for the effect]

**Point C6 — Money without restrictions does not automatically become capacity — the strongest counter-intuitive finding in the line.** C14 (567 unrestricted gifts averaging ~97% of prior revenue, matched controls, DiD) finds recipients "kept the indirect cost ratio and spending relative to cash reserves constant" and that restricted funding had not produced a "starvation cycle" (E60); the money went to pay and onward grantmaking. This is a **preprint, not peer-reviewed**, and the recipients are large US nonprofits — nothing like a volunteer-run local initiative. But it directly undercuts the assumption, common in trust-based-funding advocacy, that removing restrictions will cause small organisations to invest in their own infrastructure. *Level discipline: organisation-level, large-N, US, big gifts. It cannot be used to predict what a €20,000 light-touch grant does to a Dutch volunteer group. It can be used to refuse the claim that unrestricted money reliably builds capacity.*

**Point C7 — The funder-commissioned evidence is attribution, not effect.** C15 reports 83% of Ford BUILD partners saying BUILD contributed "to a large extent" (E61) and documents decay when funding stopped — but the evaluator states the data "documented the grantee partners' experiences" and could not isolate BUILD's effect from other multiyear unrestricted funders. [C], not [D].

**Point C8 — Reporting and compliance costs are regressive, and the best evidence is qualitative.** C17 (19 Western Australian health-promotion practitioners — same field, same organisation types) documents reporting stacked across funders on top of re-application, evaluation described as "expensive" inside small grants, and — crucially — that "competitive tendering often undermined" collaboration that everyone said they wanted (E63–E64). C18 (14 nonprofits, diary study) finds grant *submission* costlier than reporting and costs "clearly regressive" (E65) — abstract only, magnitudes unread. C16's arithmetic (31% administrative cost against a 13% allowance) is practitioner evidence (E62), and C14 is the test that failed to confirm the starvation-cycle mechanism it implies. C19's funder-side number is the useful calibration: reporting reform in a well-resourced US foundation field moved 8 hours to 6 per funder (E66) — real, but small; the binding constraint is the **number of funders**, not the form.

**Point C9 — The mechanisms the brief hoped for do not have an evidence base.** Fiscal sponsorship (C20) is "seldom studied" and sits in the "dark gray fringes" (E67); a targeted search found **no** peer-reviewed evaluation of shared back offices, pooled reporting, or backbone/intermediary services for small groups. These are plausible designs supported by practitioner literature, not by evidence. [U]

**Verdict, family C — partially established; the trade-off is real where authority and money sit, and designable at the evidentiary margin.**

- **Established [D]:** accountability requires consequences (C1); contribution analysis is a specified method for non-attributable causal claims (C3, C4) but with few documented applications (C5) and no retrieved evidence of funder acceptance [U].
- **Established [D]:** recognition and inspection regimes raise documented compliance far more than measured quality (C10), and measurement produces gaming (C13, C8).
- **Established [D]:** reporting and compliance costs are regressive on small organisations, and collaboration requirements interact badly with competitive funding (C17, C18, C16).
- **Established [D]:** removing restrictions does not by itself produce capacity investment (C14) — a counter-intuitive finding that constrains the reconciliation story.
- **Contested / not established [U]:** accountability overload as a measured effect (C9); any evaluated effect of prizes/awards as distinct from accreditation; any evaluative evidence on fiscal hosting, shared back offices or pooled reporting.
- **The explicit tension.** The literature supports reconciliation **on the evidentiary dimension** — a centre can accept a contribution claim instead of an attribution claim, and can adopt a learning rather than a control perspective. It does **not** support reconciliation **on the authority dimension**: where a centre must bind members' behaviour, someone must hold resource authority, and A5 shows that without it the arrangement does not bind. And it does not support reconciliation **on the transaction-cost dimension**: reporting has fixed costs that fall hardest on the smallest participants, and the achievable saving from better reporting practice is modest (C19). The trade-off is therefore real and cannot be designed away at the point where binding decisions and money meet; it can be substantially reduced at the points where evidence is specified, where costs are borne, and how many funders demand an account.

A third explanation must be kept in play, because the parent corpus already documents it: **the money is the constraint** — short-term, compartmentalised national and municipal funding produces the fragmentation, and no governance form fixes that.

## 5. The autonomy / financial-sustainability tension, stated honestly

**5.1 What can be reconciled.**

- **The claim type.** Attribution ("your organisation produced this outcome") is not empirically available for multi-actor work in this field; contribution ("your work was a contributory cause, neither necessary nor sufficient") is a specified alternative (E47–E48). A centre can require contribution claims without requiring exclusive attribution. That is a genuine reconciliation, and it is the strongest one the literature offers.
- **The purpose of the account.** Bovens's learning perspective is a different design from his democratic and constitutional perspectives (E46). If the centre's decisions are about what to fund next and what to stop, a learning account suffices; if they are about sanction, it does not.
- **Who bears the transaction cost.** Reporting cost is a fixed cost and therefore regressive (E65); stacking multiple funders' formats multiplies it (E63). One shared account, one format, one deadline, and the consortium rather than the smallest partner bearing the assembly cost, are all supported as *design directions* — though the only measured saving retrieved is 8→6 hours per funder (E66), which is real but small. [D for the direction; magnitudes modest]
- **Recognition's realistic job.** Recognition reliably moves documented conformity (E55) and can be run in a way that supports participants (fewer participants, more support, E58). It should not be expected to move practice or substitute for resources (E57, E59).

**5.2 What cannot be designed away.**

1. **Binding requires authority.** If the centre is to make any decision that changes what partners do, someone must hold resource authority; where planning and budget were split, the arrangement did not bind and members were not held accountable (E5–E6). Autonomy in the sense of "no decision by anyone else about my resources" is incompatible with a centre that coordinates anything. This is a real transfer of discretion, and no reporting design removes it.
2. **Measurement changes behaviour in the direction of the measure.** The stronger the consequence attached to an account, the more the account becomes the work (E53, E59). A contribution regime with hard consequences is a target regime with extra steps.
3. **Fixed compliance costs are regressive.** Small organisations pay proportionally more for the same account (E65), and the achievable saving from better reporting practice is bounded (E66). Light-touch funding does not automatically convert into capacity (E60). So a design that requires small organisations to demonstrate contribution in order to be recognised will systematically favour those with back-office capacity — the exact organisations the region is trying not to privilege.
4. **Competitive funding corrodes collaboration, including collaboration the funder says it wants.** Documented in health promotion directly: "competitive tendering often undermined it in practice" (E64). A centre that both coordinates and allocates competitive funds reproduces this contradiction inside itself.

**5.3 The honest bottom line.** The literature supports a qualified yes on **accountability-with-autonomy as an evidentiary arrangement** (contribution claims, learning purpose, shared and minimal reporting, recognition scaled to support capacity) and a no on **accountability-with-autonomy as an authority arrangement**. The two are separable in design and inseparable in practice if the centre is also the funder. Put crudely: *you can have light accounts and local autonomy, or binding coordination and local discretion over resources, but the same body cannot credibly offer both.* This is an inference from the retrieved evidence (A5, C1, C10, C13, C17, C18), not a finding of any single study. [I]
