**One structural warning before the findings.** The parent question contains two halves that the literature answers with very unequal strength. The *conditions* under which partnership and coordination "work" are documented extensively. The *effects* of partnering on health or organisational outcomes are documented thinly and inconsistently — several of the strongest reviews say so explicitly in their own conclusions (Corbin 2018, Hughes 2020, Bickerdike 2017). Any argument from "research shows partnership works" is therefore not supportable from this evidence base. An argument from "research identifies these conditions, and the effect evidence is weak and mixed" is supportable.

## 1. Memo (≈700 words)

### 1.1 Funding and accountability

Projectification is established as a governance form with identifiable effects. Nilsen & Feiring's qualitative study of Oslo's public health plan (13 interviews, three districts, 2017–2020) locates a principal barrier in "tensions between the time-limited implementation process and the permanent organisational structures" **[D]**. Godenhjelm, Lundin & Sjöblom conclude that without "interlinking mechanisms between temporary and permanent structures" projects "may fragment the ability of permanent organisations for maintaining coordination and continuity" **[D]**. Penkler et al.'s Austrian case shows the project form narrowing content: making "diversity" doable produced "seemingly clearly delineated target groups" **[D]**. Hubmann's 14-month Sierra Leone ethnography shows funder time frames actively contested by a district management team **[D]**. Maier et al. (599 sources) map funder-driven "business-like" change and its contested effects **[D; contested]**.

On output requirements, one strong transfer: Cerasoli et al.'s 40-year meta-analysis (k = 183, N = 212,468) finds intrinsic motivation predicted performance at ρ = .21–.45, but "in a 'crowding out' fashion, intrinsic motivation was less important to performance when incentives were directly tied to performance" **[D at individual level, work/school/physical domains; [I] if extended to inter-organisational funding]**. No retrieved study tests whether handoff or stopping is rewarded or penalised in health-promotion funding **[U]**.

### 1.2 Institutional boundaries and survival

Resource-dependence effects are established but cross-sectional. Guo & Acar (95 US charitable organisations) find the *formality* of collaboration rises with age, budget size and board linkages, and — counter-intuitively — with reliance on *fewer* government funding streams **[D]**. Foster & Meinhard (645 Canadian nonprofits) find size and type predict formal collaboration as mediated by perceived environmental pressure **[D]**. Professional identity and information exchange are two of seven themes in Nooteboom et al.'s 55-study review of integrated youth care **[D salience; [U] causal weight]**. The strongest boundary finding is negative: Hughes, Shaw & Greenhalgh's review of 71 sources concludes integrated care programmes "did not necessarily lead to the changes intended" **[D]**.

### 1.3 Human and relational

Evidence exists at three levels and must not be pooled. *Individual:* the psychological-ownership meta-analysis (141 studies) finds control, knowing, investment and "safety" as antecedents, and dark-side "territorial behaviours" among outcomes **[D]**. *Group:* status conflict "exerts a significant negative main effect" and "hurts performance by undermining information sharing more than other types of conflict do" **[D]**. *Network:* trust and network management both predict outcomes across 50 and 28 effect sizes, trust mattering most, with combined strategies outperforming single ones **[D]**. Founder identity, belonging and recognition were **not** established **[U]** — see §6.

### 1.4 Beneficial plurality and coordination models

Corbin, Jones & Barry's scoping review (26 studies) yields nine process elements but concludes "there is limited empirical study of the effectiveness of different types of partnerships" **[D]**. Effect evidence is thin: quality-improvement collaboratives showed "moderate positive results" across nine controlled studies, two null **[D]**; collective-impact evaluations are mostly cross-sectional process studies **[D]**. Polycentricity buys adaptability at the cost of legibility: Morrison finds "structural density and stability … can coexist with major changes in that regime's context and effectiveness," warning of "the masking effects of increasing complexity" **[D; environmental domain]**. Coordination burden is the operative cost: across Elston et al.'s 13 English inter-municipal cooperations, sustained collaborations were those where "management actions … streamlined the coordination burden imposed by joint working" **[D]**.

### 1.5 The design question

Exclusive attribution is not empirically available: Greenhalgh et al., evaluating a £15m three-service transformation, report that "the link between them and the MI was not causal in a simple or deterministic way" **[D]**. Cross-referral works through sustained relational work, not the referral act: only three of seven practices fully integrated a link-worker programme in two years **[D]**; all 15 social-prescribing evaluations were at high risk of bias, insufficient "to judge either success or value for money" **[D]**. Ending is a recognised practice with its own evidence base, and dissolution is rational when ex-post interdependence exceeds the original trigger **[D]**.

The evidence supports designing for *conditions* — trust, coordination-burden relief, time-frame bridging, protection of intrinsic motivation — and for *contribution-based* accounting. It does not support a claim that any coordination model reliably produces shared outcomes.

## 4. Negative cases and "not collaborating is reasonable"

### 4.1 Documented negative or mixed cases (six)

**N1 — Integrated care does not deliver its intended changes (system level).** Hughes, Shaw & Greenhalgh 2020, 71 sources: "integrated care programs did not necessarily lead to the changes intended in experiences and outcomes" (E4), attributed to misalignment between the aspiration for unity and the multiplicity of practice. *Mixed-to-negative at review level; the authors refuse a simple verdict.*

**N2 — A multi-organisation transformation programme where shared infrastructure failed and attribution dissolved.** Greenhalgh et al. 2009: "no major new IT system was launched successfully during the MI's lifetime" (E7); "not every subproject was an unqualified success" (E6); "the link between them and the MI was not causal in a simple or deterministic way" (E6). **Caution:** the authors "deliberately not passed judgment on the program's overall 'success'" (E9). This is a documented *mixed* case with explicit refusal of a failure verdict — not a documented failure.

**N3 — A widely promoted cross-organisational improvement model with modest effects.** Schouten et al. 2008, 9 controlled studies (2 RCTs): 7 positive on some outcome, 2 null; "may have only modest effects on outcomes at best" (E21).

**N4 — A coordinating model whose evaluations cannot establish effect.** Panjwani et al. 2023: 18 collective-impact studies, mostly cross-sectional process evaluations (E26); and Bickerdike et al. 2017: all 15 social-prescribing evaluations at high risk of bias (E22). *Two distinct literatures arriving at the same structural weakness: the coordination model is evaluated on process, not outcome.*

**N5 — Polycentric plurality masking declining effectiveness.** Morrison 2017: "structural density and stability … can coexist with major changes in that regime's context and effectiveness"; "the masking effects of increasing complexity" (E20). *This is the most important counter-evidence to a "more nodes is better" reading of plurality — the richer the structure, the harder failure is to see.*

**N6 — Collaboration generating the burden that dissolves it.** Elston et al. 2023, 13 English inter-municipal cooperations: collaboration "creates new interdependence" and dissolution follows when "ex-post" exceeds "ex-ante" interdependence (E19). *Directly documents collaboration as a cost-generating act, and bureaucracy-like coordination burden as the proximate cause of endings.*

*Also relevant, contested:* Maier, Meyer & Steinbereithner 2016 map funder-driven professionalisation and its effects (including mission drift) across 599 sources — but the review's own finding is that effects are heterogeneous and the field's concepts overlap, so this is **contested** rather than established.

*Not usable as a negative case:* no retrieved study documents a merger that demonstrably destroyed local specificity, or a new coordinating body shown to have added a net bureaucratic layer, with measured outcomes. See §6.

### 4.2 Documented grounds on which not collaborating, or preserving separateness, is reasonable

**R1 — Avoidance has rational, differentiated grounds, and the collaboration literature systematically under-reports them.** Gazley (Georgia nonprofit executive directors): the literature's "normative and institutionalist tendencies … downplay the rationales for avoiding partnerships"; attitudes "are not monolithic" (E24). *Established as a critique of the evidence base and as a documented set of managerial rationales; not as an outcome effect.*

**R2 — Ending a collaboration can be the rational response to costs the collaboration itself created.** Elston et al. 2023 (E19): dissolution where ex-post interdependence exceeds the original trigger; and sustained cases were those where "management actions … streamlined the coordination burden" — i.e. the difference between survival and dissolution was burden management, not commitment.

**R3 — Formal collaboration is a resource-dependent choice, so informality/small scale is an adaptation, not a deficit.** Guo & Acar 2005: formality rises with age, budget size and board linkages (E15). Foster & Meinhard 2002: size and type predict formal collaboration, mediated by perceived environmental pressure. *[I] Smaller, younger organisations' lower formality is consistent with capacity, not with failure to recognise benefit.*

**R4 — Output-tied requirements can cost the motivation that sustains local initiative.** Cerasoli et al. 2014: "in a 'crowding out' fashion, intrinsic motivation was less important to performance when incentives were directly tied to performance" (E14). *[D] at individual level; [I] that funder-imposed output/attribution regimes act as performance-salient incentives on the people who run local initiatives.*

**R5 — Stopping is an established, evidence-bearing practice, not a failure state.** Niven et al. 2015: 109 studies, 43 terms, 13 frameworks, and "active change interventions were associated with the greatest likelihood of de-adoption" (E25); low-value practice prevalence 16–46%.

**R6 — Diversity of institutions has a functional rationale, not merely a cost.** McGinnis 2011 (working paper, conceptual): "Institutional diversity facilitates collective action by diverse groups within a community, but at the cost of losing any clear sense of the responsibilities and accountability" (E28). *Grey literature; treat as framing, not evidence.*

## 6. What remains not established

**6.1 Directly requested, not established in the sources searched.**

1. **Founder identity as a fragmentation mechanism.** Two canonical sources (Fauchart & Gruber 2011; Powell & Baker 2014) were identified by DOI but their abstracts are publisher-elided and no OA copy was reachable. **[U]** No claim about founder identity should be built on this line without retrieving them. *This is the single largest gap relative to the brief.*
2. **Belonging and recognition** as mechanisms, at any level. Nothing retrieved. **[U]**
3. **Mutual recognition** between autonomous initiatives — no empirical study located. **[U]** The nearest retrieved material is trust as a network-level predictor (George et al. 2024) and "safety" as an antecedent of psychological ownership (Zhang et al. 2020); neither is recognition in the Honneth sense, and neither was tested on inter-initiative recognition.
4. **Whether handoff, cooperation or stopping is rewarded or penalised by funders**, in health promotion specifically. Godenhjelm et al. 2015 and Hubmann 2021 document the *time-frame* problem; none measures a funder's incentive structure against handoff behaviour. **[U]**
5. **Data ownership** as a distinct mechanism. Only indirect evidence: "Information exchange" as a theme (Nooteboom et al. 2021) and the failure to launch a shared IT system (Greenhalgh et al. 2009). No study of data ownership *as a boundary mechanism*. **[U]**
6. **A merger that destroyed local specificity**, documented with outcomes. Nothing retrieved. **[U]**
7. **A new coordinating body shown to add a net bureaucratic layer**, documented with outcomes. Nothing retrieved; Elston et al. 2023 documents coordination burden as a cause of dissolution, which is adjacent but not the same claim. **[U]**
8. **A collaboration documented to have excluded smaller actors**, with measured consequences. Guo & Acar 2005 and Foster & Meinhard 2002 establish resource-dependence gradients in formality; neither documents exclusion. **[U]**
9. **"Islands of coordination"** as a named phenomenon. Term searched; no academic literature located under it in the indexes used. **[U]**
10. **Network effectiveness** at the whole-network level (the Provan & Milward tradition). Not retrieved (§5 A5). **[U]**

**6.2 Levels and transfer cautions that must survive into any synthesis.**

- Cerasoli et al. (individual, work/school/physical) and Bendersky & Hays (small groups) are **not** evidence about organisations or networks. Using them to explain inter-organisational fragmentation is inference, not documentation.
- Zhang et al. 2020 is employee-level psychological ownership. "Territorial behaviours" is an individual-level finding; treating an initiative's resistance to merging as "territoriality" would be an unwarranted level jump. **[I at most]**
- Morrison 2017 is environmental governance. Polycentricity findings from a coral-reef regime are a **domain transfer**.
- Guo & Acar 2005 (one US metro, 95 organisations, cross-sectional), Foster & Meinhard 2002 (Canada, 2002), Gazley 2008 (Georgia) are single-country, dated, and measure dispositions or formality rather than outcomes.

**6.3 What would change the answer.**

- A retrieved empirical test of funder incentive structures against handoff/closure behaviour would convert the largest **[U]** in §1.1 into a **[D]** or refute it.
- A whole-network effectiveness study in a health-promotion setting (the Provan & Milward design applied to community initiatives) would give the parent the missing outcome-level evidence for family 4.
- Any documented case of a coordination layer with measured administrative burden would supply the negative case the brief asks for and the literature did not yield.

**6.4 Adjacent questions noticed, not researched (logged, not pursued).**

- Whether "backbone" organisations in collective impact improve or suppress local autonomy — the CI literature retrieved is evaluative, not comparative on autonomy.
- The volunteer-retention literature (individual-level commitment under organisational change) — relevant to family 3 but not retrieved.
- Mission drift specifically in health-promotion NGOs (as distinct from the general nonprofit professionalisation review).
- Whether polycentric arrangements outperform monocentric ones on health outcomes — no retrieved study tests this head-to-head.
