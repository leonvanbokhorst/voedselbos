# Alternatives to edible planting, and the constraints of edible systems

Working note for issue #22, subquestion 2. Retrieved 2026-11-26. Marks: **[D]** documented in the retrieved source; **[C]** claimed by one actor; **[I]** inference; **[U]** not established in the sources searched. Nothing here concerns Eindhoven.

## 1. Bounded question, and why it matters

A food forest proposed as a cooling intervention must be compared with what a municipality can buy — mature street trees, canopy park, treeless green, built shade, blue infrastructure — on identical indicators: radiant load, time to effect, water, maintenance. The comparison, not the label, decides whether edible planting suits a heat-critical site.

## 2. Comparison by intervention type

**Canopy trees.** From 120,285 Landsat scenes across 293 European cities: **"LSTs observed for urban trees are on average 0-4 K lower in Southern European regions and 8-12 K lower in Central Europe"**, and treeless green is **"approximately 2-4 times"** less effective (Schwaab et al. 2021, *Nature Communications* 12:6763, Abstract; Results; ~10:15 local) [D]. The same paper limits the claim: the metric is LST, not air temperature or PET; **"the cooling provided by urban trees decreases during hot extremes"** in Southern Europe and Turkey while rising in the British Isles and Scandinavia; and irrigation **"could help to maintain the high cooling … but may be limited by future water scarcity"** [D]. The operative contrast is canopy versus no canopy — a shaded park is a canopy intervention and inherits this evidence; treeless park behaves like grass.

**Treeless green and non-edible planting.** In WUR's 2022–2023 field experiments (Wageningen, Zutphen), **"Dry turf grass could be between 5 and 25°C warmer than irrigated grass"** at ~5% soil moisture, while mowing height had no effect and **"irrigation however showed (significant) influence on the surface temperature"** (Deolu-Ajayi, van der Werf & Mossink 2024, WPR-OT 1083, Summary WP2) [D]. Unshaded paving is worse: concrete, tiles and pavers **"can easily be 20-30 degrees warmer than the grass"** in late afternoon, concrete **"remained 20 degrees warmer"** for much of the night [D]. Hence the constraint governing every vegetation option: cooling scales with transpiring leaf area and soil water, so a drought-stressed planting delivers a fraction of its design performance in the week it is needed [I].

**Built shade.** An Australian school study (41 days, 17 Dec 2019–26 Jan 2020, Western Sydney; 20 loggers, >100,000 data points) is the sharpest built-versus-tree comparison retrieved. **"The lowest air and surface temperatures were consistently recorded under tree canopies"**; shading cut surface temperature **"by up to 20°C"** and "feels like" by 10 °C (Pfautsch et al. 2020, *School Microclimates*, Synopsis p.3) [D]. Tree-shaded locations warmed 6.5–7.5 °C between 06:00 and 10:00, unshaded 8.5–9.5 °C, and **"Air temperatures increased the most – by 10.0-11.0°C - where shade was provided by corrugated iron … or dark shade cloth"** [D, §4.2.1 p.27]; all school pergolas use corrugated iron roofing [D, §3.1 p.13]. The fully shaded playground was the warmest location (mean 25.3 °C; 50.5 °C at 13:20 on 4 Jan 2020) because the dark cloth **"caused air to heat up and become trapped under the shade structure"**, over artificial grass, in an enclosed yard that reduced air flow [D, p.29]. Qualification: mean air temperatures spanned only 24.0–25.3 °C and a pergola site (24.3 °C) beat a tree site (24.5 °C) in Table 6 — though the report's own morning-warming grouping puts that pergola location with the tree-shaded sites, so the labels are not clean [D/I]. The established finding is a *failure mode* — dark, low-albedo cloth over hot surfacing in still air — not that structure is intrinsically hotter; but structure alone delivers full shade in year 0 [I].

**Blue infrastructure.** Across 67 studies, **"daytime temperatures … cooled by 2.6 °C on average"**, but **"at night-time in the summer, the thermal effect showed an average urban heating intensity of 0.6 °C"**; method spread was 1.4 °C (field) to 3.4 °C (remote sensing), and cooling rose with water-body size (Fricke, Legg & Kabisch 2024, *UFUG* 101:128528, Abstract) [D]. For night-time relief, water is a partial instrument [I].

**Edible systems.** No retrieved study isolates an edible plot against a matched non-edible control on one site and reports PET, UTCI or MRT [U]. The most-cited "urban heat" source measures no temperature: Rockwell et al. found food-forest canopy **"comparable with neighborhood urban tree cover"** at 10 Miami-Dade (Florida) schools, plus higher species richness and stem density (Rockwell et al. 2022, *Urban Planning* 7(2):139–154, Abstract; subtropical) [D]. A 2025 review of 44 temperate studies still reports that food forests **"can … reduce urban heat"** citing that paper (Knook et al. 2025) [D] — citation creep the main report should not repeat [I]. An edible system cools *as vegetation with canopy and soil water*; whether stratified edible structure beats a same-sized canopy planting on MRT is [U].

## 4. Constraints of edible systems

### 4.1 Establishment time

The literature names **"The establishment phase: year 0–3"**, notes land is **"often marginal and degraded, requiring several years of soil remediation"** and that **"having water rights close to or on the land is crucial for the establishment period"**; managers **"had not anticipated how long it would take for trees to yield crops in large quantities"** [D]. **"Most food forests studied in literature are younger than five years old"** [D] (all Knook et al. 2025). On a heat-critical site the first years are small stems and bare ground — exactly when a structure would be doing the work [I].

### 4.2 Water and drought

UK allotment diaries (163 gardeners, 14,992 visits, calendar year 2018): median **7,595 L/yr** per ~250 m² plot, of which **5,993 L mains** and **486 L water butts**; 80.8% used in summer; **30.4 L/m²/yr** and 16.9 L per kg produce (Dobson, Warren & Edmondson 2021, *Sustainability* 13:2628, §3.4, §3.7) [D]. The decisive point is source: **"winter stocks of rainwater harvested by individual gardeners are insufficient in the majority of cases to meet the water demands of an allotment into the summer months"** [D, Discussion]. 2018 was the UK's joint hottest summer on record and the authors call their figures **"historically unusual"** [D]; a 2018- or 2022-type year is the design case [I]. Trees also use water — one large brush box transpired **156 L/day** for 170 m² of shade; a small banksia's 9 L/day is read as **"low evaporative cooling benefits"** [D]. Water is a vegetation cost generally; edible beds concentrate it into the peak-heat weeks [I].

### 4.3 Soil contamination and food safety

Dutch generic values (RIVM 607711004/2011, Otte et al., Tabel 2.1 p.14; Tabel 6.1 p.45) [D]: lead **interventiewaarde 530 mg/kg**; **"Maximale waarde moestuin" 70 mg/kg**; urgency threshold for a vegetable garden 140 mg/kg; urban background 50 mg/kg. Own-garden vegetables supply 27% of exposure under "wonen met tuin" and 81% under "wonen met moestuin" [D], and **"lood-plantconcentraties het hoogst zijn in bladgroenten (zoals andijvie, sla en kool) en het laagst in aardappel"** [D, Samenvatting p.9]. Official advice on contaminated ground is not to garden in it: **"Voorkom dat u lood binnen krijgt via voedsel uit uw tuin door groenten in bakken met schone teelaarde te kweken"**, washing produce that touched soil; for children's areas, **"Leg gras, grind, halfopen tegels … of een schone laag grond aan"** [D] (RIVM, *Lood in de bodem*, "Tips"). Diffuse contamination is not remediated — **"sanering van zogeheten diffuse verontreinigingen te duur en te omvangrijk"** — so residents get action perspectives instead [D] (RIVM 607050013/2012, Devilee).

Food-law benchmarks, not soil standards: Regulation (EU) 2023/915 Annex §3.1 sets lead at **0.050 mg/kg** for fruiting vegetables (3.1.4.1), **0.10** for root and tuber vegetables (3.1.2.1), **0.30** for leaf vegetables (3.1.6) and **0.10** for most fruit (3.1.1.2) — wet weight after washing [D]. These bind marketed food; for own-grown produce they are a benchmark, not a duty [I].

Countervailing evidence: RIVM revised modelled uptake *downward*. In 13 Heerenveen gardens (soil lead 90–350 mg/kg) the old model flagged unacceptable risk in six; measured crops gave risk indices **"van minder dan 0,1 tot maximaal 0,5"** [D, §5.5.1 p.41–42]. At Den Bommel (soil 354–1,271 mg/kg) lettuce measured 0.16–0.28 mg/kg fresh, potato 0.03–0.07 [D, Tabel 5.6 p.43]. Risk is site-specific and often lower than screening implies — defeating a blanket refusal and equally any claim of universal safety [I].

### 4.4 Maintenance intensity

Median **150 h/yr** and **87 visits** per plot (0.6 h/m²/yr) [D] (Dobson et al. 2021, §3.2). Food-forest-specific burdens: the need for **"food processing or sufficient demand … to avoid unharvested fruit attracting pests or becoming unsightly"**; municipal **"basic knowledge gaps about how to care for urban fruit trees"**; by-laws that **"prohibit the removal of plant material, directly conflicting with the purpose of food forests"**; and success depending on founders with agricultural, entrepreneurial and fundraising skills [D] (Knook et al. 2025, "Challenges"). A competence constraint as much as a labour-hours one.

### 4.5 Ecological trade-offs

In a German field experiment (18 species, 10 plots, Darmstadt) native insects visited wild perennials in **"67% of all visits"** versus **"related ornamental (24%) and unrelated exotic plants (9%)"**; leaf herbivory was 2.3% of leaf area on wild plants against 0.8% and 0.1%; honey bees showed no preference (Lerch, Blüthgen & Mody 2024, *Ecological Solutions and Evidence* 5(3):e12380, Abstract) [D]. Transfer to food forests is inference — the experiment used ornamentals and many food-forest species are exotic cultivars [I] — but "edible" and "biodiverse" are not synonyms. For food forests the ecological case is asserted rather than evidenced: **"few papers provide evidence for the environmental benefits of temperate food forestry"**, and **"no studies specifically focused on biodiversity effects or water quality impacts"** appeared among 44 studies [D] (Knook et al. 2025).

## 5. Counterevidence and null findings

1. **Tree planting is not a guaranteed alternative.** Cohort mortality ranged 0.6–68.5%/yr, median 4.4–6.5%, and **"annual mortality tended to be highest during the first five years after planting"**; establishment median 6.6–7%/yr (Hilbert et al. 2019, *AUF* 45(5):167–200, Abstract; Table 2 p.173) [D] — about 30% of a cohort gone in five years [I]. Canopy cover in major US cities has nonetheless been declining despite million-tree campaigns [D] (Roman 2014, *Scenario Journal* 04, Abstract).
2. **Built shade is not uniformly worse:** a pergola site (24.3 °C) beat a tree site (24.5 °C) [D, Table 6 p.29].
3. **Lead alarm can be overdone:** measured risk indices ≤0.5 in 13 contaminated gardens [D]. **Irrigated grass cools well**, 5–15 °C below air temperature at night — the failure is water, not plant type [D]. **Blue space heats at night** (+0.6 °C) [D].
4. **The food-forest "urban heat" citation measures no heat** [D]; a temperature result elsewhere in that paper cannot be excluded — the PDF was not retrieved [U].

## 6. Where edible planting appears to be the wrong intervention

1. **Where shade is needed now.** A heat-critical route, schoolyard or care garden needs full shade in year 0 — only a structure or existing mature tree gives that [I]; a food forest gives ground cover and small stems first [D, Knook].
2. **Above the garden lead standard.** Over 70 mg/kg the advice is raised beds with clean soil [D, RIVM], turning the project into container horticulture with different costs [I].
3. **Where the water source is potable mains.** Butts were insufficient and mains replaced them [D, Dobson]; the system loses cooling in the critical week *and* adds municipal demand [I].
4. **Where nobody is accountable for harvest and care.** Unharvested fruit, pests, unsightliness and by-laws against removing plant material are documented failure modes [D, Knook]; neglect is worse for amenity than paving-and-shade or native planting [I].
5. **Where habitat is the primary objective** — native planting has measured insect value edible planting cannot be assumed to match [D, Lerch; I].
6. **Where the site cannot carry stratified woody planting** (narrow canyon, shallow rooting volume over infrastructure): insufficient soil volume limits root growth and cooling [D, Schwaab]; built shade plus adjacent canopy allocates the same money better [I].

The evidence does **not** show edible planting is worse per unit leaf area on cooling [U]; it shows its cooling is unmeasured, its non-thermal constraints numerous, and its establishment delay real.

## 7. Not established in the sources searched

- Any measured PET/UTCI/MRT comparison of an edible plot against a matched non-edible control on one site [U].
- Any Dutch measured thermal series for a food forest; Dutch material surfaced was popular reporting or a carbon-stock study [U].
- Years from planting to functional street-tree shade in NW Europe; only size-dependence (20 m² vs 170 m² canopy) and the first-five-years mortality phase are established [D].
- Irrigation demand of a *food forest* as against annual beds — 30.4 L/m²/yr is allotment vegetables in a drought year [D]; no perennial equivalent, no comparative maintenance hours, and no assessment of whether neighbourhood-scale rainwater/greywater could meet edible demand in NL [U].

## 10. What would change this answer

A measured NW-European campaign comparing MRT/PET under matched food forest, canopy street, shade sail and lawn would move §2 from [U] to [D] and could narrow the gap. Evidence of functional shade within ≤5 years from fastigiate or high-density edible stock would soften §6.1. Evidence that Dutch edible systems are reliably irrigated from non-potable storage through a 2018/2022-type summer would remove §4.2, the sharpest objection. A Dutch survey showing urban soil lead typically below 70 mg/kg would make §4.3 a site check. A study isolating night-time behaviour of stratified edible canopies would test the one mechanism where a food forest might beat a mown park.
