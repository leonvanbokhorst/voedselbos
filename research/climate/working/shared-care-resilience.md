# Shared care as social resilience under heat and climate stress

Working note for issue #22, subquestion 4. Retrieval date 2026-11-26. Marks: **[D]** documented (independent observation or peer-reviewed finding), **[C]** claimed by one actor (self-report, producer report), **[I]** inference, **[U]** not established in the sources searched.

## 1. Bounded question and why it matters

Does recurring shared care in urban green spaces (community gardens, allotments, food forests, neighbourhood gardening) produce social resilience under heat and climate stress — at what cost, with what limits, and where does it stop substituting for statutory responsibility? It matters because the parent question treats shared care as the mechanism that makes a distributed edible-green network work. If care is real but fragile, concentrated in one person and evaporated by drought, the network is a social asset with a maintenance liability, not a heat intervention.

## 2. Social connection: what is documented

**The claim is well rehearsed and weakly designed.** The most-cited recent synthesis, D'Amore et al. (2026, *Current Opinion in Psychology*), reviews 50 studies and asserts that gardens build bonding, bridging and linking social capital — "psychological resilience, social connectedness, and civic engagement" — without reporting a single effect size [ABS; C/I — narrative review, no systematic method, practitioner-institute authors]. That is the shape of the literature: strong direction, thin measurement.

**Independent observation is small-n but consistent.** Young & Bauer (2022) studied a Zurich allotment site cleared for construction — field conversations plus a survey of the same gardeners before (2018) and after (2019) clearance, analysed with ANCOVA as a natural experiment [ABS]. They report a negative effect of loss on the **number of social contacts and on emotional well-being**, but not on other factors [D, ABS — numbers provisional, n not in abstract]. The tie is real enough that removing the place measurably reduces it.

**Dutch evidence has the most volume and the least independence.** The Verwey-Jonker evaluation of the Oranje Fonds *Groen Verbindt* programme (van der Klein, Wilderink & Reches 2019) followed 27→23 care-and-neighbourhood gardens in 2015–2018 with annual coordinator questionnaires (n=27/25/24/23), volunteer surveys (~270 invited, ~60% response; N=166 in reported tables) and street surveys around 14 gardens (N=576 in 2017, N=651 in 2018; 466 never-visitors as comparison) [FT, §1.3 pp. 10–11]. Year on year, 9 in 10 active participants agreed they had met new people in the neighbourhood; 5 in 10 that they now "stand ready for others" more; only 4 in 10 that they dare ask others for help; ~80% attributed progress in social contacts, happiness and health to garden participation [C — self-report, no control; §Samenvatting pp. 4–6]. The asymmetry matters: participants report **giving** more readily than **asking**. That is a weak-tie and generalised-reciprocity finding, not evidence of a mutual-aid safety net.

**Qualitative work supplies mechanism, not magnitude.** In Aberdeen, Wardle et al. (2024) interviewed 21 growers and coordinators (23 participants) [FT]; one described the garden as what stopped a panic attack — "the only thing I could do is actually go to the garden and just take care of the plants" (P19). A Canadian campus-garden study (D'Andrea Brooks et al. 2025, 14 interviews) frames the garden as a "third place" [ABS; small n]; Cumbers et al. (2018, Glasgow) find gardening work builds collective capacity and local knowledge while remaining embedded in austerity [ABS].

**Net:** routine presence, weak ties and local knowledge are [D] as reported experience and, once, as contact counts. Translation into a durable obligation to check on a neighbour is [U].

## 3. From connection to resilience: what is demonstrated, and the gap

The chain has three links. Garden → social tie is documented, modestly. Social isolation → heat death is documented, from other literatures. Garden tie → protective action during a heat event is **not documented in any source I found**.

For the middle link, Herrmann et al. (2026, *Environmental Health and Preventive Medicine*) summarise a Japan–Germany workshop and cite a South Korean time-series in which a one-percentile reduction in social gathering among older men in dense areas was associated with a **2% higher heatwave mortality risk** (95% CI 0.5–3.4) [FT §3; secondary citation, not independently verified]. The only social-connection heat interventions they could find are two studies of a Rome programme monitoring isolated over-80s, with an excess-death incidence rate ratio of **0.44 (0.32–0.60)** for the intervention group in a retrospective ecological design [FT §4; secondary, one research team]. Their conclusion: such interventions "do not intend to alleviate both, social isolation and heat health risks at a broader scale", and no study assesses single social-isolation measures inside heat-action plans [D for the review's reading; effect sizes second-hand].

Crucially, the interventions that show anything are **professional or trained-volunteer programmes** — Philadelphia's public-health-run heat "buddy" system, the French municipal heatwave registry with proactive calls, Cologne's heat hotline, GP monitoring in Lazio [D via Herrmann et al. §4]. Their active ingredient is a maintained list plus an assigned caller, not a shared garden. **[I]** A garden's contribution would be the pre-existing relationship such a list cannot manufacture; nothing in these sources tests that.

The Dutch heat survey (van de Kamp et al. 2026, n=514, aged 50+, Limburg, fielded 12–19 July 2023 directly after a 7–11 July heatwave) shows heat managed almost entirely at household level: staying indoors, wet towels, fans, misting, seeking shade; those with lower health status did **not** increase protective behaviour, and only about a third of the most multiply-vulnerable saw themselves at elevated risk [FT §3.2–3.4, §4.1]. The instrument was built from Dutch National Heatwave Plan, Red Cross, GGD and RIVM recommendations and contains no neighbour, community or garden channel [I, from the reported measures]. The state's heat-response frame runs through individual behaviour, professional outreach and municipal plans; garden care does not appear in it.

## 4. Care burden under heat and drought

**Water is the binding constraint, and it is set by statute, not by the garden.** Sustain/Capital Growth documented the August 2022 Thames Water hosepipe ban in London: community gardens fell outside the legal categories "domestic", "commercial" or "allotment", and only after lobbying were allotment holders permitted to use a hose "to water their crops (but not other plants and flowers)… where no other means of watering is reasonably possible" [FT, Sustain 2022]. Thames Water's stated reason included "the logistical challenges of navigating large allotment sites with watering cans" [D, quoted]. The network's coordinator described "the devastating effects of this summer's drought on the food our network is growing, our soil health and the biodiversity of these essential urban growing spaces" [C — advocate's claim, unmeasured]. Every adaptation listed is labour: rainwater butts, grey water, mulching, morning/evening watering, olla pots [D as practitioner guidance]. In drought the volunteer's job shifts from cultivation to hauling. Quantified plant loss, water volumes or rota hours are **[U]**: no study I found measures irrigation labour or drought mortality in community gardens or food forests.

**Absence and abandonment.** Wardle et al. is explicit that burden lands on coordinators, whose "demanding multi-functional roles" produced "fatigue and feelings of over-commitment", and that "no targeted research on burnout in community garden volunteers" existed at the time (P57) [FT]. Their resilience finding is the most transferable: volunteer-led groups "were in exactly that position of having one or two dedicated leaders"; a residents' group observed "you've not got long-term resilience if somebody moves away"; and "at the time of writing one group felt in a precarious situation following the resignation of the founder" (P50) [D]. One coordinator's ambivalence is explicit: "Sometimes it's overwhelming and I just get fed up with the whole thing, and then other times I get really enthused" (P52); the work is largely administrative — funding, promotion, gatekeeping — "an extension to working life rather than an antidote to it" (P9).

**After closure.** A Bucharest study of three gardens with social-inclusion goals that closed 2019–2021 found that 2–3 years later some commoning capacities "can endure and even outgrow terminated spaces" while others fade, tied to structural context [ABS, 2025, *Environmental Sociology*]. More honest than either "destroyed" or "persists": **some** capacity survives the place.

## 5. Limits: exhaustion, exclusion, conflict, statutory boundary

- **Who is not in the garden** [D]: Sheffield reporting Dobson et al. (*Landscape and Urban Planning*, 2020) found allotment land in ten English cities down 65% (1960–2016), with eight times more closures in the most deprived areas [university news page, secondary].
- **Exclusion** [D]: Otoadese et al. (2025) surveyed 119 Black African immigrants in Alberta plus 10 interviews and 2 sharing circles, documenting multi-level barriers to collective gardening despite reported benefits [ABS]. In the Dutch programme, volunteers were "redelijk hoogopgeleid" — about half with university or HBO education, against just over a third of surrounding residents [FT §1.3 p. 11; D, self-reported sample]. Wardle et al. summarise the wider literature as "predominately white and middle-class", or multi-ethnic under white middle-class leadership, "which can exacerbate cultural exclusion" (P3) [secondary].
- **Conflict is normal** [D]: every Dutch project passed through "storming" — conflicts over approach and leadership — before performing, with coordinators' leadership decisive [FT §1.2, §8.5].
- **Ambition vs reality** [D]: the funder expected 25 neighbours and 25 care clients simultaneously each week; "in bijna geen enkel project gelukt", monthly contact being the realistic maximum, with cold excluding older clients three-quarters of the year [FT §Samenvatting p. 6].
- **The statutory boundary.** In these sources, heat protection sits with national plans, municipalities, GGD/RIVM advice, GPs and trained volunteers; green maintenance is a municipal service. Where volunteers moved into municipal green maintenance the result is documented as a loss: Smith, Whitten & Ernwein (2023, *The Geographical Journal*) find English austerity 2010–2019 produced "service reductions, increased reliance on volunteer labour", delegation "to community groups and volunteers", and ultimately "reducing accountability and accessibility, and exacerbating inequities in parks provision" [ABS]. Cumbers et al. read it from the garden side: gardens answer state withdrawal but cannot be divorced from "regressive underlying economic and social processes" [ABS].

**[I]** Volunteer care can maintain relationships and a place, and can supply named, known neighbours to a professional heat-response list; it cannot supply coverage, liability, continuity, water rights or 24/7 response. Where it has replaced municipal maintenance, the evidence points to degraded and more unequal provision.

## 6. Counterevidence and null findings

1. **No outcome measurement.** Nothing I read measures a garden-attributable change in heat outcomes: D'Amore et al. claim resilience from a narrative review, the Dutch data are uncontrolled self-reports, Aberdeen and Canada are interview-only.
2. **The Dutch self-report is internally modest.** Only 4 in 10 volunteers dare ask others for help — the reciprocity mutual aid requires is the weakest item [D]. Demand is also not participation: waiting lists lengthen while provision shrinks (Sheffield), and programmes miss routine-contact targets, so "social infrastructure" is often an aspiration with a queue outside it.
3. **"Social resilience" is a contested measurement object.** Copeland et al. (2020, *IJDRR*) show an inherent tension between persistence and transformation, indicators that embed normative choices, and the need for transparency "toward whose resilience we hope to measure" [ABS].
4. **Substitution has documented costs, not benefits** (Smith et al. 2023); no source shows volunteer green care delivering statutory heat functions.
5. **Gentrification-by-garden** is a standard critique but I retrieved no study substantiating it — **[U]** in the sources searched.

## 7. What is not established in the sources searched

- Any documented instance of garden participants checking on, watering for, or sheltering neighbours **during a heat event**, or of a garden used as a refuge by non-gardeners.
- Quantified irrigation labour (hours, volumes, rota compliance) or plant-loss rates under drought/heat.
- A municipality whose heat plan assigns tasks to garden volunteers, or evidence such volunteers are trained, insured or counted.
- Longitudinal evidence that garden ties persist for years and convert into crisis help; the Zurich design covers one clearance, not a heat event.
- Dutch food-forest social outcome data beyond self-report: Rebisz (2021), n=48 across 24 food forests [C]; a 2026 survey of 102 Drentse Voedselbossen members claiming connection while noting "verdere professionalisering" is needed [C].
- Whether care substitutes for professional management *in a garden* with measured outcomes; the parks evidence is city-service scale.

## 10. What would change this answer

A study following gardeners and matched non-gardeners through an actual heat event — check-ins, water-sharing, shelter-seeking, health outcomes — would move section 3 from inference to finding. Documented irrigation labour and plant loss from Dutch buurttuinen or voedselbossen in the 2018/2022 droughts would harden section 4. A municipal plan naming community gardens as delivery partners, with training, insurance and reporting, would relocate the statutory boundary. Evidence that garden ties survive leadership turnover over 5+ years would soften the fragility finding; one well-designed study showing no difference in neighbourly help would collapse the premise.
