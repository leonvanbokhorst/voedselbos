# Cooling effect and limits of multilayered edible vegetation — what is actually documented

Sub-question of the parent question on community-driven urban food forests, climate adaptation and thermal comfort in a densifying Eindhoven. Retrieval date for every source below: **2026-11-26**.

## 1. Bounded question and why it matters

Does the multilayered edible vegetation of a food forest / urban agroforestry actually cool people outdoors, by how much, through which mechanism, and how does it compare with ordinary urban green (parks, street trees, lawns, paving)? This decides whether "food forest" can be claimed as a heat-adaptation measure in Eindhoven's densification debate, or only as an *inference* from generic vegetation evidence — a distinction the parent question cannot fudge.

## 2. Findings by mechanism

### 2.1 Canopy shade and radiant load (the largest, best-evidenced pathway)

The radiative pathway is where the robust magnitudes are, and it is *not* an air-temperature pathway. In a modelling study of three European cities, radiant heating "constitutes the largest part of the heat load (heat stress) on humans" during heat events and explains "89% of the variance in thermal perception (Lee et al. 2013)" [D] (Thorsson et al. 2017, Introduction). The same paper anchors the indicator: "T_mrt ≥60 °C is used to represent severe heat events. The level corresponds to a physiological equivalent temperature, PET >40 °C" [D] (Thorsson et al. 2017, "Classification of severe heat events"). Crucially, "Compared to T_a, which is characterized by rather small daytime spatial variations, T_mrt shows large spatial variations during the day … primarily determined by the shadow pattern generated by buildings and vegetation" [D] (ibid., Introduction).

Shade magnitude depends on canopy density, not on edible-ness. A Nature Communications comment synthesising the evidence states that a German study "found that dense canopy in urban environments can reduce temperatures experienced by humans by 11 °C, compared to only 4 °C with sparse canopy coverage" [D] (Croeser, Rahman & Ghosh 2026, "Effective cooling needs dense canopy"). A measured study of street-market shade in a tropical savanna climate found that "A cluster of trees with a dense canopy provided more effective cooling (with a satisfied thermal condition of 9 h) than artificial shade structures", while "the space beneath the sparse tree canopy had the warmest condition" [D] (Jareemit & Srivanit 2022, abstract). Both point the same way: shade is a radiant/surface mechanism scaling with leaf-area density and sky-view obstruction. Canopy thresholds recur — "canopy cover exceeding 40%, within 60 m of housing, was needed to provide significant cooling effects"; "When canopy exceeds 30–40%, shading and evapotranspiration effects begin to significantly lower both surface and air temperature" [D] (Croeser et al. 2026, citing Ziter et al. 2019 and Rahman et al. 2022).

### 2.2 Evapotranspiration (real, but water-limited and species-limited)

Evapotranspiration is documented as a genuine cooling flux, mostly for air temperature and only at very local scale. A systematic review of 51 green-blue-grey infrastructure types reports "The most efficient air cooling was observed in botanical gardens (5.0 ± 3.5 °C), wetlands (4.9 ± 3.2 °C), green walls (4.1 ± 4.2 °C), street trees (3.8 ± 3.1 °C), and vegetated balconies (3.8 ± 2.7 °C)" [D] (Kumar et al. 2024, abstract) — note that this "air cooling" set mixes indicators and scales and should be read as an upper envelope, not a design value.

The limiting factor is water. The same synthesis reports species-specific collapse of the cooling flux under drought: "Platanus x hispanica showed a 61% reduction in growth during soil drought, while Robinia pseudoacacia exhibited a 58% decline in transpiration under extreme drought stress"; trees in paved or compacted sites "experienced up to 60% reductions in both structural growth and cooling performance" [D] (Croeser et al. 2026, "Tree health is vital to effective cooling"). Water scarcity is also flagged as a constraint on green infrastructure cooling in dry climates [D] (Kumar et al. 2024, "Altitude"/"Temporal scale" discussion).

### 2.3 Soil moisture and water retention (site-hydrology evidence, no thermal endpoint)

The one Dutch food-forest measurement I could retrieve is a water balance, not a thermal study: a Van Hall Larenstein student report comparing food forest Ketelbroek (Groesbeek) with the adjacent arable field found that although the arable field had the highest soil organic-matter content, it had the lowest maximum storage because of shallower rooting, and that "De vele vegetatielagen zorgen namelijk voor een grotere verdamping en een grotere waterberging" [D] (Siepel et al. 2018, Samenvatting). Read strictly: multilayer structure is associated with higher evaporation *and* larger storage in this one site — the two claims pull in opposite directions for cooling, and neither is a temperature measurement. This is [D] for hydrology, [U] for any thermal consequence.

### 2.4 Wind and ventilation (mechanism asserted, penalty unquantified)

Reviews list airflow modification among vegetation's heat mechanisms — vegetation "contribut[es] to the dissipation of heat by acting as windbreaks, modifying airflow patterns, and facilitating natural ventilation" [D] (Kumar et al. 2024, "Mechanisms of temperature and heat stress regulation by GI") — i.e. the same property can reduce ventilation. The explicit night-time penalty documented in my sources is attributed to urban *form*, not to vegetation: "rollout of canopy must occur thoughtfully, avoiding potential pitfalls such as night-time 'heat trapping' in narrow, poorly ventilated street canyons" [D] (Croeser et al. 2026, "Effective cooling needs dense canopy"). Thorsson et al. generalise the trade-off — "most of the strategies to reduce radiant heating during the day have an adverse effect on the radiant component during the night and vice versa" — but single out vegetation as "One of the few solutions that mitigate both daytime and nighttime outdoor heat stress" [D] (Thorsson et al. 2017, Introduction). No retrieved study quantifies a humidity- or wind-driven comfort penalty for edible multilayered vegetation: **[U]**.

### 2.5 Surface temperature and material effects (where food-forest-adjacent evidence exists)

Surface temperature (LST) responses are much larger and better documented for edible land than air-temperature responses. A Europe-wide spatial assessment of allotments using "land surface temperature Landsat 8/9" reports cooling services reaching 4.1 million people within European functional urban areas (1.7 million of them in Germany), an average of 8,221 people cooled per square kilometre of allotment, and a cooled area about 2.8 times the allotment's own area [D, ABS] (Budzik, Sylla & Schulp 2026, abstract — numbers reconstructed from the deposited abstract index; treat as provisional). Because the indicator is LST, this cannot be converted into human comfort.

The nearest thing to a food-forest thermal study is a municipal programme evaluation in Taichung (Taiwan): field measurements plus questionnaires found the Urban Food Forest Program "significantly reduced the surrounding environmental temperature, particularly in soil areas, with an average cooling effect of 2.4 °C" and "reducing [heat island intensity] by 15%" [D, ABS] (Sung et al. 2025, abstract). The authors themselves list "relatively small sample size and limited observation period" as limitations, and the indicator behind "2.4 °C" is not identifiable from the abstract.

By contrast, the effect of peri-urban agriculture on the *surface* heat island at city scale is small: a coupled land-atmosphere simulation of Changchun found an intensification scenario produced "an average effect of 0.07–0.16 °C" on SUHI cores [D, ABS] (npj Urban Sustainability 2026, abstract). Scale and indicator decide whether a number looks impressive or negligible.

## 4. Counterevidence and null findings

1. **No air-temperature signal from an urban farm.** Six years of hourly measurements at a St Paul farm "characterized by high proportion of pervious soils and actively transpiring crops" showed mean monthly temperatures not significantly different from nearby tree-covered locations; only July–August minima were ~1 °C cooler than the broader urban area, with no difference in maxima [D, ABS] (Klink 2025). This is the single most directly relevant measured null for edible production systems.
2. **Street trees stop cooling air in extreme heat.** "Coutts et al. highlighted that street trees can cool summer daytime air temperatures by up to 1.5 °C, yet reported that the cooling effect of trees during extreme heat events was not significant between streets with and without trees" [D, quoted in Kumar et al. 2024, Introduction]. The air-temperature signal is largest in ordinary summer weather and not detectable in the conditions when it matters most.
3. **City-scale effect of urban agriculture is small.** 0.07–0.16 °C on SUHI cores in a coupled model [D, ABS] (npj Urban Sustainability 2026).
4. **The evidence base for edible green is thin, and the headline numbers are inflated by indicator mixing.** Allotments and city farms are among "the least studied categories"; allotments, private gardens and city farms are listed as having received "minimal" attention; and the review's own "most efficient air cooling" figures span 3.8–5.0 °C across structurally incomparable systems [D] (Kumar et al. 2024). Cooling efficiency also "seemed to decrease when studied for more than 400 days" [D] (ibid., "Temporal scale").
5. **Allotment cooling is framed as comparable, not superior, to other green infrastructure** in the European mapping — allotments are reported as showing effectiveness comparable to other blue-green infrastructure objects [D, ABS, provisional — reconstructed from the abstract index; the 23% "more efficient" figure in the same abstract has an ambiguous referent and is not used here] (Budzik et al. 2026).
6. **Prerequisites can erase the benefit.** Dense canopy close to people, adequate soil water, and decades of growth are all required; planting numbers alone "is not sufficient to reliably deliver urban cooling" [D] (Croeser et al. 2026, abstract & "Trees take decades").
7. **Shade alone is not sufficient at midday** in hot conditions: "tree canopies and artificial shade structures had limited application for providing comfortable conditions throughout midday" [D, ABS] (Jareemit & Srivanit 2022).
8. **The Dutch "food forest cools" claim is currently assertion, not measurement.** A KennisWest catalogue record summarising a Platform31 article states that the Ketelbroek food forest needed no irrigation in the hot dry summer of 2026 and that Van Eck "wijst op soortenrijkdom, droogtebestendigheid en verkoeling" — no data, no indicator, no site measurement [C] (KennisWest 2026, article abstract; page notes the text was produced "in interactie met AI").

## 5. What is not established in the sources searched

- **No measured T_a, T_mrt, PET or UTCI study of a temperate or NW-European food forest.** The only food-forest temperature study found is subtropical, municipal and abstract-only (Sung et al. 2025).
- **No study separates the edible component from the vegetation structure.** Nothing retrieved shows that fruiting/nut-bearing species cool differently from ornamental trees of similar leaf area and canopy density.
- **No night-time multilayer-canopy measurement.** Whether a dense food-forest canopy traps longwave radiation and warms nights (the mechanism flagged for street canyons) is untested in the retrieved literature [U].
- **No humidity/comfort penalty quantified for edible vegetation**, although humidity is repeatedly named as a moderator of green-infrastructure performance [D] (Kumar et al. 2024) and is [U] for food forests specifically.
- **No evidence on juvenile food forests.** Food forests in NW Europe are mostly young; the maturity prerequisite documented for urban trees [D] (Croeser et al. 2026) has no food-forest equivalent in my sources.
- **Coverage limits:** ScienceDirect (where most food-forest-adjacent primary studies live) and MDPI were access-blocked, so several key primary papers are ABS-level only. The food-forest review literature is thin at source: Ferrini et al. 2026 report "inconsistent definitions, limited design guidelines, and the scarcity of applied research" [D, ABS], and the 2025 systematic review they cite (Thwaites, *J. Cleaner Production* 501) was **not retrieved** — it is recorded here only as a citation inside their reference list, not as evidence.

## 6. Is there any study isolating edible/agroforestry vegetation against a matched non-edible control at the same site?

**None found.** The three closest designs are all weaker than a matched control:

- Klink 2025 compares a farm against *nearby* tree-covered locations and the wider urban area — different parcels, no matched pairing, and no edible/non-edible treatment contrast on one site [D, ABS].
- Budzik et al. 2026 compare allotments against other blue-green infrastructure types across Europe using LST — a cross-site, cross-city comparison, not a controlled contrast [D, ABS].
- Sung et al. 2025 compare programme food-forest sites against heat-island hotspots, with no non-edible green comparator reported in the abstract [D, ABS].

Consequently, any Eindhoven claim of the form "a food forest cools X °C more than a park/lawn" is currently **[I] inference** from generic vegetation studies, not a documented edible-versus-non-edible finding.

## 9. What would change this answer

- A measured summer campaign in one NW-European food forest reporting T_a, T_mrt, PET/UTCI at 1.5 m, paired with an adjacent park, lawn and paved square on the same days — this single study would convert most of the inference in §6 into documentation.
- Paired measurements under edible vs ornamental canopies of matched leaf-area index and sky-view factor, which would test whether "edible" matters at all, or only structure does.
- Nocturnal measurements inside a dense multilayer canopy showing whether longwave trapping offsets daytime shade gains.
- A drought-year study showing whether food-forest water retention (S10) sustains transpiration when lawns and street trees shut down — the most plausible food-forest-specific advantage, currently untested.
- Longitudinal data past canopy maturity (10–25 years), given that the maturity prerequisite is documented for urban trees but not for food forests.
