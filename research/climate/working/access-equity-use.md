# Access, use and the distributed-network hypothesis

**Bounded subquestion (parent issue #22, line 3).** (a) Who is exposed to urban heat, and who actually reaches and uses cool outdoor places — map proximity versus use; (b) is there evidence for many small cool places near homes rather than one large destination, including catchment, size, clustering and green-gentrification consequences; plus verification of the Dutch "≈300 m / ≈200 m²" cool-place claim and Eindhoven's adoption of it.

**Why it matters.** The parent question treats a distributed network of edible green spaces as a climate intervention. Whether that is a *cooling* claim or an *access* claim turns on evidence this note assembles: proximity is measurable and cheap, use is not, and the people most exposed are often least able to make the trip.

Marks: **[D]** documented in a retrieved source; **[C]** claimed by one actor; **[I]** inference; **[U]** not established in the sources searched. Retrieval date for all sources: 2026-11-26.

## 1. Exposure and vulnerability: who, on what evidence

Dutch national health assessment: heat exposure is universal but the *sensitivity* and *adaptive capacity* are not. RIVM states that "Mensen in een financieel kwetsbare positie hebben vaak niet de middelen om verkoelende maatregelen te nemen. Bewoners van oude, slecht geïsoleerde woningen worden meer blootgesteld aan hitte" and that isolated people, "met name ouderen", often cannot take measures such as seeking cooling in a park **[D]** (RIVM 2023-0324, §3 Factsheet Hitte, "Blootstelling"). The same report gives the number that bounds any outdoor intervention: "Mensen brengen ongeveer 80-90% van hun tijd binnenshuis door", and nursing-home residents "bijna 100% van de tijd binnen" on hot days **[D]** (ibid., "Binnenshuis versus Buitenshuis").

The 2026 national heat-plan guidance (VWS/RIVM, written by the national GGD working group with TAUW) locates the housing gradient geographically: people in overheated homes or without the money to adapt "wordt extra blootgesteld. Dit komt vaak voor in wijken met een hoog percentage inwoners met een lage Sociaal Economische Positie (SEP)" **[D]** (Handreiking Lokaal Hitteplan v2.0, Jan 2026, p. 4). It also names the groups: 75+, young children, chronically ill, homeless people, people with mental-health or intellectual disabilities, outdoor workers, refugees, pregnant women (§5, pp. 22–30).

So the exposed population is documented at neighbourhood and housing-stock level, and in the Netherlands that is the resolution at which it is known: low-SEP urban neighbourhoods, old/poorly insulated dwellings, top-floor and unshaded flats **[D for the gradient; I for "top floor", which I did not find stated as such in the retrieved Dutch sources]**. What is *not* documented in the sources retrieved: renters as a distinct category (the mechanism — split incentives, no permission to shade or insulate — is plausible but unestablished here **[U]**), and any Eindhoven-specific exposure gradient (out of scope; the Lead's line).

## 2. Access versus use: what the evidence shows

**Proximity is a weak predictor where it has been tested.** In a Brisbane survey (n = 1,479), park area within 250 m differed significantly between users and non-users, but in the general linear model nature orientation carried the largest coefficient (β = 0.570) and "park availability had the lowest (β = 0.133 and 0.182, respectively)"; at 1 km park cover "was not significant" **[D]** (Lin et al. 2014, Results, Table 1). The authors conclude "a significant group of people might not use local green spaces even if such spaces are available close to their homes"; "non-park users comprised nearly 40% of our survey population" **[D]** (Discussion). Self-reported visits, one subtropical Australian city, 2012.

**Observed mobility shows most visits are local but the catchment is far larger than 300 m.** An analysis of ~60 million anonymised smartphone park visits across 20 US metros found exponential distance decay with a metropolitan "half-distance": about 2.3 miles in New York, about 2 miles in Detroit, nearly 5 in Minneapolis **[D — reported by the researchers' university press office, not read in the peer-reviewed text]** (NYU Tandon, 19 Nov 2026, describing Zhou, Wei, Huang et al., *Landscape and Urban Planning*). Two findings matter here: large parks draw regional populations, and distance decay is *steeper* "in neighborhoods with larger shares of households without a vehicle, and in those with larger shares of residents from racial and ethnic minority groups" **[D, press-release level]**. The same source concedes phone data "may underrepresent older and lower-income residents" — precisely the group of interest **[D]**.

**Who actually uses a cool place, when one exists.** The strongest use-side dataset retrieved is the Maricopa County (Arizona) cooling-centre survey: 944 visitors at 15 centres and 1,260 public respondents, August–September 2023, average daily high 42.2 °C **[D]** (Gettel et al. 2025, MMWR 74(14)). Visitors were 65% homeless versus 12% of the public, while "a higher percentage of the public than cooling center visitors were aged ≥65 years (45% versus 16%)" **[D]** (ibid., Investigation and Outcomes). The group with the highest heat risk was the group least present in the cool place. Barriers: "No transportation" 31% of visitors; "Wasn't aware cooling centers exist" 36% of visitors and 49% of the public; hours unusable 14%; "Concerns about safety" 11%; pets not allowed 7% (33% of the public would bring one) **[D]** (Table). Median desired closing time 7 p.m. against 9–5 weekday hours **[D]**.

**A systematic review finds the same barriers and almost no outcome evidence.** Six studies; "No studies conclusively measured health and wellbeing outcomes directly after cooling centre use"; barriers were "accessibility, operational hours, and public perceptions, which often misalign with user needs"; centres inside libraries and community centres worked better; one model gave a number needed to treat of 1 million to avoid one heat death **[D]** (Dearman et al. 2024, ABS — figures provisional).

**Route shade changes behaviour, but less than designers may assume.** A controlled forced-choice experiment in Singapore (74 participants, 46 analysed, 408 decisions) found "the distance walked in the shade is discounted by a factor of 0.86 compared to the distance walked in the sun" — sun walking is perceived as ~16% longer — and "tree shade is perceived as 50% as intense as … building shade" (E[ρ] = 0.50) **[D]** (Melnikov et al. 2022, Sci Rep 12:2441). Selection: campus population, tropical city, short exposures; not older or frail walkers.

**The Dutch national proximity map is explicitly not a usability map.** The Kennisportaal Klimaatadaptatie page for the "Afstand tot koelte" map states that the national cool-place definition can be extended "met aanvullende criteria, zoals toegankelijkheid, veiligheid, sanitaire voorzieningen etc. Dit is niet in de standaard landelijke kaart opgenomen" **[D]**. That is a direct, official admission that a 300 m "pass" measures distance, not a place someone would sit.

**Drinking water, toilets, seating.** Retrieved evidence is thin and indirect: the Maricopa table records belongings storage (10%) and pet rules as barriers; the national Dutch map does not check sanitation **[D/U]**. I found no retrieved study quantifying benches, toilets or fountains as determinants of cool-place use in the Netherlands; for the claim that toilet availability keeps older people at home I found only press coverage of a local survey — **[U]** here.

## 3. The distributed-network hypothesis

**What the evidence supports.** Park trips decay over kilometres, and more steeply for people with fewer mobility options **[D, press-release level]**; a single large destination is therefore a regional facility and a poor instrument for reaching the carless and the housebound. Small, frequent, near-home places are the geometry that matches a population whose median trip is short. The Dutch guidance is itself built at that scale — a 200–400 m² cool place within 300 m of every dwelling **[D]** (below).

**What the evidence does not support.**
- *Catchment of a 200–400 m² space*: no retrieved study quantifies it. **[U]**
- *Clusters*: the one directly relevant study located — "How do mini-parks serve in groups? A visit analysis of mini-park groups in the neighbourhoods of Nanjing" (Zhou et al., *Cities* 129:103804, 2022) — is paywalled and its abstract was unavailable through the open indexes I could reach; I can confirm the paper exists and its title, not its findings **[U]**. Search-snippet claims about it are not evidence.
- *Substitution*: the 3-30-300 components are close to statistically independent. Across 2.5 million buildings in eight cities, "we find very weak relationships between results for each of the three tests", the correlation between tree views and distance to parks being "close to zero" **[D]** (Croeser et al. 2024, Nat Commun 15:9333, Results). Adding many small spaces therefore does not deliver canopy, and vice versa; the three standards are three different policies.
- *Threshold validity*: the same study calls 3-30-300 "a simple management heuristic … without much contextual nuance" and lists as unresolved "when is a park too small to be counted in the '300' test?" **[D]** (ibid., Introduction, Discussion). Its own results show the '300' test is "patchy", with ~75% of Amsterdam's buildings within 500 m of a park **[D]** — so the Dutch capital would plausibly pass a loosened 300 m test in most places while failing canopy badly.

**Equity consequences.** The strongest quantitative test across 28 cities in 9 countries found that greening in an earlier decade is a relevant predictor of subsequent gentrification in 17 cities, but not in 11 — and "those cities that present a negative or non-relevant association for citywide green gentrification are mostly European (Amsterdam, Bristol, Lyon, Sheffield, Valencia, Vienna, and Dublin)" **[D]** (Anguelovski et al. 2022, Nat Commun 13:3816, Results). The paper also reports the unresolved size question: Rigolon and Németh found new parks near city centres trigger gentrification "regardless of their size and function … holds true for small parks", while other US and Chinese studies found small parks *not* linked to gentrification **[D]** (Introduction). For Eindhoven, green gentrification is an untested hypothesis, not a finding **[U]** — the city is not among the 28.

## 4. Dutch cool-place guidance: what the figures actually are

The compound claim "within ~300 m and at least ~200 m²" is **partly verified, partly a conflation**, and is guidance, not a binding requirement in any source retrieved.

Soms wordt in verband met andere kwetsbare doelgroepen ook wel 200 meter aangehouden." | Kennisportaal Klimaatadaptatie (national knowledge portal), stress-test annex, undated page, retrieved 2026 | Guidance; **WHO attribution not verified** — see below |
| 300 m | "In deze richtlijn wordt een afstand van 300 meter geadviseerd tot een koelteplek die minimaal een afmeting van 200m2 schaduw moet hebben." | Platform31/Hogeschool van Amsterdam, summarising *De Hittebestendige stad* (HvA, 2020) | Research-based design guideline |

| 400 m² + 9 m²/person | "Bij een minimale koele oppervlakte van 200 m2 betekent dit dat gehele aangename koele plekken … minimaal 400 m2" and "minimaal 9 m2 per bewoner" | Kennisportaal Klimaatadaptatie, explaining the Platform31 definition | Advice, explicitly "niet in de standaard landelijke kaart" |

Three corrections follow. (i) The **200 m²** is a *shade* area in the HvA/Platform31 logic, which is why the total cool place doubles to **400 m²** — citing "200 m²" as the size of a cool place understates the Dutch guidance by half. (ii) The **300 m** figure has more than one lineage, and the portal's WHO attribution is unverified: I could not retrieve the WHO 2016 text (IRIS returned a JavaScript stub; the WHO landing page confirms the publication and its "green space accessibility indicator", not the number). Treat "WHO advises 300 m" as **[C]** until that PDF is read; the Dutch research lineage (HvA 2020 → Platform31 2023) is **[D]**. (iii) Nothing here binds anyone: the national map is a screening tool, Platform31's product is "advies", and the heat-plan guidance states "Een lokaal hitteplan is niet verplicht voor gemeenten, maar wordt wel sterk aangeraden" **[D]**.

**Eindhoven has adopted the 300 m figure — as an ambition, in a draft.** The *Ontwerp Omgevingsprogramma Klimaatadaptatie 2027–2032* (September 2026) states: "We werken volgens de 3-30-300 regel … Daarnaast willen we dat iedereen vanaf 2030 binnen vijf minuten lopen van zijn woning een koele plek kan bereiken. Dit is ongeveer 300 meter" **[D]** (§3.3, "De 3-30-300 regel"). Its analysis uses the national map: "Afstand tot koelte: bewoners moeten binnen vijf minuten lopen (circa 300 meter) een koele plek hebben bij zeer warme dagen" **[D]** (Bijlage 3, p. 45). The target is paired with route-shade indicators — "minimaal 40% schaduw" on main walking and cycling routes, "minimaal 30%" on neighbourhood footpaths — and with "We maken een hitteplan" **[D]**. Two qualifications: the document is an *ontwerp* due for adoption around December 2026, and it is "bindend voor de gemeentelijke organisatie" — binding inside the municipality, not on developers or residents **[D]**. No 200 m² or 400 m² criterion appears in the pages read **[D for absence]**.

## 5. Counterevidence and null findings

- Proximity did not predict park use in the one multivariate test retrieved; orientation did **[D]**.
- Older adults, the highest-risk group, were the *least* represented among actual cooling-centre users **[D]**.
- A systematic review of cooling centres found no study measuring health outcomes, and a modelling NNT of one million **[D, ABS]**.
- Phone-based catchment estimates fall off over miles, not metres, and fall faster for carless and minority neighbourhoods — which cuts both ways: it supports local provision and undermines the idea that "nearby" is sufficient **[D/I]**.
- Green gentrification is real in 17 of 28 cities but absent or negative in 11, Amsterdam among them; and the small-park evidence is contradictory **[D]**. A food forest is not automatically a displacement engine, nor automatically safe.
- Tree shade is discounted by pedestrians relative to building shade **[D]** — a caution against equating "shaded by trees" with "perceived cool".
- Indoor exposure dominates (80–90% of time indoors; near 100% for nursing-home residents) **[D]** — an outdoor-network claim cannot reach the most exposed people on its own.

## 6. What is not established in the sources searched

1. Catchment, visit frequency or dwell time for 200–400 m² cool places, in the Netherlands or anywhere **[U]**.
2. Whether a *cluster* of small spaces outperforms one larger space for use (the Nanjing mini-park study could not be read) **[U]**.
3. Benches, toilets, drinking water and opening hours as measured determinants of Dutch cool-place use **[U]**; only transport, awareness, hours and safety are quantified, and only in Arizona.
4. Renters as a distinct exposure or access category **[U]**.
5. Children's independent access to cool places **[U]**.
6. Whether any Eindhoven green space currently qualifies as a "koele plek" under the 200 m² / PET < 35 °C definition, and how many residents meet the 300 m ambition today **[U]** (the Lead's dataset line).
7. Whether the 300 m figure originates with WHO **[C]**.

## 9. What would change this answer

1. The HvA 2023 guideline PDF or the Nanjing mini-park paper in full — either could convert two **[U]** items (cluster effects; refined cool-place criteria) into findings.
2. A Dutch observational or intercept study of who sits in 200–400 m² cool places, with age and disability recorded.
3. Eindhoven's own "Afstand tot koelte" map output, showing the current share of dwellings within 300 m — which would turn the 2030 ambition into a baseline with a gap.
4. Any measured route-shade intervention showing changed walking or park entry, rather than stated preference.
5. Evidence that the 300 m norm traces to WHO (the 2016 PDF) would upgrade S1 from **[C]** to **[D]**; failure to find it would suggest the norm is a planning lineage (HvA/3-30-300), not a health recommendation.
