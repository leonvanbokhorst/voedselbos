## 1. Answer to the research question

**Short answer: a community food forest is a plausible multifunctional *shade-and-care* intervention in Eindhoven. The retrieved evidence supports local radiant-comfort mechanisms, but neither establishes an edible-specific cooling advantage nor quantifies the aggregate contribution of a distributed network to city-wide heat reduction.** The latter remains an open, scale-dependent hypothesis, not a demonstrated benefit or a disproven possibility.

Six findings carry the answer.

1. **Eindhoven's heat problem is documented as a modelled daytime comfort deficit, rather than a retrieved local measurement series. The city's draft identifies deficits of *distance* and *shade*; height alone does not explain them.** The draft climate-adaptation programme states that "not every resident has access within five minutes' walking (about 300 m) to a cool or green place, while this *is* a goal in our environmental vision", names the neighbourhood types where the distance is too large (Tuindorp-era and the newer high-rise areas, the centre with offshoots into Tongelre, Woensel Zuid, Strijp-S, Stratum and Gestel), and names Strijp-S and Fellenoord as places that need attention [D, `E6`]. The same record attributes the mechanism to hard surfacing, little street green, little public space and reduced ventilation at low sky-view factor — not to tall buildings as such [D, `E3`,`E6`].
2. **No local urban heat measurement series or independently validated municipal heat map was retrieved.** Its one municipal heat product is a tiles-only rendered raster ("Gevoelstemperatuur 2050 Eindhoven") produced by Tauw in March 2021, whose own service metadata carries **no scenario, no reference period, no legend, no method and no validation**, and whose metadata endpoint returns 404 [D, `E1`]. The only primary method description retrieved is a **2022 project study** — 1×1 m PET, mean 12:00–18:00, one DPRA 1-in-1000 heat day fixed to **1 July 2015**, forced by KNMI station Eindhoven, with the authors warning that another heat day would move results "by several degrees" [D, `E3`]. The national 2050 product is documented and reproducible but is **KNMI'14 WH**, computed at 1 m, published at 2 m, rounded to whole degrees, validated in Amsterdam, Rotterdam and Wageningen with no Eindhoven validation documented in that source [D, `E4`] — and the scenarios behind it were superseded by KNMI'23 in October 2023 [C, secondary, `E5`]. Whether the municipal 2050 raster uses WH2050 at all is **[U]**.
3. **Shade is the best-evidenced cooling mechanism available, and the retrieved evidence concerns generic vegetation mechanisms (shade and transpiration), not an independently established edible-specific effect.** The national method note states the mechanism plainly: "shade formation is the most effective cooling principle" for perceived temperature, and the sun-versus-tree-shade difference "can be more than 10 °C" [D, `E4`]. Measured and modelled studies give dense canopy an 11 °C improvement in experienced temperature against 4 °C for sparse canopy, with a 30–40 % canopy threshold for effects on air and surface temperature [D, `A`]. **No study retrieved isolates edible or agroforestry vegetation against a matched non-edible control at the same site, and none measures thermal comfort in a temperate or NW-European food forest** [U, coverage in §3.3]. A "food forest cools more than a park" claim is therefore inference, not finding.
4. **Two important constraints on new planting, including food forests, are time to useful canopy and water availability.** The municipal heat study's own conclusion is conditional on maturity — the mapped improvement "is only reached when the newly planted trees are fully grown", and the early phase will not have it [D, `E3`]; the same caveat recurs in the literature, where the first decade of a tree's life "generally offer[s] very limited shade" [D, `A`]. And drought is a documented local constraint: lowest groundwater is below 2 m across almost the whole municipality, long dry periods kill shallow-rooted planting and cause tree die-off, the city's own plant vitality is **75 % in 2026 against a 90 % target**, and drought raises pressure on both urban green and drinking water [D, `E6`]. A new planting reliant on potable water in July may compete for the resource the same document seeks to protect; actual demand and alternatives are site-dependent.
5. **Distributed small green places form part of the city's proposed response to the distance problem, but the object it counts is defined too thinly to test.** The ambition map answers "distance to coolth too large" with "**zoekgebied extra pocketparks**", plus greening cycle routes, cemeteries as cool places and green playground associations [D, `E6`]. But a "green verblijfsplek" (green place to stay) is defined as "a place outside with green and (partly) shade" — **no minimum area, no seating, no water, no opening hours, no accessibility** — and the 300 m figure is a walking distance: the 200 m² that travels with it is a **national map-screening criterion** (PET < 35 °C, public access, ≥200 m²), and the national guidance states explicitly that accessibility, safety and sanitary provision are *not* in it [D, `E6`,`C` — verified by Lead]. Whether 300 m of map distance corresponds to a place a heat-vulnerable resident can actually reach and use is **[U]** for Eindhoven; no public cool-place inventory, "distance to coolth" layer or canopy baseline value was retrieved, although a per-neighbourhood canopy baseline *measured in 2024* is announced [D, `E6`,`E9`].
6. **Shared care offers a plausible complementary social function; its contribution to heat resilience remains unestablished.** Recurring garden participation is associated with new neighbourhood contacts and with a reporting asymmetry that matters: 8–9 in 10 Dutch volunteers say they have met new people, but only **4 in 10** say they now dare *ask* for help — and the evaluation notes that asking is always harder than giving [D, `D`]. No retrieved study shows garden participants checking on, watering for or sheltering neighbours during a heat event, no retrieved heat plan establishes such a role for garden volunteers, and the retrieved cooling-centre review found no studies conclusively measuring health outcomes directly after use [U, `C`,`D`]. Watering restrictions and coordinator fatigue are documented risks in other settings [D, `D`]; the magnitude and distribution of heat-related care burden in Eindhoven remain unknown. The potential combination of social infrastructure, enjoyable shared care and thermal refuge warrants investigation; it is not an established heat-response capability.

**So the answer to "how can community-driven urban food forests contribute" is conditional and narrower than the question's framing:**

- **As cooling:** assess local shade and radiant comfort, existing and maturing canopy, ventilation and site-specific water needs. The city's consultant estimates **+10 percentage points of green ≈ −0.5 °C at city scale** [D, `E3`]; this is a context-dependent estimate, not a universal upper bound or a prediction for distributed food forests. District-scale and indoor effects are not quantified by this report.
- **As adaptation infrastructure:** test whether distributed, accessible edible cool places can complement street trees, pocket parks, schoolyards and playgrounds identified by the draft programme [D, `E6`]. Compare actual coverage, use, thermal performance and maintenance against non-edible alternatives; neither distributed superiority nor any specific location is established.
- **As social infrastructure:** recurring presence and shared care may create opportunities for encounter, pleasure, learning and mutual recognition, including for people who do not garden. Whether those relationships support protective action during heat events is unknown. Access to a cool place must not depend on contributing labour or agreeing to a health intervention. Any formal heat-response role would require separate consent, capacity and appropriate professional coordination [I/U].
- **Not as a substitute** for statutory heat response, public green maintenance, or the municipal water and canopy obligations [D/I].

## 3. Evidence

### 3.1 Eindhoven's heat problem, as the record states it

**What the city says about itself.** The draft programme's heat section is the most concrete municipal statement retrieved [D, `E6`]:

> "Hittestress heeft niet op iedereen dezelfde invloed. Ouderen, jonge kinderen en mensen met gezondheidsproblemen zijn extra kwetsbaar. Het toenemende aantal warme nachten speelt hierbij een belangrijke rol, omdat het lichaam zich dan niet goed kan herstellen. Vooral in wijken met weinig schaduw en koele plekken zijn de gevolgen merkbaar. Niet iedere inwoner heeft binnen vijf minuten lopen (ongeveer 300 meter) toegang tot een koele of groene (verblijfs)plek, terwijl dit wel een doel is in onze omgevingsvisie."

That single passage carries four things the main question needs: **night-time heat is named as the mechanism of harm** (recovery, not just daytime discomfort); **vulnerability is specified by group**; **the deficit is defined as distance and shade**; and **the 300 m standard is stated as an unmet goal, not a measured condition**.

**Where the city locates the problem** [D, `E6`, pp.46–47]:

> "Binnen de ring: er zijn diverse wijken binnen de ring van Eindhoven waar de afstand tot koele plekken in de openbare ruimte te groot is, met name wijken die zijn gebouwd met de Tuindorpgedachte en de recentere hoogbouwgebieden. Dit concentreert zich met name in het centrum met uitlopers naar Tongelre, Woensel Zuid, Strijp S en directe omgeving (!), Stratum en Gestel. Dit zijn allemaal dichtbebouwde wijken, met weinig groen in de straat en weinig openbare ruimte."

> "Ook is de verwachting dat Fellenoord een zeer warme plek gaat worden." … "Groen arme buurten. Dit zijn buurten met weinig groen en veel verstening."

Two readings of "hoogbouwgebieden" are possible and the document does not decide between them: that height *per se* is the problem, or that recent high-rise districts happen to have little street-level green and public space. The second is supported by the consultant's own mechanism note — adding height and trees lowers the sky-view factor, so "there is less cooling air exchange through wind and the heat stays 'stuck' in the plan" [D, `E3` §4.2] — which is a **ventilation** effect, not a height effect, and it would apply equally to a dense low-rise courtyard. This report adopts the second reading as **[I]**, not as finding.

**The night is the weak point.** The municipal products are daytime-only: the 2050 raster is described as the perceived temperature "on an extremely hot summer afternoon" [D, `E1`], and the documented method averages 12:00–18:00 [D, `E3`,`E4`]. Night-time heat exists in Eindhoven's policy text as a stated harm and in the national atlas as a raster of average tropical nights per year, built on **KNMI'14 WH** and derived from **Rotterdam** measurements translated nationally by paving, green and mean building height [D, `E7`]. **Nothing Eindhoven-specific measures night-time heat** [U].

**Indoor exposure is explicitly out of scope of the municipal heat work** — "this study says nothing about indoor temperatures" [D, `E3` §5] — even though the same document notes indoor heat can cause health complaints in vulnerable groups. Any claim that a food forest improves indoor thermal conditions is unsupported by anything retrieved here [U].

**The consequence for the main question is severe and simple.** A food forest's contribution could in principle be tested exactly this way — a before/after and with/without-comparator PET difference — and the city already commissions such studies for individual developments: the 2022 Emmasingelkwadrant study reports that the design keeps perceived temperature "in many places below 35 °C (strong heat stress)" and turns a car park into "a cool pleasant place to stay", **conditional on the trees being mature** [D, `E3` §4.1]. But no such study was retrieved for an edible site in the city [U], the municipal raster cannot be interrogated for values, and the one published instrument that could rank sites (the "distance to coolth" map used in the knelpuntenanalyse) is **not public** [D, `E6`,`E9`].

### 3.3 Cooling mechanisms and their limits

**The mechanism that matters is radiant, and it is shade.** [D, `A`,`E4`]

- "Schaduwvorming is het meest effectieve verkoelingsprincipe om de gevoelstemperatuur te verlagen. Het verschil tussen gevoelstemperatuur in de zon en in de schaduw van een boom kan meer dan 10 °C zijn." [D, `E4` p.9]
- Dense canopy "can reduce temperatures experienced by humans by 11 °C, compared to only 4 °C with sparse canopy coverage"; canopy "exceeding 30–40 %" is where shading and evapotranspiration begin to lower surface *and* air temperature; canopy "exceeding 40 %, within 60 m of housing" was needed for significant cooling in the Wisconsin study [D, `A`].
- Radiant load "constitutes the largest part of the heat load" on people and explains most of the variance in thermal perception, while air temperature "is characterized by rather small daytime spatial variations" [D, `A`].

**The air-temperature effect is small, and can be diminished during extreme heat.** Street trees "can cool summer daytime air temperatures by up to 1.5 °C, yet … the cooling effect of trees during extreme heat events was not significant between streets with and without trees" [D, `A`]. Six years of hourly data at an urban farm with pervious soils and transpiring crops found **no significant difference in mean monthly air temperature** against nearby tree-covered sites, only ~1 °C cooler July–August minima [D, ABS, `A`]. And the city's own consultant's scale rule — **+10 percentage points of green ≈ −0.5 °C at city level**, "not much when it is more than 30 °C" [D, `E3` §5] — is a context-dependent consultant estimate, not a physical ceiling or a food-forest-specific result.

**Evapotranspiration is real but water-limited.** Species-specific collapse under drought is documented: *Platanus × hispanica* −61 % growth in soil drought, *Robinia pseudoacacia* −58 % transpiration under extreme drought, trees in paved or compacted sites "up to 60 % reductions in both structural growth and cooling performance" [D, `A`]. This is the same failure mode the Eindhoven programme describes from the other direction: shallow-rooted plants die first, and low, fluctuating groundwater causes tree loss [D, `E6`].

**What is specifically, distinctively a food forest?** From the retrieved evidence: **nothing thermal.** The only food-forest temperature study found is a subtropical municipal programme evaluation reporting an average 2.4 °C cooling with an unidentifiable indicator, a small sample and a limited observation period [D, ABS, `A`]; the only Dutch food-forest measurement retrieved is a **water balance** (larger storage despite lower soil organic matter, with explicitly greater evaporation from the many vegetation layers — a trade-off, not a cooling figure) [D, `A`]. The nearest edible-specific evidence with any weight is a Europe-wide allotment mapping using **land-surface temperature**, which cannot be converted into human comfort [D, ABS, `A`].

**Interpretation [I].** A food forest's thermal performance is a function of **structure** (canopy density, height, coverage, continuity, sky-view obstruction, soil water) and of **where it is**, not of edibility. Edible species and management choices may affect canopy structure, water demand, use and care; no independent cooling advantage from edibility has been demonstrated. That is the pivot of this report: the food forest's climate case must be made on shade and care, not on the label.

### 3.4 Alternatives, and what an edible system costs

**Canopy is the strongest vegetation instrument, by a wide margin.** Across 293 European cities, land-surface temperature under urban trees is "8-12 K lower in Central Europe" against continuous urban fabric, while "treeless urban green spaces are overall less effective … and their cooling effect is approximately 2-4 times lower than the cooling induced by urban trees" [D, `B`]. The same paper records two limits that matter here: "the cooling provided by urban trees decreases during hot extremes" in southern Europe, and "irrigation could help to maintain the high cooling provided by vegetation in these regions but may be limited by future water scarcity" [D]. **A shaded park is a canopy intervention and inherits this evidence; a mown treeless park is closer to grass.**

**Water decides whether any vegetation option performs.** In Dutch field experiments, "dry turf grass could be between 5 and 25 °C warmer than irrigated grass", and paving "can easily be 20-30 degrees warmer than the grass" and "remained 20 degrees warmer … during the evening and much of the night"; mowing height did not matter, irrigation did [D, `B`]. This is the same mechanism the Eindhoven programme encounters from the groundwater side [D, `E6`].

**Built shade can work immediately and has a documented failure mode.** In an Australian school study the lowest air and surface temperatures were "consistently recorded under tree canopies", and shading reduced surface temperature "by up to 20 °C", lowering "feels like" temperatures by 10 °C; but air temperature rose most — "by 10.0-11.0 °C" — where shade came from corrugated iron or dark shade cloth, and the fully shaded playground was still the warmest location because "the dark shade cloth above the playground caused air to heat up and become trapped under the shade structure" [D, `B`]. The honest reading is a failure mode (dark, low-albedo cover over hot surfacing in still air), not a general ranking: in the same study one pergola location (24.3 °C) was cooler than one tree location (24.5 °C) [D/I, `B`].

**Blue infrastructure is modest and partially counterproductive at night.** Across 67 studies: mean daytime cooling 2.6 °C, night-time *heating* 0.6 °C, with daytime figures varying by method from 1.4 °C (field) to 3.4 °C (remote sensing) [D, ABS, `B`]. For a city that names night-time recovery as the harm [D, `E6`], water is not a night instrument.

**Edible systems: the cooling is unmeasured, the non-thermal constraints are well documented.**

- *No isolated cooling evidence.* No retrieved study compares an edible plot against a matched non-edible control on one site with PET/UTCI/MRT [U, `A`,`B`]. The most-cited "urban heat" citation in the food-forest literature measures no temperature at all: a Miami-Dade school study found "the food forest canopy was comparable with neighborhood urban tree cover" alongside higher species richness and stem density [D, ABS, `B`] — and a 2025 scoping review of 44 temperate studies still repeats this as evidence that food forests "reduce urban heat", which is citation creep [D, `B`].
- *Establishment.* "The establishment phase: year 0–3"; land for food forests is "often marginal and degraded, requiring several years of soil remediation"; "drought can be challenging, so having water rights close to or on the land is crucial for the establishment period"; and "most food forests studied in literature are younger than five years old" [D, `B`]. On a heat-critical site the first years are small stems and bare ground.
- *Water.* English allotments (163 gardeners, 14,992 visits, 2018): median **7,595 L/yr** per ~250 m² plot, of which **5,993 L from mains**; **80.8 %** used in summer; 30.4 L/m²/yr and 16.9 L per kg produce. The decisive finding: "winter stocks of rainwater harvested by individual gardeners are insufficient in the majority of cases to meet the water demands of an allotment into the summer months", and "if UH is to be upscaled with its current reliance on mains water, the pressure on the urban water supply would be exacerbated" [D, `B`].
- *Soil and food safety.* Dutch generic values put the lead intervention value at 530 mg/kg but the "maximale waarde moestuin" at **70 mg/kg** [D, child-reported from RIVM 607711004, Tabel 2.1 — not re-verified by the Lead]. The official advice where ground is contaminated is not to grow in it: "prevent taking in lead through food from your garden by growing vegetables in containers with clean soil" [D, verified by Lead, `B`]. EU food-law limits (0.050–0.30 mg/kg depending on crop group) apply to marketed produce, not to own-grown food [D, `B`]. Countervailing: RIVM's revised uptake model found no unacceptable garden among 13 contaminated sites (risk indices ≤0.5) [D, `B`] — so this is a **site check**, not a general veto.
- *Maintenance and competence.* Median 150 h/yr and 87 visits per plot [D, `B`]; food-forest-specific burdens are matching harvest to demand "to avoid unharvested fruit attracting pests or becoming unsightly", municipal knowledge gaps on caring for urban fruit trees, and by-laws that "prohibit the removal of plant material, directly conflicting with the purpose of food forests" [D, `B`].
- *Ecological trade-off.* In a German field experiment, native insects made "67 % of all visits" to wild perennials against 24 % to related ornamentals and 9 % to unrelated exotics [D, `B`] — "edible" and "biodiverse" are not synonyms, and for temperate food forests "few papers provide evidence for the environmental benefits", with "no studies specifically focused on biodiversity effects or water quality impacts" [D, `B`].

**Where edible planting is the wrong intervention on this evidence** [I, from `B`]: where urgent shade needs cannot be met by the existing or proposed vegetation without complementary shade measures; where soil lead exceeds the garden standard (the intervention becomes container horticulture, a different project); where projected irrigation would create unacceptable demand on potable water; where no one is accountable for harvest and care (unharvested fruit, pests and by-laws are documented failure modes); where habitat is the primary objective; and where the site cannot carry stratified woody planting (narrow canyon, shallow rooting volume, sightlines).

### 3.5 Who is exposed, who reaches, who uses

**Exposure is documented at the level the Netherlands measures it: housing and income, not body.** RIVM states that "people in a financially vulnerable position often lack the means to take cooling measures" and that "residents of old, poorly insulated homes are more exposed to heat", with isolated older people often unable to seek cooling in a park [D, `C`]. The 2026 national heat-plan guide places the gradient geographically — overheated or unadaptable homes are "often … in neighbourhoods with a high percentage of residents with a low socio-economic position" [D, `C`] — and names 75+, young children, chronically ill, homeless people, people with mental-health or intellectual disabilities, outdoor workers, refugees and pregnant women [D, `C`]. **But the same national assessment bounds any outdoor intervention: "people spend about 80–90 % of their time indoors", and nursing-home residents "nearly 100 % of the time"** [D, `C`].

**Proximity alone does not establish use.** In a Brisbane survey (n = 1,479) park area within 250 m differed between users and non-users, but "nature orientation" carried the largest coefficient (β = 0.570) against "park availability" (β = 0.133/0.182), and at 1 km park cover "was not significant"; "non-park users comprised nearly 40 % of our survey population" [D, `C`]. Mobility data point the same way and further: ~60 million anonymised park visits in 20 US metros show exponential distance decay with metropolitan half-distances of roughly 2–5 miles, decaying faster "in neighborhoods with larger shares of households without a vehicle, and in those with larger shares of residents from racial and ethnic minority groups", while conceding that phone data "may underrepresent older and lower-income residents" [D, press-release level, `C`].

**Some groups at risk were underrepresented among users of cooling centres in one study.** In Maricopa County (944 cooling-centre visitors, 1,260 public respondents, August–September 2023, average daily high 42.2 °C): visitors were "65 % versus 12 %" homeless, while "a higher percentage of the public than cooling center visitors were aged ≥65 years (45 % versus 16 %)" [D, **verified by Lead**, `C`]. Barriers: no transportation 31 % of visitors, unaware that cooling centres exist 36 % of visitors and 49 % of the public, unusable hours 14 %, safety concerns 11 %, pets not allowed 7 % [D, `C`]. A systematic review of six studies found "no studies conclusively measured health and wellbeing outcomes directly after cooling centre use", barriers of "accessibility, operational hours, and public perceptions", and a modelled number-needed-to-treat of one million to avoid one heat death [D, ABS, `C`].

**Route shade shifts behaviour, but less than designers assume.** A forced-choice experiment in Singapore found "the distance walked in the shade is discounted by a factor of 0.86 compared to the distance walked in the sun", and "tree shade is perceived as 50 % as intense as … building shade" [D, `C`] — a caution against equating "shaded by trees" with "perceived cool", and against assuming an edible canopy reads as refuge.

**The distributed-network hypothesis: plausible for proximity and untested for comparative canopy, catchment and use.**

- *Supported*: distance decay is steep and steeper for people with fewer mobility options, suggesting a potential access advantage for distributed places, though this is not a direct comparison of small and large cool places in Eindhoven [D, `C`].
- *Unsupported*: no retrieved study quantifies the catchment, visit frequency or dwell time of a 200–400 m² cool place [U]; the one directly relevant study on mini-park *clusters* (Nanjing, *Cities* 2022) could not be read [U]; and the three 3-30-300 components are "very weak[ly]" related across 2.5 million buildings in eight cities, with the correlation between tree views and distance to parks "close to zero" [D, `C`] — **proximity, canopy and actual usability must be evaluated separately.**
- *Contested*: 3-30-300 is called "a simple management heuristic … without much contextual nuance", with "when is a park too small to be counted in the '300' test?" listed as unresolved [D, `C`].
- *Equity*: across 28 cities in 9 countries, earlier greening predicted later gentrification in 17 cities and not in 11 — "mostly European (Amsterdam, Bristol, Lyon, Sheffield, Valencia, Vienna, and Dublin)" [D, `C`]. For Eindhoven this is an untested hypothesis, not a finding [U].

**The Dutch "300 m / 200 m²" claim, verified.** The compound figure is real but composite, and as usually stated one number is wrong [D, **verified by Lead**, `C`]:

> "De World Health Organization (WHO) adviseert een afstand van 300 meter lopen tot koelte. Soms wordt in verband met andere kwetsbare doelgroepen ook wel 200 meter aangehouden." (Kennisportaal Klimaatadaptatie)

> "De landelijke kaart gebruikt de volgende definitie voor een koele plek: De gevoelstemperatuur moet onder de 35 graden liggen. De plek moet openbaar toegankelijk zijn, ook voor mensen met een beperking. De koele oppervlakte moet minstens 200 m2 groot zijn. Er moet een minimale afstand tot wegen zijn: 5 meter … 10 meter … 15 meter …" (ibid.)

> "In 2023 is een nadere definitie van aangename koele verblijfsplekken gemaakt … Daarin staat dat koele plekken ook plekken zijn met deels zon en deels schaduw (50 % - 50 % …). Bij een minimale koele oppervlakte van 200 m2 betekent dit dat gehele aangename koele plekken vooral ook groot genoeg moeten zijn: minimaal 400 m2. Dit past ook bij de eis dat in de buitenruimte minimaal 9 m2 per bewoner beschikbaar moet zijn … Deze ontwerpeisen staan niet in de landelijke kaart. Het zijn adviezen voor vervolgkaarten." (ibid.)

So: **300 m is guidance with an unverified WHO lineage** [C — the WHO text itself could not be retrieved]; **200 m² is a cool *shade* area in the national screening definition, and the design guidance for a pleasant cool place is 400 m² plus 9 m² per resident**; and the national guidance says explicitly that the standard map does **not** include accessibility, safety or sanitary provision — the three things that decide use [D]. The heat-plan guide likewise states "a local heat plan is not compulsory for municipalities, but is strongly advised" [D, `C`].

**Eindhoven's position.** The city has adopted the 300 m figure as a **2030 ambition in a draft**: "we want everyone from 2030 to be able to reach a cool place within five minutes' walking of their home. That is about 300 metres", paired with 40 %/30 % route-shade indicators and a heat plan to be drawn up [D, `E6`]. The document is an *ontwerp* (adoption expected around December 2026) and binds the municipal organisation, not developers or residents [D, `C`]. No 200 m² or 400 m² criterion appears in the pages read [D for absence].

### 3.6 Shared care: what it buys, what it costs, what it cannot do

**Social connection is documented, modestly and mostly by self-report.** In the Dutch *Groen Verbindt* evaluation (27→23 care-and-neighbourhood gardens, 2015–2018; volunteer survey N = 166; street surveys N = 576/651 with 466 never-visitors as comparison), "eight to nine in ten volunteers" agreed they had met new people in the neighbourhood, and 8/10 that they had learned more about nature and green; but only **"4/10"** agreed that "in the past year I dare ask more people for help" [D, **verified by Lead**, `D`]. The report itself names the asymmetry: "asking for help is always more difficult than [being there for others]" [D]. The volunteers are "fairly highly educated — about half have a university or HBO education", against 35–37 % of surrounding residents in the same measure [D, **verified by Lead**, `D`]. **Giving is reported more readily than asking; that is a weak-tie finding, not a mutual-aid safety net.**

**The chain from connection to protection has a missing link.** Garden → social tie is documented (including one natural experiment: clearing a Zurich allotment site measurably reduced gardeners' number of social contacts and emotional well-being [D, ABS, `D`]). Isolation → heat death is documented from other literatures, including a cited association between reduced social gathering among older men and 2 % higher heatwave mortality [D, secondary, `D`]. **Garden tie → protective action during a heat event is documented nowhere retrieved** [U, `D`]. The interventions that do show an effect are professional or trained-volunteer programmes — Philadelphia's heat "buddy" system, the French municipal heatwave registry with proactive calls, Cologne's heat hotline, GP monitoring in Lazio, a Rome programme monitoring isolated over-80s (excess-death IRR 0.44, 0.32–0.60) [D, secondary, `D`]. Their active ingredient is a maintained list plus an assigned caller, not a shared garden [I]. A Dutch survey fielded directly after a July 2023 heatwave (n = 514, aged 50+) found heat managed almost entirely at household level — staying indoors, wet towels, fans, seeking shade — and the instrument, built from national heat-plan, Red Cross, GGD and RIVM advice, contains **no neighbour, community or garden channel at all** [D/I, `D`].

**Care costs can rise during heat and drought.** During the August 2022 Thames Water hosepipe ban, community gardens fell outside the legal categories of domestic, commercial or allotment, and only after lobbying were allotment holders permitted to water food crops "where no other means of watering is reasonably possible"; the water company cited "the logistical challenges of navigating large allotment sites with watering cans" [D, `D`]. The volunteer's job shifts from cultivation to hauling, and the garden's water becomes a regulated, contested input [I]. **Quantified irrigation labour, volumes or plant-loss rates for community gardens or food forests under drought are [U]** [D, `D`] — which is precisely the number Eindhoven's deep groundwater and 75 % plant vitality make most valuable.

**Limits, all documented.** Coordinator roles are "demanding multi-functional", producing "fatigue and feelings of over-commitment", and volunteer groups "were in exactly that position of having one or two dedicated leaders" — one resident: "you've not got long-term resilience if somebody moves away", with one group "in a precarious situation following the resignation of the founder" [D, `D`]. Exclusion is documented: volunteers in the Dutch programme are more educated than their neighbourhoods [D], and the wider literature describes groups "predominately white and middle-class" [D, secondary, `D`]. Structural loss of growing space is documented: allotment land in ten English cities fell 65 % from 1960 to 2016, with eight times more closures in deprived areas and waiting lists rising from under 10 to over 50 per 100 plots [D, secondary, `D`]. Programme ambition exceeded reality: the funder expected each garden to host 25 neighbours and 25 care clients weekly — "in almost no project succeeded", with monthly contact the realistic maximum [D, `D`]. And where volunteers moved into municipal green maintenance under austerity, the documented outcome was "reducing accountability and accessibility, and exacerbating inequities in parks provision" [D, ABS, `D`].

**The boundary this report adopts [I]:** shared care may sustain relationships and places. It does not establish emergency coverage, liability arrangements, continuity, water rights or 24/7 response. Participation in any formal heat response would require separately agreed roles, consent and professional safeguards. The examples of unequal provision after municipal maintenance was devolved are reasons to test these arrangements, not reasons to discount the intrinsic value of voluntary care.

### 3.7 What spatial evidence Eindhoven actually offers

[D, `E8`,`E9`]

- **Trees: yes, canopy: no.** The municipal tree register is public and large — **127,870 records**, with owner (municipal/private), manager, species, a **height class**, planting year and status. It has **no crown diameter or crown area**, so canopy cover cannot be computed from it, and its own metadata warns that it is incomplete and that unlisted trees may still be protected, "for example in green-poor areas which are additionally protected under the ordinance".
- **No heat, coolth or canopy dataset in the open-data portal.** A catalogue query for *hitte*, *koelte* and *klimaat* returns ten datasets (open-data principles, four water-vision maps, `vergroeningsprojecten`, `bomen`, groundwater `ghg_glg`, NO₂ measurements). **No PET grid, no "afstand tot koelte" layer, no canopy layer.**
- **What exists as policy is not accessible as data.** The knelpuntenanalyse, the ambition map and the distance-to-coolth map are printed figures in a PDF [D, `E6`]; the canopy baseline is announced as measured per neighbourhood and its value is not published [D, `E6`].

**Consequence [I]:** the "distributed network of edible cool refuges" cannot be defensibly allocated or ranked from the retrieved public evidence alone. A defensible ranking would need, at minimum: a public cool-place inventory with the criteria that make a place usable; the 2024 canopy baseline per neighbourhood; the walking network with route shade; and soil/contamination and water checks. These prerequisites were not fully retrieved in this pass.

## 4. Counter-evidence and tensions

1. **The cooling contribution specific to a food forest remains unestablished.** No matched temperate PET/UTCI measurement was retrieved; an urban-farm air-temperature comparison reported a null result, and a tree study reported a non-significant air-temperature effect during extreme heat [D, ABS, `A`]. Those findings constrain the claim that a food forest changes air temperature, but do not refute local radiant-comfort benefits or the aggregate effect of a suitably designed network. Do not relabel the generic 11 °C dense-canopy figure as a food-forest measurement.
2. **Shade has a night-time cost.** "Rollout of canopy must occur thoughtfully, avoiding potential pitfalls such as night-time 'heat trapping' in narrow, poorly ventilated street canyons" [D, `A`]; the Eindhoven consultant makes the same point about lowering sky-view factor with height and trees [D, `E3` §4.2]. Since night-time heat is the mechanism the city names for harm [D, `E6`], a dense edible canopy on a narrow street could work against the stated goal while improving the daytime figure. **[I]**, untested locally.
3. **Water cuts both ways.** Multilayer vegetation both stores and evaporates more [D, `A`]; in a municipality whose lowest groundwater sits below 2 m nearly everywhere and whose green is already at 75 % vitality against a 90 % standard [D, `E6`], an irrigation-dependent edible planting is a liability unless a non-drinking water source is secured.
4. **The city's own numbers disagree with each other.** The shade standard is 50 % on walking routes in the measure list and 40 %/30 % in the monitoring section of the same draft [D, `E6`]; a reused sibling claim that the 3-30-300 rule has "no published baseline" needs qualification because a 2024 per-neighbourhood canopy baseline is announced (`EHV-C-0416` vs `E6`). Recorded, not reconciled.
5. **The 4–5 °C city-versus-countryside figure is a consultant's assertion, not a measurement** [C, `E2`] — no station, method or period — and it should not be used as an Eindhoven UHI magnitude.
6. **A reused claim is narrower than its own source.** The sibling evidence base's `EHV-C-0428` states the heat map is "validated only in Wageningen"; the retrieved national method note reports quantitative validation in **Amsterdam, Rotterdam and Wageningen** [D, `E4` §3]. The two may refer to different objects (WUR's method note versus Witteveen+Bos's implementation), but as stated the reused claim is too narrow. The substantive point — no Eindhoven validation — stands.
7. **The access evidence cuts against the promise of a cool refuge.** The group with the highest heat risk was the least present in provided cool places (16 % of visitors aged 65+ against 45 % of the at-risk public) [D, `C`]; a systematic review found no study measuring health outcomes from cool-place use [D, ABS, `C`]; and 80–90 % of time is spent indoors, so an outdoor network cannot reach the most exposed people on its own [D, `C`].
8. **The edible label is not a biodiversity credential, and the empirical food-forest literature is thin.** Native plants drew 67 % of insect visits against 24 % and 9 % for related ornamentals and unrelated exotics [D, `B`], and a 44-study review found "no studies specifically focused on biodiversity effects or water quality impacts" [D, `B`].
9. **The Dutch social-value evidence is self-report without controls, and its own numbers are modest** [C, `D`]; "social resilience" is itself a contested measurement object whose indicators embed normative choices about "whose resilience" is being measured [D, ABS, `D`].
10. **A contradiction inside one document, preserved.** Eindhoven's draft programme states the shade standard as "50 % shadow on walking routes" in its measure list and as "at least 40 % … on important walking and cycling routes … at least 30 % on neighbourhood footpaths" in its monitoring section [D, `E6`]. Neither version has a published baseline, so neither can be audited yet.

## 8. What would change the answer

1. **One paired field campaign**: an NW-European food forest against an adjacent park, lawn and paved square, same days, reporting T_a, T_mrt and PET/UTCI at 1.5 m, including night. This single study converts most of §3.3's inference into documentation.
2. **Publication of the 2024 canopy baseline, the cool-place inventory and the distance-to-coolth layer.** With these, a distributed-network proposal becomes siteable and falsifiable; without them it is a hypothesis with a plausible map legend.
3. **Evidence on care under heat**: whether watering rotas and volunteer presence survive a heatwave, and what the irrigation source is. This decides whether edible care is a resilience asset or a liability in exactly the week it is needed.
4. **A drought-year comparison** of food-forest water retention against lawn and street trees — the most plausible *food-forest-specific* advantage, currently untested.
5. **A revised heat map on KNMI'23** (the second-round DPRA stress test was carried out in 2026 [D, `E6`]; its scenarios and results are not public in the sources searched).
6. **An Eindhoven cool-place audit**: how many dwellings currently meet the 300 m ambition under the national definition (PET < 35 °C, public, ≥200 m²), and how many of those places have seating, water and usable hours. 7. **One Dutch observational study of who actually sits in 200–400 m² cool places**, with age and disability recorded; and a study of garden participants through a real heat event. These two would decide whether the social case is a finding or a hope.

## 10. Implications for the main question

**[I] — interpretation, not finding.**

1. **Start with shade where people already are, without closing the city-scale question.** Local radiant comfort is the best-supported mechanism in the retrieved evidence. The additional district-scale or aggregate benefit of a distributed network remains open and would depend on coverage, canopy, water, spatial arrangement and access. Compare edible and non-edible cool places for thermal performance *and* opportunities for meaningful everyday presence, learning, encounter and shared care. Neither added social value nor city-wide cooling is established simply by planting.
2. **Sequence planting and near-term thermal comfort.** Existing mature trees, temporary or built shade and suitable water arrangements could complement gradually maturing planting. The timing and magnitude of benefits need site-specific assessment; the city's mature-canopy scenario is not a forecast of a young food forest.
3. **The strongest municipal openings are ordinary and already written down**: the canopy baseline and the 40 %/30 % shade standard on walking routes, the "zoekgebied extra pocketparks", the greening of schoolyards, playgrounds and sport parks, cycle-route greening, and the principle of social climate justice that prioritises areas with lower health scores [D, `E6`]. A community food-forest network can position itself as a *delivery partner* for those lines without needing a new policy.
4. **Continuity remains a separate enabling condition.** The closed Eindhoven food forest was on private land [D, `E10`]; the commissioner reports that the land was sold [P, see ../meaning/note.md]. The exact terms, decision and other causal contributors are not independently established. Hosting, tenure, succession and care capacity deserve investigation; loss of a site does not establish loss of its community. A cooling argument does not resolve these questions.
5. **Pillar 2 ("connect into a city-wide ecosystem") gains a concrete, non-symbolic purpose**: a shared canopy-and-shade standard, a public cool-place inventory, and a watering arrangement for dry summers are possible shared functions to investigate, without presuming a new organisation or volunteer responsibility. Pillar 3 ("nature closer to everyday life") is where proximity and route shade offer plausible design opportunities. Pillar 1 (De Wielewaal as "green motor") remains open as a potential host or destination; the retrieved evidence does not establish its comparative contribution to neighbourhood heat exposure.

---

**Review qualification (26 November 2026):** The evidence does not yet discriminate a multifunctional edible cool-place network from matched non-edible alternatives on combined thermal, ecological and experiential value. A future comparison could assess these dimensions separately (including unpurposed presence and the distribution of care), without assuming a health effect or reducing participation to maintenance capacity. This revision changes interpretation and claim scope, not the underlying source register or retrieval record.
