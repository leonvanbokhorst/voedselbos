### 1.2 What the programme layer actually shows

- **The unit of intervention is almost never the community; it is the referral route or the municipality.** Natuur op Recept's deliverable is a *doorverwijskaart* and a GP who asks about nature; Groen doet Goed's deliverable is a municipal contract; GNW's is a weekly walk. [D]
- **Where participants exist in the record, the evaluation is monitoring or a student thesis.** GNW has thousands of participants and a one-sentence research claim; ZBZH has 549 questionnaires and no comparison; VTTB has one qualitative thesis. The only Dutch programme in this set with a named external evaluator (GNW/RUG) publishes no design, N, instrument or reference. [D]
- **Nobody in this set measures literacy, learning or capability.** The nearest is VTTB's self-reported skills theme. [D]
- **Two documented capacity failure modes already appear in the programme layer:** GNW states "het aantal begeleiders heeft moeite dat bij te houden" and is developing partly professional-led variants to cope [D]; and the national green-health directory of 1,300 initiatives went offline for lack of a maintainable model [D].
- **An overlap worth flagging for the Lead:** Agnes van den Berg is listed as a GNW *bestuurder* in 2024 [D] and as an NFH council member in 2020 [D], and she directs the environmental-psychology research practice whose subject is nature and health [D, natuurvoormensen.nl]. GNW's research sentence attributes its evidence claims to "RUG". That the board member and the cited researcher are the same person is **[I]** — I did not retrieve a GNW publication author list. It matters because the claim is repeated as external validation.

## 2. Welzijn op Recept and social prescribing: what the evidence supports

### 2.1 The Dutch model

- **Description [D]:** "mensen met psychosociale klachten door de huisarts worden doorverwezen naar een welzijnscoach in het sociale domein. Samen met de cliënt zoekt de welzijnscoach naar een passende activiteit" — with the stated aim of raising wellbeing *and* reducing first-line care use (RIVM & TNO 2024-0027, full text). Nationally the most-used form of social referral in NL; GALA policy context.
- **The Dutch problem is durability, not effect [D]:** "Ondanks de groeiende aandacht … laat de praktijk zien dat de implementatie … op sommige plekken niet doorzet"; the intervention "blijft hangen in de fase waarin er op beperkte schaal ervaring wordt opgedaan"; referrals "stagneert, dan wel afneemt". Four experienced sites were followed; two of the four "bleken … keuzes te maken die ertoe leidden dat deze niet meer aan de definitie van Welzijn op Recept voldeden".
- **The referral model mis-fits complexity [D]:** in the Schilderswijk site "sluit de interventie Krachtige Basiszorg vaak beter aan bij de cliëntenbehoeften dan Welzijn op Recept", because of multi-problem clients — i.e. the qualifying condition (mild psychosocial complaints, willing to join an activity) excludes the people most present in the practice.
- **Dutch effect claims are asserted, not shown.** The ZonMW *Effectstudie Welzijn op Recept* (MOvactor, 2014–2015) page says results were "verrassend" and that "de invloed op zorggebruik ziet er positief uit", but reports no effect size, no N, no comparison (ZonMw project page, full text) [C]. I did not retrieve the underlying eindverslag [U].
- **The review base is about implementation.** The RIVM/TNO rapid realist review included 22 articles and produced five "leidende principes" (awareness, trust relations, link-worker skills, client support, cross-sector alignment) — a theory of *how to embed*, not of *whether it works* [D].

### 2.2 The UK green social prescribing programme: the best-resourced test, and what it did not show

- **Scale and reach [D]:** 8,339 people with mental health needs across seven "test and learn" sites; £5.77m; "57% of participants were from the most socio-economically deprived areas"; "21% of participants were from ethnic minority populations" — reach *better* than typical social prescribing (National Evaluation Final Report Summary, Jan 2024, full text).
- **Outcomes as reported [D]:** statistically significant improvement on all four ONS-4 items (happiness 5.3→7.5; life satisfaction 4.7→6.8; worthwhile 5.1→6.8; anxiety 4.8→3.4); one pilot's HADS depression 8.1→5.6 and anxiety 11.1→8.5. Nature connectedness improved in one site and showed "no improvement" in another. Cost per participant £419; SROI £2.42 per £1.
- **The design caveat is stated by the evaluators themselves [D]:** "Because the data was collected in routine settings and not for everyone who accessed GSP, it is unknown how representative the data is." And the baseline HADS were moderate: "The baseline scores were not particularly high indicating that GSP was supporting people primarily with pre-determinant and moderate mental health issues."
- **The evidence problem is named in the report, not by critics [D]:** "There is a persistent perception at local and national level that … evidence for GSP is not sufficiently compelling or rigorous and a lack of agreement around what evidence is needed."
- **Barriers to engagement, in the evaluators' words [D]:** "Barriers to engagement included poverty, a lack of access to transport or equipment".
- **The HNY cohort evaluation (2023) gives the effect sizes and the design limits [D]:** 224 valid 'before', 173 'after', 22% lost to follow-up; medium SMDs for life satisfaction 0.65, worthwhile 0.61, happiness 0.50, anxiety −0.39; HADS anxiety −2.63 and depression −2.54; best duration 5–12 weeks (for anxiety/depression "no benefit was found for GSP interventions that were a longer duration than 5–8 weeks"); "non-randomised and uncontrolled design, leading to potential selection bias and confounding"; participants "represent a self-selected group". Referral route: 31% self-referral, 8% GP.

### 2.3 The systematic-review layer: mostly weak, and the mechanism is participation not referral

| Source | Design | What it found |
|---|---|---|
| Bickerdike et al. 2017, *BMJ Open* (abstract) | Systematic review, 15 evaluations of UK link-worker schemes | "Most were small scale and limited by poor design and reporting. **All were rated as a having a high risk of bias**"; "Despite clear methodological shortcomings, most evaluations presented positive conclusions"; conclusion: "current evidence fails to provide sufficient detail to judge either success or value for money" |
| Huang et al. 2026, *Front Public Health* (abstract) | Bayesian re-analysis of RCTs, 10 reports, 4,840 participants | Social prescribing increased likelihood of meeting physical-activity recommendations (OR 1.31, 95% CrI 1.07–1.67); **no consistent dose–response**; "**Successful participation, rather than referral alone, appears to be the principal mechanism** underlying these benefits" |
| Lynch et al. 2026, *Front Public Health* (abstract) | Systematic review of health economics, 18 studies (5 RCTs) | "Robust economic evidence on social prescribing remains limited"; standard economic methods "rarely applied" |
| Pescheny et al. 2018, *BJGP Open* (full text) | Qualitative, 10 service users, 3 GPs, 2 navigators, Luton | Uptake promoted by trust in the GP, a navigator's phone call, free sessions; **barriers: "fear of stigma of psychosocial problems", patients' expectation of "an investigation, or a drug", and "the short-term nature of the programme"**. Also: free sessions ending can "hinder long-term adherence" |

**NICE.** What exists is NICE NG44, *Community engagement: improving health and wellbeing and reducing health inequalities* (2016), covering peer/lay roles and "making it as easy as possible for people to get involved" (overview page, full text). **No NICE guideline recommending nature-based social prescribing was retrieved [U].** Anyone quoting a "NICE position on green prescribing" should be asked for the reference.

**Verdict for section 2.** Established [D]: social prescribing reaches people, is liked, and shows medium pre-post improvements in self-reported wellbeing in uncontrolled designs; cost per participant is low relative to therapy. Contested [D]: whether the improvement is attributable to the prescription, the referral conversation, the activity, or to self-selection — and the 2026 re-analysis locates the effect in *participation*, which is precisely the step at which uptake studies show people drop out. Not established [U]: any Dutch controlled comparison of Welzijn op Recept; any NICE recommendation for green prescribing.

## 3. Community cooking and food literacy: what has been measured

| Source | Design | Measured | Result | Attrition / follow-up | Critique |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reicks, Kocher & Reeder 2018, *J Nutr Educ Behav* (abstract) | Systematic review, 34 articles, adults, 2011–2016 | Fruit/vegetable intake, weight, cooking confidence, knowledge | "The majority of studies showed positive dietary behavior changes and improvements in cooking confidence and knowledge" | "Limitations included the lack of a control group, **no follow-up past after intervention**, the use of nonvalidated assessment instruments, and small convenience samples"; "Less than half of the studies included a control group" | The review's own conclusion: "Intervention design and assessment tools need to be strengthened" |
| Hollywood et al. 2018, *Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr* (abstract) | Critical review of 59 cooking/food-skill interventions, coded with CALO-RE behaviour-change taxonomy | Design, BCTs, theory, outcomes | Three most frequent techniques: "Provide information on consequences", "Provide instruction on how to perform the behavior", "**Prompt Practice**"; "Fifty-six interventions reported positive short-term outcomes" | "Only 14 interventions reported long-term outcomes" | The mechanism actually used is instruction + prompting practice — i.e. supervised doing — but long-term and transfer measurement is rare |
| Flego et al. 2014, *PLoS ONE* — Jamie's Ministry of Food Australia (abstract) | Quasi-experimental, wait-list control, repeated measures | Cooking confidence, cooking from basic ingredients, vegetable/fruit/takeaway frequency | Significant group×time improvement in all cooking-confidence measures and in cooking from basic ingredients; **+0.52 servings vegetables/day**; "sustained at 6 months post program" | 694 completed baseline, 383 at T2, **214 at 6 months** (≈69% attrition from baseline) | The strongest design retrieved, and it still rests on self-report; high attrition |

**Does anything measure transfer to the home kitchen?** Only indirectly. Flego measures *frequency of cooking from basic ingredients* and *vegetables with the main meal* — behaviour in the home — which is the closest thing to transfer retrieved [D]. Reicks's finding of "no follow-up past after intervention" in most studies is the honest statement of the gap [D]. Hollywood's count — 14 of 59 with long-term outcomes — quantifies it [D].

**Dutch material.** The only Dutch community-food case with participants in the record that I retrieved is **Van Tuin Tot Bord** (Nijmegen): community kitchens where residents cook weekly "under the supervision of a chef", harvest supplied by the project's own gardens. Its evaluation reports "improved skills and knowledge of cooking and gardening" among participants' strongest experienced benefits, but rates self-reliance and "finding opportunities and hobbies" weakly (WUR thesis 2022, full text) [D]. **"Samen Koken Samen Eten" is not a national programme in the record** — only local event notices (Vaals, Itteren) surfaced and none was retrieved [U]. Buurtkeuken effect studies in Dutch were not found [U].

**Verdict for section 3.** Established [D]: cooking interventions reliably raise *cooking confidence* and self-reported vegetable intake in the short term (+0.5 servings/day in the best-controlled study), with high attrition. Not established [U]: that these gains persist past 6 months, that they transfer to everyday home cooking, or that they change food budgets. A "food-literacy" claim in Eindhoven would be borrowing confidence-and-intake evidence, not capability evidence.

## 4. Situated and experiential learning: does it produce transferable capability?

**The theory itself was not read.** I did not retrieve Lave & Wenger (1991); what follows is the *critique* literature, which restates the theory's claims in order to assess them. Treat any statement about Lave & Wenger's own text here as second-hand [U].

- **The canonical critique [D]:** Contu & Willmott (2003, *Organization Science*, abstract) argue that "Lave and Wenger's embryonic appreciation of power relations as media of learning is displaced by a managerial preoccupation with harnessing (reified) 'communities of practice' to the fulfillment of (reified) corporate objectives", and commend "a revitalization of situated learning theory in which learning practices are understood to be enabled and constrained by their embeddedness in relations of power". Read for a food forest: the theory's radical content is *who is allowed to move from the edge to the centre*, and that is the part usually dropped.
- **The empirical critique of peripheral participation [D]:** Fuller, Hodkinson, Hodkinson & Unwin (2005, *BERJ*, abstract) test "legitimate peripheral participation" against ESRC research in UK manufacturing and secondary schools and conclude it "has significant limitations", because "these complex settings play a crucial role in the configuration of opportunities and barriers to learning that employees encounter" — i.e. the movement from periphery to core is organised by the institution, not by the practice.
- **Health promotion has used communities of practice, and measured practice change but not outcomes [D]:** Barbour et al. (2018, *Journal of Knowledge Management*, full text) reviewed 12 studies of 10 CoPs in the public-health workforce: "CoPs support the prevention workforce to change their practice when they provide structured problem-solving, reflective practice and networking opportunities. **None of the studies described the impact of CoPs on public health outcomes.**" Quality: 7 of the quantitative studies rated "weak", 2 "moderate"; qualitative ratings ranged from 1/10 to 10/10. "Existing methods used to evaluate CoPs lack rigour; thus, the true impact of CoPs on population health remains unknown."
- **The mechanism appears in the cooking literature as "prompt practice" [D]** (Hollywood 2018): the interventions that report short-term success do so by providing instruction *and* structured practice. That is the "learning by doing alongside someone who knows" mechanism, and its measured yield is short-term confidence.
- **In the Dutch green case, the expert is a paid supervisor, not a peer [D]:** VTTB's gardens run "under the supervision of an expert in the field of gardening" and kitchens "under the supervision of a chef"; the thesis then rates participants' self-reliance gain at "+ (few examples)". So even in a sympathetic qualitative study, the "learning alongside someone who knows" produced confident participation more clearly than portable capability.
- **Failure modes documented or implied:** (a) participation changing practice without changing outcomes (Barbour) [D]; (b) movement to the core being institutionally gated (Fuller) [D]; (c) expertise and power concentrating in the reified "community" (Contu & Willmott) [D]; (d) **teaching as a burden** — GNW: "het aantal begeleiders heeft moeite dat bij te houden" [D], and the GSP evaluation's finding that "Link Workers' capacity is stretched, and many are seeing people with urgent or complex needs" [D]; (e) dependency on a single paid expert, visible in VTTB's design [D].

**Verdict for section 4.** Established [D]: situated learning is a serious theory; in health promotion its community-of-practice form changes *practitioner behaviour* and has never been shown to change population outcomes. Not established [U]: that participation in a garden or kitchen produces transferable capability. The literature that would show it does not exist in retrievable form, and the two studies that look (VTTB, Barbour) find confident participation and weak transfer.

## 5. Accessibility and exclusion: where this issue can go wrong

### 5.1 The overlap of limited literacy, limited health literacy and limited digital skill

From Stichting Lezen en Schrijven, *Taal maakt gezonder* (2023 factsheet, full text) [D]:

- "18% van de Nederlandse bevolking van 16 jaar en ouder heeft moeite met lezen, schrijven en/of rekenen. Dat zijn 2,5 miljoen mensen. Vaak hebben ze ook problemen met het gebruik van de computer."
- "25% van de Nederlandse bevolking van 18 jaar en ouder heeft moeite met het verwerken van informatie over hun gezondheid … Zij zijn beperkt gezondheidsvaardig."
- "**Alle laaggeletterden hebben beperkte gezondheidsvaardigheden.**"
- "Ongeveer 55% van de laaggeletterden heeft geen migratie-achtergrond" — so language is not the main driver, and an *anderstaligen*-only framing will miss most of the group.
- "Bijna 40% van de mensen met alleen een diploma van het vmbo of mbo 1 is laaggeletterd."
- Consequence claim: "Laaggeletterdheid kost de Nederlandse samenleving ruim 1,13 miljard euro per jaar. 257 miljoen daarvan zijn kosten van extra gezondheidszorg."

Caveat [I]: the factsheet's own citations are on a separate web page I did not retrieve, so the primary studies behind 18% / 25% / €1.13bn are unverified here. The figures are internally consistent with the known PIAAC/health-literacy literature but should be re-sourced before quotation in a brief.

The design implication the factsheet itself draws is *not* "teach literacy" but "communiceer duidelijk" (max 3 messages, short sentences, concrete), "herken laaggeletterdheid", and refer to a Taalhuis [D].

### 5.2 Who does not visit, and who does not join

- **England, MENE 2009–2012 (Natural England DATA 005, full text) [D]:** 38% of the Black, Asian and minority ethnic adult population are frequent nature visitors vs 57% of the rest; 26% are rare visitors (<3 visits/year) vs 15%. Frequent-visitor shares: BAME 38%, urban deprived 43%, DE socio-economic 45%, 65+ 46%, disability/long-term illness 42%, against 54% for the adult population. "The demographic factors that account for most of the variation … are, in descending order: whether or not someone has a disability; whether people are members of the BAME population; socio-economic status; age." **Note the period: 2009–2012.** [I] These are the best-retrieved figures on differential access and they are more than a decade old; a current claim needs a current source.
- **Green social prescribing reached further than expected [D]:** 57% from the 30% most deprived areas and 21% from ethnic minorities, "largely achieved through specific targeted activities, roles, and collaboration with local community groups" (GSP Final Report Summary).
- **But one cohort evaluation did not [D]:** 92% of HNY GSP participants were "British" by ethnicity, 65% from the most deprived quintile, 80% limited in activities of daily living, 74% referred for a mental health reason (HNY report).
- **Practical barriers named by the evaluators [D]:** "poverty, a lack of access to transport or equipment"; and cost is decisive in Pescheny: patients dropped out when free sessions ended — "What is the point then if it's not free anymore?"
- **Disability is designed for in the best Dutch case [D]:** VTTB has a garden "for people that cannot bend down, such as elderly" with raised structures — a small, concrete instance of access by design rather than by invitation.
- **Digital exclusion is the live risk for any "online community" or registration route [D/I]:** Lezen en Schrijven states low-literate people "vaak ook problemen" with computer use; NFH's own community-building moved to LinkedIn [D]. [I] A LinkedIn-based community and a paper form reach different populations.

### 5.3 The design problem: a community that people must qualify for

The evidence retrieved points at three distinct mechanisms, and they are worth keeping apart:

1. **Qualification by diagnosis.** Welzijn op Recept requires a GP referral for psychosocial complaints. The RIVM/TNO action research found that where clients have multi-problem lives, the intervention "vaak beter" suited a different, broader offer — and one site concluded "veel cliënten weten nog niet van het bestaan van Welzijn op Recept af" [D]. A referral gate both stigmatises and under-recruits.
2. **Stigma in the conversation.** UK qualitative evidence: patients decline because "they are afraid of being stigmatised. They feel that in some way they have not created the social environment that maybe is best for their wellbeing"; and because "Patients expect to be referred to an investigation, or a drug, not to SP!" (Pescheny 2018) [D]. Note that this stigma attaches to *the reason for referral* (psychosocial problems), not to the garden.
3. **Withdrawal of the offer when the programme ends.** Free, time-limited sessions create uptake and then a cliff: "they are not interested anymore" once the free sessions stop (Pescheny) [D]. [I] A genuinely low-threshold offer is therefore one without an end date as well as without a referral.

**What makes an offer genuinely low-threshold, on the retrieved evidence** — assembled, not quoted from any single source: free at the point of use [D, Pescheny: "I thought: 'It is free, I try it.'"]; drop-in and weekly so a missed week is not an exit [D, GNW and VTTB both run weekly, no registration described]; self-referral accepted [D, 31% of HNY participants self-referred]; no measurement burden on participants [D/I — note that both GNW and GSP *do* collect questionnaires, and GSP's evaluators record that "data collection by VCSE providers requires training and resourcing" and "The complexity of GSP poses multiple data collection challenges"]; and a person who makes the first contact [D, Pescheny: the navigator's phone call and being met at reception were decisive for adherence].

**Not established [U]:** any Dutch study of stigma in "Positieve Gezondheid" conversations. I attempted one (a Dutch academic article on critical questions about Positive Health) and hit an access wall (see access log). The critique exists in the Dutch literature; I did not retrieve it, and I make no claim about it.

## 6. Reciprocal learning: does the institution ever learn from the community?

This is the thinnest evidence base in the file, and that is itself the finding.

- **The one strong documented case is community-based participatory research, not green health programmes [D].** Jagosh et al. (2015, *BMC Public Health*, full text, realist evaluation of 11 partnerships, 24 interviewees): trust built over time produces "partnership synergy" and a "ripple effect" with outcomes including "achieving systemic transformations". Crucially, learning runs both ways: community partners "became experts in applying research methods and fundraising", "community organizations were transformed from service delivery entities to community-based research resources, thus transferring research expertise from universities to communities"; and "academic members used their university positions to spread their gained expertise back to the university". It also documents the *conditions*: partnerships oriented to "shared decision-making and co-governance"; "healthy mistrust" and "earned scepticism" as an asset; and the risk that "a naïve sense of trust and enthusiasm … increas[es] their risk of being exploited".
- **Caveat on that case [D/I]:** 11 of 23 reviewed partnerships were interviewed, three were "no longer active", seven did not respond, two declined — i.e. the sample is surviving, willing partnerships reporting retrospectively. That is a success-biased sample, and the authors say so.
- **In the Dutch green programmes I retrieved, learning is claimed in one direction only.** Natuur op Recept asks GPs to change their advice; Groen doet Goed asks municipalities to buy an approach; GNW asks volunteers to guide; the zorgboerderij asks clients to work toward personal goals; the research relation is programme → evaluation. **I retrieved no Dutch green-health programme document that describes the programme changing because of what participants taught it.** Where participant knowledge appears, it is as an *outcome* ("improved skills and knowledge") not as an input [D for VTTB; I for the pattern].
- **Two partial exceptions worth noting.** (a) VTTB is described as "a project that is organized for and by local residents", with residents in the gardens and kitchens and guests eating in the restaurants — governance by residents is claimed but not documented in the thesis [C]. (b) GSP's evaluation records that "co-design activities to refine referral pathways … proved more time consuming than expected" and that in some cases this "limited meaningful engagement of people with relevant lived experience in decision making and planning" — an honest account of reciprocity attempted and truncated [D].

**Verdict for section 6.** Established [D]: reciprocity is documented and theorised in participatory *research*, with trust and co-governance as mechanisms. Not established [U]: any case of a Dutch green-health programme or a health institution demonstrably changing its practice because of what a community practice taught it. If the issue claims "reciprocal learning", the claim is currently unsupported for the programme class it belongs to.

## 11. What would change the picture

- **A Dutch controlled evaluation of Welzijn op Recept** (the Groningen centre in S7 says it joined a ZonMW effect study that "loopt nog"). If that study reports a null on wellbeing or care use, section 2's cautious reading hardens into a negative one.
- **Any programme publishing participant-level outcome data with a comparison.** A single such document would move a whole row of the proposition table from [C] to [D].
- **A transfer study**: cooking or gardening intervention with 12-month follow-up measuring behaviour at home and, ideally, a capability measure rather than confidence. This is the specific study the issue's "self-directed behaviour change" claim needs and does not have.
- **A Dutch health-literacy field experiment** testing whether an open, unregistered, unmeasured garden/kitchen attracts people with limited health literacy — plus a count of who comes and who does not.
- **Evidence from the GSP programme's own follow-up publications.** The evaluation names an "NIHR research" strand; if that reports effects separately from participation, P3 changes.
- **A retrieved copy of the Positive Health critique** (S25's counterpart). If Dutch practitioners document that "het andere gesprek" is experienced as moralising by low-income or low-literacy patients, that would materially strengthen P8.
