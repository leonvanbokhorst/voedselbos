## 1. What has actually been measured

Six families of study exist, and they are not interchangeable: (a) gardening meta-analyses that pool everything; (b) community/allotment-garden systematic reviews; (c) horticultural-therapy reviews in clinical populations; (d) nature-based-intervention (NBI) reviews with RCT pools; (e) school-garden cluster RCTs with child dietary outcomes; (f) programme evaluations of green social prescribing. Full detail in the study table (§7).

**1.1 Gardening in general gives a small-to-moderate pooled effect, driven largely by weak designs.** Soga et al.'s meta-analysis pooled 22 case studies / 76 comparisons and found Hedges' *d* = 0.42 (95% CI 0.36–0.48) — *d* = 0.47 for wellbeing variables vs 0.31 for "health" variables [D, S1]. The instructive part is the subgroup split, which the authors report and which I read directly: **before/after comparisons *d* = 0.60 vs gardener/non-gardener comparisons *d* = 0.32; horticultural-therapy studies *d* = 0.61 vs non-therapy *d* = 0.31; patients *d* = 0.61 vs non-patients *d* = 0.32**. The largest effects sit in the designs with the least protection against regression to the mean, expectancy and enthusiasm [D, S1; interpretation I]. The authors nonetheless conclude robustly, and they detected publication bias ("Egger's test indicated the presence of publication bias… significant positive effects of gardening remained after adjusting for this using trim and fill analysis"; 16 comparisons imputed) [D, S1].

**1.2 For community gardens specifically, the honest reviews say the quality is low.** Hume et al. (53 studies; nutrition/food security k=23, health k=16, psychosocial k=16, community k=7): "Effects appeared positive for fruit and vegetable intake, some psychosocial and community outcomes, but mixed for physical health outcomes. **Evidence quality overall was low**"; and, in their own words, "poor evidence quality suggests the effects of community gardening may be **overestimated**" [D, S4 — exact wording]. Tharrey & Darmon (15 studies, high-income countries): F&V higher in gardeners; "Mixed results were found for physical activity and physiological health"; and "the vast majority of included studies were cross-sectional and presented selection bias (n = 13 of 15 for both) and very few used objective measurement methods (n = 3 of 15)" [D, S5]. Their conclusion is that longitudinal work is needed "before the health benefits … suggested by existing studies can be confirmed" [D, S5].

**1.3 Psychosocial outcomes rest on very few quantitative studies.** Spano et al.'s meta-analysis of community gardening / horticultural intervention on social cohesion, support and trust drew on **7 studies, 22 effect sizes, 11 comparisons**, found a positive moderate pooled effect, and reported "an effect of publication bias and study heterogeneity has been detected" plus "quantitative studies are lacking" [D, S6]. A seven-study meta-analysis cannot carry an institutional claim [I].

**1.4 Physiological and clinical outcomes are thinner than the popular literature implies.** BMI: Zick et al. compared 198 community gardeners to neighbours and to siblings — BMI −1.84 (women) and −2.36 (men) vs neighbours; −1.88 vs sisters, −1.33 vs brothers [D, S8]. This is a *comparative cross-sectional* design with a genuinely clever control (siblings share upbringing), but it cannot order cause and effect [I]. Cortisol: van den Berg & Custers randomised 30 allotment gardeners to 30 minutes of gardening vs indoor reading after a stressor and found cortisol fell more in the gardening arm and positive mood was restored [D, S10] — a **same-session** result, n=30, in people who already garden [D/I]. Blood pressure and mortality: **no gardening-specific trial was retrieved**; the only mortality signal in my set is for greenspace *exposure* in general, from an umbrella review that also reports "Ambivalent results … for cardiovascular and metabolic health" and that "most of the systematic reviews … had a low to moderate methodological quality and a high risk of bias" [D, S20]. Food forests: **no study of a food forest as an intervention was retrieved** [U].

**1.5 Clinical horticultural therapy splits into "promising but very low certainty" and "insufficient".** Giang et al. pooled 27 studies (11 RCTs, 1,629 adults ≥60 without dementia): self-efficacy *g* = 0.52 (95% CI 0.26–0.79), self-esteem *g* = 0.52 (0.26–0.79), aerobic endurance *g* = 0.84 (0.54–1.15) — with the authors' own verdict: "the certainty of this evidence is **very low** due to the quasi-experimental design and potential bias in outcome measurements" [D, S12]. Kamioka et al.'s all-RCT review found only **4 eligible RCTs** and concluded: "Although there was **insufficient evidence** in the studies of HT due to poor methodological and reporting quality and heterogeneity, HT may be an effective treatment…" [D, S11]. In cancer survivors, Obembe et al. (8 studies, 634 participants; 3 in the meta-analysis) found pooled effects on physical health SMD −0.38 (−0.62, −0.13), physical performance 0.19 (0.01, 0.37) and lifestyle behaviours 0.67 (0.27, 1.06), but "**No significant effect was found for psychosocial health outcomes**" [D, S14]. Direction of travel across reviews is consistent; certainty is not [I].

**1.6 Diet and physical activity: the best-designed studies are the ones that find least.** Christian et al.'s cluster RCT (23 schools, 641 children, 18-month follow-up, 24-hour food diaries, RHS-led vs teacher-led gardening) is the cleanest test in my set: adjusted intervention effect −40 g/day fruit and vegetable (95% CI −88 to 1; p = 0.06); the authors write "The results have found **very little evidence** to support the claims that school gardening alone can improve children's daily fruit and vegetable intake" [D, S16]. Their one positive signal is a dose-response in *implementation*, not allocation: schools that moved up three levels on a six-level gardening-involvement scale averaged +81 g/day (95% CI 0–163; p = 0.05) [D, S16]. Hutchinson et al. (21 London schools, 7–10-year-olds, 18 months) found "**limited evidence** that gardening activity packages led by external specialists (RHS-led) provide additional benefits over those led by teachers trained by the RHS. Indeed, the latter were potentially more effective" [D, S17]. Rochira et al.'s meta-analysis of 33 school-gardening studies did find BMI percentile −1.37% and waist circumference −1.30 cm, described by the authors as "a **modest** but positive impact" [D, S18]. Ohly et al.'s mixed-methods review (40 articles) is blunt: "**Generally the quantitative research was poor. Evidence for changes in fruit and vegetable intake was limited and based on self-report**" [D, S15].

**1.7 The largest real-world programme is pre/post only.** The England-wide Green Social Prescribing evaluation reached **8,339 people** and reports ONS4 improvements — happiness 5.3→7.5, life satisfaction 4.7→6.8, "worthwhile" 5.1→6.8, anxiety 4.8→3.4 — plus one pilot's HADS depression 8.1→5.6 and anxiety 11.1→8.5, and an estimated SROI of £2.42 per £1 [D, S22]. Its own caveat is the one that matters here: "Because the data was collected in routine settings and not for everyone who accessed GSP, it is **unknown how representative the data is**" [D, S22]. There is no comparator arm [D].

**The specific claim the PI asked me to test — "a well-known finding is a pre/post single-arm result with 20 participants".** In my retrieved set this pattern is real but not a single culprit: the *pooled* estimate is inflated by exactly such studies (Soga's pre/post subgroup *d* = 0.60 vs 0.32 for between-group comparisons [D, S1]), and the most-cited physiological and clinical results are single-session or single-arm (n=30, 30 min [S10]; the Norwegian depression work appears in Soga's table as n=18–46 pre/post horticultural therapy with no randomised comparator [D, S1]). I did **not** retrieve and read Gonzalez et al.'s depression papers directly, so I state the design only as Soga characterises it [D via S1] and flag it as second-hand [I].

**What this means in one line.** The literature reliably shows that people who garden, and people in gardening programmes, report better mood, lower distress and more social contact than comparison groups. It does not reliably show that gardening *causes* those differences in free-living adults, and where the design is strongest the dietary effect shrinks toward zero [D, S4/S5/S7/S16; synthesis I].

## 3. Randomised and controlled evidence: does anything survive a comparator?

| Design strength | Study | Survives a comparator? |
|---|---|---|
| Cluster RCT, objective-ish dietary outcome, 18 months, n=641 | Christian et al. 2014 [S16] | **No** for allocation (p = 0.06, CI spans zero, point estimate negative); **partial** for implementation dose (+81 g/day, CI touching 0) |
| Cluster RCT, knowledge/attitudes, 18 months, n=777 | Hutchinson et al. 2015 [S17] | **Barely**: one item favoured RHS-led, two favoured teacher-led; no association between knowledge change and intake change |
| RCT pool, mental health, 16 RCTs / 50 studies | Coventry et al. 2021 [S3] | **Yes**, with caveats: depression −0.64 (−1.05, −0.23), anxiety −0.94 (printed CI "0.94 to −0.01" — see note), positive affect 0.95 (0.59, 1.31), negative affect −0.52 (−0.77, −0.26); "There was **less evidence** that NBIs improved physical health" [D] |
| Randomised crossover, physiology, n=30 | van den Berg & Custers 2011 [S10] | **Yes** for acute stress recovery in an existing-gardener sample; nothing about lasting change |
| All-RCT review, clinical | Kamioka et al. 2014 [S11] | **Not established** — 4 RCTs, "insufficient evidence" |
| RCT + quasi-experimental pool, older adults | Giang et al. 2024 [S12] | **Yes statistically, no on certainty** — "very low" |
| RCT-only review, 47 trials | Heinen-Stach et al. 2026 [S19] | **Mixed** — 17 of 47 trials showed significantly greater improvement; the authors call for long-term studies |

*Note on the Coventry anxiety confidence interval.* The retrieved abstract prints "reducing anxiety -0.94 (95% CI: 0.94 to -0.01)", which is internally inconsistent (an upper limit of 0.94 with a lower limit of −0.01 around a point estimate of −0.94). I quote it as printed rather than repair it; the direction is negative and the interval excludes zero only if the first bound is −0.94. **Anyone citing this number should read the table in the paper, not the abstract** [D as printed, flag I].

**Verdict on §3.** Effects on *self-reported mental state* do survive randomisation in pooled analyses, at small-to-moderate size, in people who are recruited into structured programmes, measured at the end of the programme. Effects on *diet, weight and physical activity* do not survive the best comparators, or survive only as small anthropometric changes in children or as dose-response in implementation quality [D, synthesis I].

## 4. The transfer and persistence question (the pivot for #25)

This is where the retrieved literature is thinnest, and the thinness is a finding, not a gap in my search alone.

**4.1 The one study designed to test transfer found none.** JArDinS (Montpellier) followed **66 people entering a community garden in 2018 against 66 pairwise-matched non-gardeners for 12 months**, measuring nutritional quality, environmental impact and cost of household food supplies, physical activity by **accelerometer**, mental and social wellbeing, food-waste sensitivity and connection to nature. Result: "Participating in the community garden had **no significant impact, in spite of sufficient statistical power**, on fruit and vegetables supplies (main outcome), nor on physical activity parameters, nor on others of the social/health, environmental and economic lifestyles components investigated" [D, S7]. This is the closest thing in the literature to a direct test of "does the garden change life outside the garden", and the answer at 12 months is no [D].

**4.2 Where transfer was claimed, it was measured inside the programme or by self-report.** School gardening: Christian's null [S16]; Ohly's "limited and based on self-report" [S15]; Holloway's realist synthesis of school gardening and food literacy is in the literature but I retrieved only its metadata, so I make **no claim** from it [U]. Programmes' wellbeing gains in the GSP evaluation are measured around participation, with the evaluators stating representativeness is unknown [D, S22].

**4.3 No retrieved study measures outcomes *after* participation ends.** Across the reviews I read, follow-up is consistently "at the end of the intervention" (Coventry: 8–12 weeks optimal dose [S3]; GSP: during/after activity [S22]; school RCTs: 18 months *with the garden still running* [S16/S17]). I found **no** retrieved study reporting a measurement taken after a garden programme closed, after a plot was given up, or after a site was lost, in adults [U]. The nearest thing is Wardle et al.'s qualitative finding that COVID closures had "disproportionate impacts … on vulnerable groups" [D, S23] — which is evidence of *dependence* on the site, and therefore of context-bound rather than portable benefit [I].

**4.4 Barriers reported by participants are about the practice itself, not about transfer.** JArDinS's interviews list "lack of time, lack of gardening knowledge, physical difficulty of gardening, health problems and conflicts with other gardeners" [D, S7]. None of these is a health-literacy barrier; they are participation barriers. That distinction matters for the issue's framing [I].

**4.5 Retention/lapse evidence was not found.** I searched for allotment turnover, plot surrender and lapsed gardeners and retrieved nothing usable from the bibliographic databases (see access log, row 3). The literature I did retrieve treats participation as a fixed attribute of a sample ("gardeners") rather than as a state people enter and leave [D across S5/S7/S9; I]. **So: is there retrieved evidence that a capability acquired in a garden carries into everyday life and persists after the site closes? No. There is one well-powered 12-month test that found nothing, and no study at all of the after-the-end period** [U, with coverage stated in §10].

## 5. Null, negative and contested findings

This section is deliberately longer than the positive one.

**5.1 Reviews that conclude the evidence is insufficient.**
- Kamioka et al.: "insufficient evidence … due to poor methodological and reporting quality and heterogeneity" [D, S11].
- Hume et al.: evidence quality low; effects "may be overestimated" [D, S4].
- Tharrey & Darmon: benefits "suggested by existing studies" cannot be confirmed without longitudinal work [D, S5].
- Ohly et al.: "Generally the quantitative research was poor" [D, S15].
- Bryer et al. (36 systematic reviews): most had "low to moderate methodological quality and a high risk of bias"; cardiometabolic outcomes "ambivalent" [D, S20].
- The GSP evaluation itself records "a persistent perception at local and national level that … evidence for GSP is **not sufficiently compelling or rigorous**" [D, S22].

**5.2 Studies with no significant effect.**
- JArDinS: no effect on the primary outcome or on physical activity at 12 months [D, S7].
- Christian et al.: adjusted intervention effect on child F&V intake −40 g/day, p = 0.06 [D, S16].
- Obembe et al.: "No significant effect was found for psychosocial health outcomes" in cancer survivors [D, S14].
- Hutchinson et al.: no advantage of specialist-led over teacher-led gardening [D, S17].
- van den Berg et al.: **younger** allotment gardeners (<62) "did not differ from younger neighbors" on any health or wellbeing measure [D, S9] — i.e. the Dutch allotment effect is an old-age effect, not a gardening effect per se [I].

**5.3 Negative effects.**
- **Coordinator burden and burnout**: the greatest time and wellbeing costs fell on volunteer coordinators, with "fatigue and feelings of over-commitment" [D, S23]. Over-reliance on grant funding and on volunteers is named as a threat to success in the school-garden review too [D, S15].
- **Displacement and exclusion**: in gentrifying neighbourhoods, "longtime, marginalized residents are negatively impacted by green gentrification as they experience a lower sense of community, feel that they do not belong in green space, and, in many studies, use green space less often than newcomers" [D, S21]. Note this is about urban greening broadly, **not** about community gardens specifically — the scoping review found only 15 studies and calls the research "limited" [D, S21].
- **Conflict between gardeners** is a reported barrier to participation [D, S7].
- **Closure harms the vulnerable most** [D, S23].
- **"Garden guilt"/harvest anxiety**: **not established.** I did not retrieve a study measuring anxiety about harvest failure or guilt about neglected plots. I looked for it and did not find it indexed in the places I searched; treat any such claim as unverified [U].

**5.4 Failures to replicate / contested constructs.** The pattern across S1 → S4/S5/S7 is effectively a replication failure at the level of *design*: the effect size halves (0.60 → 0.32) when pre/post designs are replaced by between-group comparisons [D, S1], and disappears for the primary dietary outcome when a matched prospective cohort replaces a cross-sectional one [D, S7]. That is the single most important quantitative fact in this file [I from D].

## 6. Who benefits, and who is absent

**6.1 Who is in the samples.** Soga's 22 studies: mean age 52.3 (range 8.5–84.7), 67.8% female (range 29.2–100%) [D, S1]. The Dutch allotment sample splits at 62 and the benefit is confined to the older half [D, S9]. JArDinS gardeners had **lower education** and lower BMI than their matched non-gardening neighbours, and ate fewer meals outside the home [D, S7] — a useful corrective to the assumption that gardeners are the already-advantaged, but also a reminder that the comparison group is not a random one [I].

**6.2 Who programmes can reach when they try.** The GSP evaluation reports 57% of participants from the most socio-economically deprived areas (IMD deciles 1–3) and 21% from ethnic minority populations, and states it "was able to reach a broader range of people compared to many other social prescribing initiatives" [D, S22]. This is the strongest retrieved counter-evidence to "green health is only for the middle class" — but it describes a *referred* population in a funded national programme, not a volunteer-led food forest [I].

**6.3 Who is absent from the evidence.** Children under 5 (no retrieved study), older adults with frailty outside institutional settings, non-Western populations (Bryer-type reviews note the geographic skew; I did not retrieve that specific bias statement for gardening reviews, so this is [U] for this literature specifically), and **non-participants**. I retrieved no study of people who were offered a garden and declined [U]. The nearest is JArDinS's *dropout* and barrier data, which describes people who had already joined [D, S7].

**6.4 Displacement.** The gentrification evidence is about green space and housing, not about who gets a plot [D, S21]. Whether a community garden raises or lowers the position of poor residents in a Dutch neighbourhood is **not established in anything I retrieved** [U].

## 11. Not established, and what coverage I can claim

**What I searched.** Europe PMC (REST API: `core` and `lite` records, `SRC:MED`), directly by PMID/DOI for ~24 studies; title- and abstract-field queries on *allotment garden*, *community garden(ing)*, *school garden(ing)*, *garden-based*, *horticultural therapy*, *nature-based intervention*, *green gentrification*, *community gardens + governance*; one web search for the UK green social prescribing evaluation and one for allotment attrition; one PDF retrieved from an institutional repository.

**What I did not search.** Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, GreenFILE, CNKI; no grey-literature databases beyond the two web searches; **no Dutch-language sources at all** (no TNO, RIVM, Wageningen or Trimbos grey literature); no forward/backward citation chasing from any review; no PROSPERO search for registered-but-unpublished trials; nothing published after the retrieval date, and nothing in languages other than English.

**Therefore, honestly:**
- **Established on retrieved evidence.** Gardening programmes produce small-to-moderate improvements in self-reported mental wellbeing and distress, including in RCT pools [D, S1/S3/S12/S19]. Community-garden participation is associated with higher F&V intake in cross-sectional studies [D, S4/S5]. Coordinator burden is a real and measured cost [D, S23]. Evidence quality is low and the effect is probably overestimated [D, S4/S5/S20].
- **Contested.** Whether gardening *causes* dietary or physical-activity change in free-living adults: contradicted by the only well-powered prospective test [D, S7] and by the strongest school RCT [D, S16], supported by cross-sectional associations [D, S4/S5] and small anthropometric meta-analytic effects in children [D, S18].
- **Not established, sources searched.** Any transfer of capability to everyday life after participation [U]; any persistence after a site closes [U]; any effect of a food forest as such [U]; any measured "garden guilt"/harvest anxiety [U]; any study of people who declined a plot [U]; Dutch-language evidence of any kind [U].
- **Not knowable with these methods.** Whether an Eindhoven food forest would reproduce the JArDinS null or the GSP gains — both are context-dependent programme effects, and no retrieved study varies the *organisational form* (volunteer commons vs referred programme) as the exposure [I].

**The single most important limitation.** I read **no full text of any empirical study in this file except Soga (partially) and the GSP summary (partially)**. Every effect size and every quotation above comes from an abstract or from the retrieved portion of a document. Abstracts are written to summarise favourably; the numbers I quote are the authors' own, not independently checked against their tables. This is a real ceiling on the confidence of §1–§6 and should be stated in any downstream use.

## 12. What would change the picture

1. **A plot-allocation natural experiment with objective outcomes.** Randomised or waitlist-based allocation of allotment/community-garden plots, ≥24 months, with objective dietary biomarkers (serum carotenoids), accelerometry and a mental-health scale, in a Dutch or comparable setting. Nothing retrieved resembles this; JArDinS is the closest and it is an observational entry cohort [S7].
2. **A follow-up study of lapsed gardeners and closed sites.** Participants measured at exit and 12–24 months later. **This is the study the issue actually needs**, and I found no version of it. Wardle et al.'s COVID-closure finding is the only adjacent evidence [S23].
3. **An organisational-form comparison.** Volunteer-led commons vs referred, staffed programmes, same outcomes, same period — because the retrieved difference between JArDinS (null) and GSP (large pre/post gains) is confounded by referral, staffing, dose and population [S7 vs S22; I].
4. **A Dutch replication of the ≥62 allotment finding** with an allocation design, testing whether the age-moderated effect [S9] is ageing or selection.
5. **A trial of a garden intervention with a household-level behavioural outcome** (food purchasing, cooking frequency, hydration, help-seeking) rather than garden-level produce — the outcome that would let anyone speak about transfer.
6. **Any controlled evaluation of a food forest.** If one exists, it would change my [U] on P13.

## 13. Implications for the main question (as I read them — inference, not finding)

- The evidence supports a claim about **participation and its immediate correlates**: people in garden programmes tend to report better mood and more social contact while they are in them [D]. It does **not** support a claim that a food forest would raise health literacy, change diet, or produce self-directed behaviour change that outlasts the site [U].
- The strongest retrieved signal for a *public-health* framing is not gardening at all but **structured, staffed, referred programmes with defined dose (8–12 weeks, 20–90 min) and ONS4/HADS measurement** [D, S3, S22] — a different organisational animal from a volunteer food forest, and one that carries the coordinator-burden cost documented in S23 [I].
- JArDinS is the most useful study for this issue precisely because it is the one that tried to detect transfer and failed, with adequate power and accelerometers, in a high-income country [D, S7]. Any Eindhoven claim should be written against it.
- If the Lead needs one sentence for the note: *the literature can show that gardeners feel better while gardening; it cannot show that gardening makes people better at health, and the best-designed study that looked for the second thing found nothing.*
