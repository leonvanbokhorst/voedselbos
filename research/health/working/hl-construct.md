## 1. The construct: from functional skills to system responsiveness

### 1.1 The definition actually in use in the Dutch measurement

The RIVM's own operational definition, retrieved in full [D]:

> "Gezondheidsvaardigheden verwijzen naar het geheel aan cognitieve, sociale en praktische vaardigheden die mensen nodig hebben om informatie over gezondheid te verkrijgen, te begrijpen, te beoordelen en toe te passen bij het nemen van beslissingen over hun gezondheid. […] Gezondheidsvaardigheden vormen een dynamisch samenspel tussen individuele capaciteiten en de complexiteit van het zorg- en informatiesysteem." — **S4**, p. 11.

That last clause matters more than it looks: it is the *relational* definition (skill × demand), not a trait definition, and it is the definition the national monitor uses. The Nivel report states the same thing in operational language [D]:

> "Ontoereikende of problematische gezondheidsvaardigheden zijn het gevolg van een mismatch tussen persoonlijke vaardigheden en de complexiteit van de omgeving waarin iemand leeft, zorg ontvangt of werkt." — **S6**, sheet 6, citing Parker (2009).

### 1.2 Nutbeam's three levels, and what they are for

Nutbeam's functional / interactive (communicative) / critical hierarchy is the typology the field still organises around, and I retrieved it **as used**, not from the 2000 paper itself (which was Cloudflare-blocked — see access log). Retrieved attestations:

- Chinn (2011) explicitly "takes as a starting point the expanded model of health literacy advanced by Nutbeam (2000)" and identifies critical health literacy's constituent domains as **"information appraisal, understanding the social determinants of health, and collective action"** [D] — **S9**, abstract only.
- A Dutch empirical study using the Functional, Communicative and Critical Health Literacy measure (**S18**, abstract only) shows the three levels do **different** work: "Mainly interactive health literacy was associated with patients' perceived ability to organize care, interact with healthcare providers and perform self-care, whereas only functional health literacy was associated with number of GP visits" (N=2,508, National Panel of People with Chronic Illness or Disability) [D].
- The hierarchy is still being applied as an analytic coding frame, e.g. to adolescents' workshop responses in Vanuatu, where functional and interactive responses dominated and critical responses were rare (5–12%) (**S34**, abstract only) [D].

**What the distinction buys.** It separates *decoding* (functional: can you read and follow the leaflet) from *negotiating* (interactive: can you ask, discuss, act with others) from *contesting* (critical: can you judge the message and its conditions, and act collectively). Analytically that is the difference between a leaflet intervention, a group programme, and a governance demand. **What it does not buy:** the levels are not cleanly separable empirically. Chinn & McCarthy's own AAHLS validation found subscale reliabilities "less consistent" and a four-factor rather than three-factor solution (**S33**, abstract only) [D]. And Nivel's 2025 measurement found that, at Q12 level, the headline rate "verschilt niet of nauwelijks naar type vaardigheid" [D] — **S6**, sheet 15 — i.e. the skill-type story is weakly supported by the short instrument. Treat the three levels as a *design heuristic*, not a measured partition.

### 1.3 "Risk factor" versus "asset"

Nutbeam (2008) describes the concept as emerging "from two different roots — in clinical care and in public health", respectively health literacy **as a clinical "risk"** and **as a personal "asset"**; the risk framing supports screening and clinical reorganisation, the asset framing draws on adult-learning and health promotion and is "less well developed", focused on "skills and capacities intended to enable people to exert greater control over their health and the factors that shape health" [D] — **S8**, abstract only.

This distinction is the fork in the road for issue #25. A *risk* framing makes a food forest a place to screen and refer. An *asset* framing makes it a place where capability is produced. The two imply different outcome measures and different funders.

### 1.4 The move to organisational responsiveness

The strongest post-Nutbeam development is the shift of the unit of analysis from person to organisation. Trezona, Dodson & Osborne's Org-HLR framework defines the characteristics of health-literacy-responsive organisations, on the premise that "the health literacy skills required by individuals to interact effectively with health services depends on the complexity of those services, and the demands they place on people" — developed by concept mapping with six groups of professionals (N=42) plus an online consultation with 153 professionals (**S10**, abstract only) [D]. It was then built into a self-assessment tool (**S11**/**Org-HLR**, field-tested in four Victorian organisations, 43 participants) [D].

Two retrieved findings discipline how far this can be pushed:

- **The outcome-measurement base is thin.** Aaby et al. (2026) report that a literature search for organisational health literacy responsiveness outcomes "identified 9 publications, yielding a total of 383 outcomes", reduced by a 24-expert Delphi to 15 core domains — and state plainly that "research on outcome measures for organizational health literacy responsiveness interventions is scarce, making it difficult to synthesize findings from evaluations and compare effects" (**S12**, abstract only) [D].
- **The tool landscape exists but is unvalidated in most settings.** Chu et al. (2024) found 16 self-assessment tools (2007–2023, eight countries), 661 items, 647 retained, 210 kept for future instrument development — i.e. a *pool of items*, not a validated instrument (**S13**, abstract only) [D].

### 1.5 The Dutch practice: *gezondheidsvaardige organisatie*

Pharos runs the Dutch operationalisation as **ten characteristics** of a *gezondheidsvaardige organisatie* — awareness, policy, understandable information, understandable digital care, easy to find, understandable conversations, extra support, inclusive research, reaching everyone, and experiential knowledge central (**S3**, full text) [D]. The Alliantie Gezondheidsvaardigheden is "een landelijk netwerk van ruim 145 partners" coordinated by Pharos (**S2**, full text) [D].

Crucially for the framing question, Pharos states the demand-side position as institutional doctrine, not as a critique from outside [D]:

> "Dat zoveel mensen beperkte gezondheidsvaardigheden hebben ligt niet aan hen. Veel informatie is ingewikkeld, zowel mondeling als schriftelijk." — **S2**.

> "Gezondheidsvaardigheden worden niet alleen bepaald door wat iemand zelf kan, maar ook door de eisen die de omgeving stelt." — **S1**.

**Consequence for the issue.** The Dutch field has already absorbed the anti-deficit argument at the level of rhetoric. What it has *not* done is demonstrate that organisational responsiveness interventions change outcomes (S12). So a claim that a green practice "strengthens health literacy" is making an **asset-side** claim in a field whose evidence base is currently strongest on the **demand side** — and weakest on measuring either.

## 2. The numbers: 44.3% and 52.8%, and what sits behind them

### 2.1 What Pharos actually says (and the small errors in the brief)

The Pharos infosheet, **October 2026** (**S1**, full text) [D]:

> "In Nederland heeft ongeveer de helft van de Nederlanders (44-52,8%) moeite met het vinden, begrijpen, beoordelen en/of gebruiken van gezondheidsinformatie. 1, 2 Vooral het beoordelen van informatie vinden zij moeilijk."

So the brief's "approximately 44–53%" is a rounding of **44–52.8%**; the factsheet itself is **internally inconsistent**, using 52.8% in the headline range and 52.3% in its own explanatory paragraph (§ "Toelichting cijfers") [D]. Two different Q47 figures in one document, unexplained.

The brief's phrase "depending on the 2026 survey and instrument" is **misleading in two ways**:

1. **Both surveys did fieldwork in 2025**, not 2026 — RIVM 6 June–6 July 2025 (**S4**, p. 9, 16); Nivel November 2025 (**S6**, sheet 10). 2026 is the *publication* year.
2. It is **not two surveys giving a range**; it is essentially **one population survey read at two lengths** (Q47 = 52.8%, Q12 = 46.6% in the *same* sample — S4, p. 24) plus **one small panel survey** (Q12 = 44.3% — S6). The 8.5-point range is mostly an *instrument-length* effect, not a population or survey effect.

### 2.3 Why the spread exists — a decomposition

The spread is **instrument-driven first, cut-off-driven second, sample-driven third, and only fourth a difference in what is being measured.** Retrieved evidence for each:

**(a) Instrument length / item selection — the largest single effect.** Q47 and Q12 correlate at r=0.92 yet disagree by 6.2 percentage points *within the same respondents* (**S4**, p. 24, 29) [D]. The authors' explanation, quoted verbatim by Pharos: the Q12 items "was gebaseerd op het conceptuele raamwerk en niet op basis van uitkomsten van de Q47", and "De Q12 is dus geen inhoudelijk equivalent van de Q47" (**S1**) [D]. RIVM adds that the Q12 subscales contain only 3–4 items each, so skill- and domain-level scores must be read with reliability in mind (**S4**, p. 30) [D].

**(b) Cut-off, which is conceptual rather than empirical.** RIVM states it directly [D]: "De scores werden volgens de M-POHL afkappunten geordend in vier categorieën: Ontoereikende (score 0-50); problematisch (>50-66.67); voldoende (>66.67-83.33); excellent (>83.33-100). Deze afkappunten zijn **conceptueel vastgesteld** op basis van het oorspronkelijke HLS-EU-Q47 model" (**S4**, p. 18).
**[I]** Because the score is the rescaled mean of a 4-point item set anchored 1 = *heel moeilijk* … 4 = *heel makkelijk*, a score of exactly 50 corresponds to a mean item rating of 2.5 — the arithmetic midpoint between "difficult" and "easy". "About half" is therefore close to what a population scoring around the *neutral midpoint* would produce by construction. The headline is not calibrated against any external criterion of functional difficulty, and no retrieved source reports a validation of the 50/66.67/83.33 boundaries against behaviour or outcomes.

**(c) Mode and sample.** RIVM attributes part of the 29.7%→52.8% rise to method: 2011 used personal interviews with a smaller sample, "Persoonlijke interviews kunnen leiden tot sociaal wenselijke antwoorden", while self-completion questionnaires exclude non-readers and may be misread (**S4**, p. 29) [D]. The panel arm and the BAG arm differ markedly (BAG respondents were older, lower-educated, more often born abroad — S4, p. 21–22) [D]. Pilot response was 55% in the panel and **6%** in the BAG sample (**S4**, p. 15) [D].

**(d) Instrument *family* — the clearest demonstration that this is not one quantity.** RIVM explains the contradiction between Nivel's panel (small or no migration differences) and the HELIUS cohort (large differences, lowest among Turkish, Moroccan and Ghanaian participants even after adjustment) by noting that HELIUS "niet gemeten werden op basis van zelfrapportage, maar aan de hand van een test, de Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine" (**S4**, p. 30) [D]. Same country, same construct name, opposite finding — because self-reported task difficulty and a word-recognition test measure different things.

**(e) A genuine fourth component: system complexity.** RIVM does not reduce the whole rise to method; it also argues "dat de hedendaagse complexe zorgsystemen met toenemende digitalisering een groter beroep doen op gezondheidsvaardigheden" (**S4**, p. 29) [D]. **[I]** This is a plausible and unfalsified explanation from a cross-sectional design; it cannot be separated from the instrument change with the data retrieved.

**The official Dutch framing agrees with (a).** De Staat van Volksgezondheid en Zorg states of the 44.3%: "Daardoor is het percentage hoger dan in eerdere jaren, toen het percentage schommelde tussen 29 en 35% maar waar gebruik gemaakt werd van de HLS-Q16" (**S7**) [D]. That is the national statistics portal saying the step change is a *measurement* step change.

### 2.4 What is *not* being measured, in the sources' own words

> "aan respondenten werd gevraagd hoe moeilijk of makkelijk zij bepaalde taken vinden. Dit meet **ervaren moeilijkheid, niet objectieve vaardigheid**." — **S4**, p. 31.

> "Tot slot is de vragenlijst hoogstwaarschijnlijk alleen ingevuld door mensen die kunnen lezen, waardoor mensen met de laagste gezondheidsvaardigheden waarschijnlijk ontbreken." — **S4**, p. 31.

**[I]** Both caveats push in the same direction for issue #25: the 44–53% band describes *self-reported difficulty with a stated task in a stated domain*, in a sample that excludes the least literate. It is a defensible population indicator and an indefensible individual diagnosis.

**Note on instruments the brief asked about.** I found **no** Dutch national series using the NVS, the HLQ, or a single-item screen; the Dutch series is HLS-EU-Q47 (2011), HLS-EU-Q16 (2012–2023, Nivel panel) and HLS19-Q47/Q12 (2025) [D, S4/S6/S7]. I also found **no** CBS Gezondheidsmonitor health-literacy item: the Staat VenZ kerncijfer is sourced to the Nivel Consumentenpanel, not to the CBS/GGD monitor (**S7**) [D/absence]. **[U]** whether a GGD-regional health-literacy item exists.

## 3. Does health literacy predict anything?

### 3.1 Associations — real, consistent in direction, and weaker than usually claimed

| Finding | Evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Low health literacy associated with more hospitalisation, more emergency care, lower mammography and influenza-vaccination uptake, poorer ability to demonstrate medication use and interpret labels; among elderly, poorer overall health status and **higher mortality** | Berkman et al. 2011, updated systematic review, **96 good/fair-quality studies in 111 articles**, AHRQ-funded (**S15**, abstract only) | **[D]** as *associations*; the review's own conclusion is "Low health literacy **is associated with** poorer health outcomes and poorer use of health care services" |
| Health literacy **partially mediates** SES → health behaviour/status, but accounts for only **3.8–16.0% of the total effect** | Berete et al. 2024, Belgian health interview survey linked to insurance data, n=8,080, HLS-EU-Q6 (**S16**, abstract only) | **[D]** — this is the single most useful number in this file for calibrating claims |
| Mediation is not monotonic: older adults had higher fruit/vegetable intake but **lower** health literacy; higher education was associated with better health literacy but a **negative** direct path to intake | Kirbiš et al. 2025, Slovenian HLS19, n=3,360 (**S17**, abstract only) | **[D]** — direction of effect is contested even within one dataset |
| The three Nutbeam levels predict **different** things (interactive → perceived control; functional → GP visit count) | van der Heide et al. 2015, Dutch, N=2,508 (**S18**, abstract only) | **[D]** |
| Of 167 critical-health-literacy articles, **only 28** explicitly reported associations with health behaviours, "the majority" significant, some mixed, and "**some indicated that CHL was associated with reduced health behaviors**" | Færevaag et al. 2026, scoping review, 11 databases (**S19**, abstract only) | **[D]** — the strongest retrieved counter-evidence to a simple "more literacy, better behaviour" story |

Pharos' own consequences page asserts effects on "gezondheidsuitkomsten, het zorggebruik en de zorgkosten", citing Berkman, Shahid, Hyvert, Persell and Koh (**S1**). Those are **[D]** as cited associations in Pharos' text; they are **not** causal estimates, and Pharos does not present them as such.

### 3.2 Is it modifiable? Yes — narrowly, and on the measured construct

- Digital health literacy interventions for older adults: **SMD 0.47 (95% CI 0.08–0.86)** favouring intervention, from five RCTs; 23 studies, 3,702 participants. Larger gains where digital *and* health knowledge were combined than digital skills alone (**S21**, abstract only) [D].
- Health literacy training for older adults: 15 of 18 included articles found an increase; meta-analysis of nine found positive effects vs control, with heterogeneity from instrument and format (**S22**, abstract only) [D].
- **Critical** health literacy interventions: 53 distinct interventions across 19 countries, concentrated in educational settings and mostly targeting school and university students; most addressed appraisal skills and individual action, while "broader CHL dimensions such as understanding social determinants of health and collective action were rarely addressed"; the review opens by stating "evidence on CHL-promoting interventions is low" (**S20**, abstract only) [D].

**[I]** What is demonstrably modifiable is *the score on the instrument being taught to* — knowledge, appraisal, digital navigation. There is no retrieved trial showing that raising a health-literacy score produces behaviour change or health gain. The causal chain is assumed, not shown.

### 3.3 Where causality and confounding are contested

- RIVM, explicitly: "De resultaten tonen verbanden, maar geen causaliteit" (**S4**, p. 31) [D].
- RIVM on confounding: poverty, education and work "zijn niet onafhankelijk van elkaar en liggen in elkaars verlengde. Dit laat zien dat gezondheidsvaardigheden ingebed zijn in bredere sociale en economische omstandigheden" (**S4**, p. 30) [D]. **[I]** If health literacy is largely co-determined by income and schooling, then an intervention that raises health literacy without touching income is fighting an effect that is mostly elsewhere — consistent with Berete's 3.8–16% mediation share.
- The measurement itself may be a proxy for something else: RIVM notes that very high Cronbach's alphas "wijzen erop dat sommige items mogelijk sterk overlappen in inhoud" (**S4**, p. 31) [D]. **[I]** A scale whose items are near-redundant and whose score tracks self-rated health and education may be partly measuring *experienced strain and position*, not a skill.

## 4. Food literacy: a sibling construct, with a Dutch operationalisation

### 4.1 Definitions

The definition used in Dutch practice, retrieved in full [D]:

> "Onder voedselvaardigheid (ook wel food literacy genoemd) wordt verstaan: 'het hebben van kennis en vaardigheden om eten te kunnen plannen, regelen, kiezen en bereiden ten behoeve van een gezonde, veilige en duurzamere voedingsinname'" — **S26** (Voedingscentrum authors in *Voeding Nu*, 8 Oct 2021), citing **Vidgen & Gallegos, *Appetite* 2014;76:50–59**.

**[U] I did not retrieve Vidgen & Gallegos 2014 itself** — it appears only as a citation inside S26 (record-only, second-hand). I therefore make no claim about its internal structure.

### 4.2 The Dutch instrument

Poelman et al. 2018 developed and validated the **Self-Perceived Food Literacy (SPFL)** scale on **n=755 Dutch adults** (mean age 44.8; **90.7% women; 59.1% highly educated**), reducing 50 items to **29 across eight domains** (planning, managing, selecting, preparing, eating healthfully). Convergent validity: self-control r=0.51, impulsiveness r=−0.31; higher SPFL associated with fruit ≥5×/week, vegetables ≥5×/week, fish ≥1×/week, ≥200 g vegetables/day. Discrimination: dieticians (n=207) scored only slightly higher (B=0.08) (**S25**, abstract only) [D].

Two cautions from that abstract itself: it is **self-perceived**, and the validation sample is a highly selected one. **[I]** A scale validated on 90.7%-female, 59%-highly-educated adults has unknown behaviour in exactly the population the Dutch health-literacy literature says is at risk.

### 4.3 How food literacy differs from health literacy

| | Health literacy (HLS19) | Food literacy (SPFL / Voedingscentrum) |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Health information in three domains (zorg, preventie, bevordering) | Food: planning, managing, selecting, preparing, eating |
| Core operation | Find, understand, appraise, apply information | Knowledge **plus** concrete domestic and provisioning skills |
| Includes behaviour? | No — perceived task difficulty only | Yes — the eight domains include planning, managing and preparing, not only knowledge |
| Orientation | Navigating a *system* (care, prevention) | Navigating a *household and a food environment* |
| Overlap | Q12 item 9 ("Vinden van informatie over gezond leven, bijvoorbeeld over lichaamsbeweging en gezond eten") and item 7 (appraising information about unhealthy habits) are the only food-adjacent items (**S6**, sheet 12) | — |

**[I]** The practical difference for issue #25 is that food literacy is the construct that *already contains doing* — cooking, planning, growing-adjacent skills — while health literacy contains *appraising*. A food forest plausibly produces food-literacy capability; claiming it produces health literacy requires an extra step through information appraisal that the construct does not supply by itself.

The one place the two constructs meet in the retrieved literature is "sustainable food literacy", which appears once among 17 environment-related literacies in **S27** [D].

## 5. The decisive question: has gardening/green practice been used *deliberately* as a health-literacy or food-literacy intervention?

### 5.1 Method

Four searches, all recorded in the source table and access log:

- **S-A** Europe PMC, **abstract-level**: `ABSTRACT:"health literacy" AND ABSTRACT:"garden"` → **3 hits**.
- **S-B** Europe PMC, **full-text**: `("community garden" OR "community gardening" OR "urban agriculture" OR "food forest") AND "health literacy"` → 127 hits (full-text matching; title-level inspection).
- **S-C** Europe PMC, **abstract-level**: `ABSTRACT:"food literacy" AND (ABSTRACT:"garden" OR ABSTRACT:"gardening")` → **20 hits**.
- **S-D** Europe PMC, exact phrase `"green health literacy"` → **1 hit**, unrelated (a scientometrics paper on COVID-19 social science).

### 5.2 What I found

**Health literacy as the outcome of gardening: not found.** The three abstract-level hits for health literacy + garden are: mental-health-literacy awareness among employees *of* a botanical garden (**S32**, abstract only); a trial arm nicknamed "Tea Garden" in an asylum-seeker psychoeducation study; and tea-garden workers' health in Bangladesh. None uses gardening as the intervention and health literacy as the outcome. Full-text searching added nothing at title level; the one conceptually interesting hit is a 2024 *European Journal of Public Health* abstract titled "Community gardening: metaphorical reconstruction of transformative health governance" — i.e. community gardening used as a **metaphor** for governance, not as an intervention [D, title-level only].

**"Green health literacy" is not an established construct.** The nearest real family is *environment-related* health literacy. Kallenbach et al. (2026) scoping review found **28 studies** describing **17 distinct concepts** — climate health literacy, climate-specific health literacy, environmental health literacy, planetary health literacy, One Health literacy, carbon health literacy, eco-medical literacy, sustainable food literacy — and concludes the field is "characterized by conceptual heterogeneity and overlap, with a focus on individual competences"; the framework developed "requires empirical validation"; the target group is overwhelmingly **health professionals and students**, not the public (**S27**, partial full text) [D].

**Food literacy as the outcome of gardening: well established — for children, in schools.** Twenty abstract-level hits, and the pattern is near-uniform:

- A Danish **quasi-experimental** study protocol (FoodACT / "Gardens to Bellies", six school-garden sites, preregistered NCT05839080) testing effects on food literacy, climate-change literacy, school motivation and physical activity (**S28**, abstract only) [D].
- A Tasmanian **realist evaluation** of a school kitchen-and-garden programme, framed explicitly as "Enhancing Food Literacy and Food Security through School Gardening" (**S29**, abstract only) [D].
- A **systematic review of school-based interventions on Food and Nutrition Literacy**, structured by **Nutbeam's functional / interactive / critical levels** as the primary outcomes (**S31**, abstract only) [D] — the clearest retrieved bridge between the two literatures.
- A US school-based **"Therapeutic Greenhouse"** programme that "bridges hands-on food literacy education with clinical care delivery", running since 2020 in an underserved community (**S30b**, abstract only) [D].
- Australian primary schools: 111 staff surveyed; the paper opens "School gardens are an effective strategy to increase food literacy" and then documents barriers of funding, time and training [D].
- Adult/community exceptions are **qualitative and non-outcome**: the askîy urban-agriculture project (Canada) on capacity-building among young adults (**S30**, abstract only) [D].

**The Dutch operationalisation is the *schooltuin*, and it is children's education.** The Voedingscentrum article retrieved in full states that "De effectiviteit van voedselonderwijs wordt vergroot door kennis te combineren met vaardigheden, zoals eten bereiden, proeven, samen eten of **tuinieren in de schooltuin**", within a curriculum framework (SLO leerlijn) and the Gezonde School approach; and that "Voedseleducatie is niet verplicht en daarmee geen structureel onderdeel van het Nederlandse curriculum" (**S26**, full text) [D]. Smaaklessen carries a CGL recognition of "eerste aanwijzing voor effectiviteit" — the *second-lowest* tier of the Dutch recognition system [D].

### 5.3 What I did not find — stated as clearly as what I did

- **[U]** No retrieved study in which an adult community garden, allotment, urban farm or food forest is used **deliberately as a health-literacy intervention**, with health literacy measured as an outcome.
- **[U]** No retrieved Dutch practice labelled *gezondheidsvaardigheden* that uses growing, gardening or a food forest as its vehicle. Pharos' instrument set (S3) is entirely communication-and-organisation facing: information, digital care, wayfinding, conversation training, extra support. Nothing horticultural.
- **[U]** No retrieved evidence that a *green* setting is claimed, by any health-literacy actor, as a health-literacy intervention setting.
- **[U]** No Dutch adult *food*-literacy intervention delivered through a community garden; the Dutch route is school-based voedselonderwijs.

**[I]** The honest reading: for **food literacy**, gardening is an established delivery mechanism with a school-age evidence base of moderate quality and an outcome vocabulary (functional/interactive/critical) that already matches Nutbeam. For **health literacy**, the mechanism is absent from the literature — not refuted, simply not attempted or not published in the sources I could reach. Any claim in the Lead's note that green practice "strengthens health literacy" would be an **inference from a sibling construct with a different outcome measure**, not a documented effect.

## 6. Counter-evidence and tensions I went looking for

**6.1 The deficit-model critique — and it is current, not historical.**
Okuhara, Okada & Furukawa (2026) name the pattern "**health literacy reductionism**": "the tendency to preferentially translate complex health communication and health promotion problems into a lack of individual health literacy among patients or the public", which backgrounds "relational, organizational, and structural explanations". They distinguish it from adjacent critiques (deficit framing, victim blaming, stigma, epistemic injustice, structural approaches, medicalization) and propose four diagnostic codes: **deficit attribution, presumed incomprehension, omitted alternatives, design invisibilization** (**S14**, abstract only) [D]. Chinn (2011) records the older version of the same charge: that the concept is "poorly defined", "stretches the idea of 'literacy' to an indefensible extent", and "adds little to the existing concerns and intervention approaches of the better established discipline of health promotion" (**S9**) [D].

**6.2 The demand side argument is stronger than the skill side in the retrieved record.**
RIVM's policy recommendations are almost entirely organisational and systemic — simplify services, provide understandable information, align communication, put health literacy "centraal in beleid, kwaliteitsverbetering en systematische meting" — and name "organizational health literacy" as "een belangrijk aangrijpingspunt" (**S4**, pp. 31–32) [D]. Pharos says the same (**S2**). The counterweight is 6.1's point inverted: the demand-side position is *also* under-evidenced — Aaby et al. found only nine publications with organisational responsiveness outcomes (**S12**) [D]. **[I]** Neither side of the Dutch debate currently has outcome evidence; the disagreement is about where to intervene, not about what works.

A third, quieter tension: Pal et al. (2026) analysed 27 sets of plain-language-resource development recommendations and found "only ∼40% specifically incorporated health literacy principles", with guidance "often equated understanding of content with purely reading and writing comprehension" (**S30c**, abstract only) [D]. The producers of health information are not applying the framework they endorse — which is evidence for the demand-side critique from the supply side.

**6.3 Measurement critiques.**
- **Cut-offs are conceptual, not validated** — see §2.3(b), quoted from **S4** p. 18 [D].
- **Dimensionality does not hold cleanly.** Rasch analysis of the Chinese HLS-EU-Q47 in 957 older adults "revealed **substantial item misfit and DIF in the original HLS-EU-Q47**", requiring item removal to reach a working 12-item form (**S23**, abstract only) [D]. A Slovenian HLS-EU-Q6 validation found "A one-factor structure demonstrated good fit among older adults but **weaker fit among youth, adults, and the total sample**" (**S24**, abstract only) [D].
- **The domain story is instrument-dependent.** On Q47, domains differ substantially — gezondheidsbevordering 63.0%, ziektepreventie 51.4%, gezondheidszorg 50.0% (**S4**, p. 24) [D]. On Q12 in the panel, they barely differ — 54.2% / 55.2% / 48.8% — and Nivel states the rate "verschilt niet of nauwelijks naar type vaardigheid" (**S6**, sheets 15–16) [D]. **[I]** "People struggle most with health promotion" is a Q47 finding that the shorter instrument does not reproduce; a note citing it should cite the instrument.
- **Self-report vs test divergence within one country** — Nivel vs HELIUS/REALM, §2.3(d) [D].

**6.4 Null or weak associations.** §3.1: only 28 of 167 CHL articles reported behavioural associations, with some showing *reduced* behaviour (**S19**); mediation share 3.8–16.0% (**S16**); non-monotonic directions within one national dataset (**S17**). **[I]** The defensible claim is "health literacy is a marker of strain and of position in a system", not "health literacy causes behaviour".

**6.5 A tension I could not resolve.** RIVM finds **men** report more problems than women (54.9% vs 50.9%, **S4** p. 26), and Nivel reports the same direction (**S6**, sheet 17), against a literature and policy framing that treats low health literacy as primarily a women's, older people's and lower-educated issue. The gender finding is stated but not explained in either source. **[U]** why. It sits awkwardly beside the strong, consistent education and income gradients and is worth a footnote in the Lead's note rather than a claim.
