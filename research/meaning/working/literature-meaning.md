# What the literature actually supports about meaning and motivation in the care of green places

**Line of investigation:** task-2, serving issue #19. **Author:** literature-meaning (bounded researcher).
**Retrieved:** 2026-11-26, UTC. **Deliverable status:** working note for the PI; primary output of this line.

**What this file is.** A claim-by-claim account of what peer-reviewed and review literature says about (1) self-determination theory (SDT) applied to nature volunteering and community gardening, (2) nature connectedness, (3) place attachment and place loss, (4) ecological grief / solastalgia, (5) meaningful activity / occupational science, (6) care farming and social & therapeutic horticulture, (7) motivations to join versus what emerges through participation, including episodic participation and dropping out. Every row carries design, N, country, the finding actually reported, and the critique or counter-evidence. Read status is stated for each source: **abstract read in full**, **abstract + intro read**, or **bibliographic record only** (no abstract retrievable — such sources are *not* used for substantive claims).

**What this file is not.** It is not an admission of evidence to the corpus; that is the reviewer's act under `PROVENANCE.md`. It is not a wellbeing-outcome review (that is a different issue and a different line); health effects appear here only where they are the vehicle by which the literature makes its meaning claims. Training knowledge was not used: every claim below rests on a record I retrieved, and where a paper looked promising but I could not read it, it is listed as a dead end rather than paraphrased.

**Retrieval routes used.** Publisher landing pages for Elsevier, Taylor & Francis, SAGE and Springer were not attempted beyond registry checks: the abstract-bearing records below come from (a) the OpenAlex public API record for the DOI (title, venue, year, citation count, abstract), (b) the Semantic Scholar API record for the DOI (used where OpenAlex held no abstract), and (c) one open-access full-text PDF on HAL for Barragan-Jason et al. No paywall, login or challenge was bypassed. Where no route yielded an abstract, the paper is marked *record only* and excluded from claims. This is a real limitation: I read abstracts and, for one paper, the abstract plus introduction — **I did not read the full text of any of the empirical studies**, so I report what the authors themselves state they found, not an independent check of their tables.

## 1. Self-determination theory: does it explain joining, continuing and quitting?

| # | Claim as stated in the literature | Citation | Design | N / country | What was actually reported | Critique / counter-evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | Three innate psychological needs — competence, autonomy, relatedness — when satisfied yield enhanced self-motivation and mental health; when thwarted, diminished motivation and wellbeing. | Ryan et al. (2000) *American Psychologist*. DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68. **Abstract read** | Theoretical synthesis of a research programme | n/a; USA-based programme, international reach | The three-need postulate and the claim that social-contextual conditions facilitate or forestall intrinsic motivation | This is a *lens*, not a test. It is stated as a postulate derived from prior findings. Nothing in this paper tests nature volunteering, gardening or care work. Citation count (~73,600 in OpenAlex) reflects influence, not evidence. |
| S2 | In environmental stewardship, volunteers' *initial* motives are helping the environment and learning; but *commitment* is predicted by social factors and project organisation. | Ryan et al. (2001) *Journal of Environmental Planning and Management*. DOI 10.1080/09640560120079948. **Abstract read** | Cross-sectional survey of long-term volunteers in three stewardship programmes | N=148; Michigan, USA | "Helping the environment and learning were important initial motivations. Other motivations, including social factors and project organization, were found to be significant predictors of volunteer commitment." Volunteers also reported "many significant changes in their environmental outlook and actions during their involvement". | The key tension in this whole file: the motive that brings people in (nature/helping) is not the one that predicts staying. Effect sizes are not given in the abstract. Single US state, self-report, cross-sectional: it cannot order joining, continuing and quitting. Retrospective reports of changed outlook are also exactly what a post-hoc-justification reading predicts. |
| S3 | SDT's ordering of motives does not hold cleanly: identified regulation predicts initial/short-term adoption more strongly than intrinsic motivation, while intrinsic motivation predicts long-term adherence. | Teixeira et al. (2012) *Int J Behav Nutr Phys Act* (article 78). DOI 10.1186/1479-5868-9-78. **Abstract read** | Systematic review of 66 empirical studies (experimental, cross-sectional, prospective) | 66 studies; international; **exercise/physical activity, not nature** | "a trend towards identified regulation predicting initial/short-term adoption more strongly than intrinsic motivation, and intrinsic motivation being more predictive of long-term exercise adherence"; "mixed evidence" on introjected regulation; "majority of studies have employed descriptive (i.e., non-experimental) designs" | The strongest retrieved contradiction of a simple intrinsic-first story — but from a different behavioural domain. It implies the useful question for a food forest is not "are motives intrinsic?" but "does the motive become more internalised over time, and what supports that?" |
| S4 | Volunteer commitment is a staged trajectory, not a stable motive: nominee → newcomer → emotional involvement → established volunteering → retiring, with shifting benefits, costs and relationships. | Haski-Leventhal et al. (2008) *Human Relations*. DOI 10.1177/0018726707085946. **Abstract read** | Ethnography, single organisation | Volunteers for at-risk youth, Israel (N not stated in abstract) | Five phases with "transitions between the phases" and "the process, experiences, and emotions involved in each phase" | Ethnographic and single-organisation; not nature. But it is the best retrieved source for the *shape* of continuation and exit, including a "retiring" phase — i.e. leaving is normal, not failure. |
| S5 | Episodic (short-term, event-based) volunteers' motives cluster on helping others, socialising and self-enhancement; the evidence base is inconsistent and mostly atheoretical. | Dunn et al. (2016) *VOLUNTAS* DOI 10.1007/s11266-015-9548-4. **Abstract read**. Corroborated by Hyde et al. (2014) *BMC Public Health* (article 992), DOI 10.1186/1471-2458-14-992. **Abstract read** | Two systematic reviews | Dunn: 33 studies; Hyde: 41 articles (20 in health/social welfare); predominantly North America | Dunn: "Over 80% of motives were classified using VFI functions, particularly enhancement, values and social functions." Hyde: "little consistency in EV antecedents and outcomes, except motives which primarily related to helping others, forming social connections, and self-psychological or physical enhancement"; "most studies were atheoretical" | Motive inventories measure *stated* reasons. Neither review finds a nature/place motive as a distinct category — a caution for any claim that ecological care is the thing that draws people. Also note Hyde's sample description: episodic volunteers were "predominantly female, middle aged, Caucasian (North American) and college/university educated" — a selection problem, not a population description. |

**Verdict for topic 1.** SDT is a usable *lens* (it generates the right questions about autonomy, competence and relatedness at a work morning) but I retrieved **no study that applies SDT to community gardening, food forests or nature volunteering and tests need satisfaction against continuation or quitting**. The nearest applications (S2, S3) qualify the theory rather than confirm it, and both put social and organisational factors ahead of the nature motive for staying.

## 2. Nature connectedness: relationship to wellbeing and pro-environmental behaviour, and its critique

| # | Claim as stated | Citation | Design | N / country | What was actually reported | Critique / counter-evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | Nature connectedness correlates with eudaimonic wellbeing, and the association is no stronger than with hedonic wellbeing. | Pritchard et al. (2019/2020) *Journal of Happiness Studies*. **Verdict for topic 2.** Established: connectedness correlates with self-reported wellbeing (small, r≈0.2) and with pro-environmental behaviour (r≈0.37), and short-term contact experiments raise connectedness. Contested: causal size (d≈0.21 when unpublished work is included), and whether connection is cause or consequence of participation. Not established in anything I retrieved: cultural specificity of the measures beyond the authors' own admission of Northern/adult bias; any validation of these measures in Dutch or in a food-forest volunteer population.

## 8. Synthesis: established, contested, not established — and lenses versus findings

### Established on the retrieved evidence (with design caveats stated every time)
1. **Structured nature-based activity has pooled mental-health effects in reviews** (T1: depression g≈0.64, anxiety g≈0.94, 16 RCTs among 50 studies) and **gardening reviews find positive effects that survive publication-bias correction** (T2: 22 studies). Both are about *interventions*, not about community volunteering.
2. **Nature connectedness correlates with self-reported wellbeing (r≈0.20–0.24) and with pro-environmental behaviour (r≈0.37)**, with causal estimates far smaller (d≈0.21) once unpublished experiments are included (N1, N2). Effects are small and entirely self-report.
3. **Contact with nature raises connection to nature in short-term experiments** (N3, N5).
4. **Social and organisational factors — not the nature motive — predict volunteer commitment** (S2, N=148; M1, M2), and in Dutch community gardens social cohesion arises even among people who came to grow vegetables (M2).
5. **The prime stated motive for gardening is pleasure, not health** (M3, n=5,766), while what participants *describe* as valuable in care farming is achievement, fulfilment, belonging and contact (O5).
6. **Observational volunteering–health findings are not confirmed by experimental studies** (M5, 40 papers; RR 0.78 mortality from cohorts only).
7. **Care farms show no measured quality-of-life improvement** despite rich accounts of meaning (O5). **The one community-gardening RCT shows small, partly ambiguous effects** (T6).
8. **Mean "meaning" is not the same as measured wellbeing** — and the literature repeatedly demonstrates the gap rather than closing it.

### Contested
- **Does SDT's ordering hold?** Identified (not intrinsic) regulation predicts short-term adoption; intrinsic predicts long-term adherence (S3, 66 studies). So "intrinsic motivation" may be an *outcome* of good participation rather than its precondition.
- **Direction between connection and participation** (N6 vs N5): retrospective volunteer reports (participation → outlook) versus short-term experiments (contact → connection). Neither addresses years-long unpaid care.
- **Whether gardening/nature benefits are causal in ordinary, self-selected community settings.** Best causal design (T6) is modest; the Dutch comparison (T5) shows benefits only for older gardeners with acknowledged self-selection.
- **Whether place attachment survives the loss of a specific site.** Not tested in anything retrieved.
- **Whether green care is a health intervention or an instrumentalisation of care.** Fixsen (T7) shows practitioners themselves are divided, and access follows advantage.

### Not established in the sources I could reach
- Any study of **grief for a lost garden, allotment or volunteer community**. Solastalgia and ecological grief are about landscapes and homes (P2, P3, G1); transplanting them to a closed food forest is **analogy**.
- Any **SDT application to community gardening or food forests** with continuation/attrition outcomes.
- **Dropout, burnout, obligation or exclusion among gardeners/volunteers** — no retrieved study; the "novelty then decline" pattern is undocumented here.
- **Portability of place attachment** to a successor site (Voedselbos 2.0).
- **Cultural validation of nature-connection measures** outside Northern adult samples; the authors of the review of reviews concede the bias (N3) and the measures review concedes construct proliferation (N4).
- Any **community food forest** evidence at all: searches for Dutch food-forest and community food-forest research returned nothing usable (dead end).

### Lenses versus findings
- **Lenses (conceptual, do not carry empirical weight):** SDT's three needs (S1); place attachment as person–process–place (P1); solastalgia as a concept (P2, G2); ecological grief (G1); "doing, being, belonging, becoming" and the critique of productivity categories (O2, O3); "dark side of occupation" (O4, unread).
- **Findings (specific, bounded, with numbers and designs):** N1, N2, N3, T1, T2, T5, T6, M3, M5, S2, M2, O5.
- The issue's instruction to treat concepts as possible lenses rather than explanations is therefore *supported by the literature itself*: the most-cited constructs in this field are frameworks, while the numerical findings are small, self-reported and domain-specific.

## 9. The strongest rival interpretation: that the meaning is social, post-hoc, or obligation dressed as pleasure

The PI asked for the rival reading to be stated plainly. Three versions, each with the best retrieved support:

**(a) People come for company; the green setting is the pretext.** Strongest support: M2 — seven Dutch gardens, social cohesion and mutual help arose "even if people are not particularly driven by social motivations", and the social aspect "bring[s] added value for those participants who are motivated primarily by growing vegetables". S2 reinforces it: social factors and project organisation predicted *commitment* while the environmental motive predicted only entry. M5 shows that the wellbeing story is strongest in observational designs, which is what you would expect if healthier, better-connected people both volunteer and report wellbeing. Counter-consideration: M2 is qualitative and reports interaction, not the meaning people assign to the interaction; and M3's 5,766 gardeners named direct pleasure — not company — as the prime motive. The rival reading is credible but **not decisive**: both can be true at different points in a volunteer's trajectory, which is exactly what S4's staged model predicts.

**(b) Stated meaning is post-hoc justification.** Strongest support: S2's retrospective reports of changed environmental outlook ("many significant changes… during their involvement") from a cross-sectional survey with no baseline — equally consistent with genuine change and with justification of years already given. N1's finding that *personal growth* rather than *meaning* carries the connectedness association also suggests that when people are asked about meaning, they may be reporting a story about themselves. No retrieved study tests post-hoc rationalisation directly (e.g. comparing motives at entry with accounts at exit). **This is a live alternative that the evidence cannot exclude** — and it is a reason to collect entry-time accounts in any future fieldwork rather than retrospective ones.

**(c) Care is obligation dressed as pleasure.** The literature I retrieved does not establish this, and I want to be honest that the case is largely inferential: unpaid care work is gendered and unequally distributed in the broader literature (not retrieved here), the gardening/volunteering samples are disproportionately female, middle-aged, educated and (in the volunteer reviews) North American and Caucasian (S5), and the care-farming review (O5) shows that the *organisational* setting — a service, with staff and referrals — shapes what people take from the activity. The obligation reading is best supported not by a gardening study but by T7: green social prescribing routes people into green activity through a referral system, and link workers are the ones who see the transport, mobility and anxiety barriers. The most defensible formulation: **meaning and obligation are not opposites in this literature; the same activity can be freely chosen by one participant and a referred duty for another, and no retrieved study measures the mix.** The careful report will say "volunteers describe it as meaningful" without claiming that this settles whether it is freely chosen.

**One more rival worth naming:** meaning may be **place-specific and therefore non-transferable**. The solastalgia literature (P2, P3) is the only retrieved body of work that takes seriously the possibility that meaning is bound to a particular place and is damaged when that place changes. If that is right, Voedselbos 2.0 cannot inherit the meaning of the 0.7 ha site; it can only grow a new one. Nothing I retrieved tests this, but it is the rival interpretation with the largest practical consequence for the case, and it is where the evidence is thinnest.

## 11. What this means for the desk-research question, and what would change the answer

**Implications.** The literature supports a *middle* position, which is convenient for issue #19 and should be stated without inflation:
- It is defensible to say that people who tend green places commonly describe pleasure, achievement, belonging, sanctuary and self-development, and to quote that as meaning (M3, M4, O5, T4).
- It is defensible to say that shared care produces social connection even for people who did not come for it (M2) — the best Dutch evidence in this file.
- It is **not** defensible to claim that a food forest improves wellbeing, builds nature connection, or retains volunteers, on the strength of this literature. The causal designs are few, modest and ambiguous (T6); the observational/experimental divergence is explicit (M5); the Dutch data show age-moderated and self-selected effects (T5); and quality-of-life outcomes did not move in the best care-farming review (O5).
- For Voedselbos 2.0 the literature's message is about *design*: motivations differ and change (S2, S3, S4), organisational and social factors hold people (S2, M2), and access is sorted by advantage unless actively managed (T7). Meaning is not a property of the site that can be carried across; on the evidence available, it has to be rebuilt, which is a hypothesis the case can test but the literature cannot settle.

**What would change the answer.** A longitudinal study of volunteers with baseline measures at entry (motive, connection, attachment) and follow-up at exit — none retrieved; a study of a garden closure with pre/post attachment or grief measures — none retrieved; a Dutch food-forest volunteer study — none retrieved; a full-text reading (not abstract) of T6 and O5 to check whether the printed CIs and the null QoL finding hold in the tables; and any test of the post-hoc-justification reading, which would require entry-time accounts.
