## 2. Synthesis: what is evidenced, assumed, contradicted

**Well evidenced (multiple designs, several countries)**
- *Tie capacity is finite and layered.* The 5–15–50–150 structure with linear maintenance costs (row 7) is the strongest available constraint on any claim that a place "builds community" at scale. A 30-volunteer Friday group is structurally a ~50-layer body in which only a handful of relations can be close `[I]`.
- *Contact changes attitudes under conditions, not relationships by default.* The 515-study meta-analysis (row 6) is a genuinely strong finding, and it is about prejudice. Everything beyond that — friendship, trust, mutual obligation — is not established by it.
- *Gardens are real sites of informal, peer-to-peer learning.* Two independent qualitative studies on two continents (rows 1–2) converge: people learn the practice by working beside others, and they say so unprompted.
- *Commons survive or fail on institutions, not goodwill.* Ostrom's principles and the >100-study assessment (row 14) plus garden-level evidence of uneven contribution and conflict (row 15) together say: the presence of free-riders is normal; the absence of monitoring, sanctions and cheap conflict resolution is the pathology.
- *Gardens have measurable external effects on land value* (row 16), especially in poor neighbourhoods.

**Assumed, thin, or circulating without support in what I retrieved**
- *"Shared activity creates durable relationships."* No retrieved study measures durable ties formed at a garden, with a comparison group or a follow-up. Kingsley's participants say social connection sustained their involvement — that is a motive report from the already-involved. The strongest empirical statement available is weaker than the common claim: gardens produce *acquaintance and mutual recognition of competence*, and sometimes friendship, by participants' own account.
- *"Social infrastructure generates encounter."* This is the Klinenberg position, and I could **not** retrieve Klinenberg (`[U]`, §6). What I did retrieve is a peer-reviewed critique arguing the encounter effect "cannot be assumed" (row 4) and a feminist critique that the concept erases the labour that makes the space work (row 5). The optimistic version is currently better described as a widely repeated framing than as an established finding for gardens `[I]`.
- *"Care is reciprocal and pleasurable."* Care ethics supplies the vocabulary for its failure modes (rows 9–11) but no retrieved measurement of who does the caring in this kind of initiative.
- *"Visibility builds community."* Nothing retrieved connects publicity to recognition among peers (see §4).

**Contradicted, or at least directly qualified**
- *Contact → community.* Qualify hard: prejudice reduction ≠ relationships (row 6); gardeners self-select for similarity (row 8).
- *Community garden → community.* Contradicted by governance evidence: some gardens are explicitly individualistic plot-holding regimes (row 18).
- *Care → good.* Contradicted by the multispecies-care case, where care practices carry "harm" and produce inclusion/exclusion (row 11), and by the care-ethics critique that valorised care can entrench subordination (row 10).
- *Participation → empowerment.* Contradicted structurally by Arnstein (row 13) and empirically-adjacent by Rosol's finding that participation can be an expression of neoliberalisation (row 17).
- *Commons → harmony.* Contradicted: commons are governed, monitored, sanctioned, and bounded; boundary-drawing is principle 1A, i.e. exclusion is constitutive, not a bug (row 14).

## 3. Care as pleasure vs obligation; belonging vs conformity

### 3.1 Pleasure and obligation are co-present in the same person, in the same hour

The sharpest retrieved evidence is Schoneboom's portrait of Emma, a caregiver and silversmith, who calls the allotment "incredibly relaxing and fruitful" and "stress-inducing at the same time" `[FT]`. The same set of interviews yields: flow and escape ("no-one knows where I'm here, and no-one can come and get me"); guilt when the plot is neglected; and "weed warnings" — the site's own enforcement that a plot must be cultivated. Obligation here is not the opposite of pleasure; it is the condition under which the pleasure is available (a plot one must keep up), and it is externally policed.

Kingsley et al. add the motivational asymmetry that matters for the issue's warning to separate *motivations to join* from *experiences through participation*: "Some participants cited social reasons for joining, however this was more a driver for sustaining involvement" `[FT]`. Joining is about the practice (childhood, food, nature); staying is disproportionately about people. Time costs and governance were the named barriers.

Theory supplies the mechanism for why obligation is intrinsic rather than accidental. Tronto's third and fourth elements — competence and responsiveness — make care answerable to the cared-for, so a care relation always contains a demand one can fail (row 9). The feminist critique adds the political economy: when care is unpaid and gendered, its pleasures are subsidised by someone's disadvantage, and an ethic that celebrates care without asking *who cares for whom* is not liberatory (row 10). Hall's critique makes the same move at the level of the concept: defining social infrastructure as *spaces* rather than *labour* erases the (gendered) work that keeps them going (row 5).

**Strongest evidence for the pleasure side:** Schoneboom's autonomy/flow accounts; Kingsley's "part of something bigger", stress relief, identity and pride; Honneth's theoretical claim that being esteemed for one's contribution is a condition of self-esteem (row 12).
**Strongest evidence for the obligation side:** the same two studies' guilt, time-cost, conflict and enforcement accounts; Tronto's competence/responsiveness as built-in failure points; the unpaid/gendered critique; Arnstein's "therapy" rung, where being cared for is a way of being managed (row 13).

### 3.2 Belonging vs conformity

Belonging in the retrieved evidence is repeatedly described in the language of similarity: "like-minded people", "clicked", "belonging to a family" (Kingsley `[FT]`). That is warm, and it is also a selection mechanism. Three retrieved findings make the conformist risk concrete:

1. **Esteem requires shared values.** In Honneth, the third sphere is precisely "networks of solidarity and shared values" — worth is acknowledged *against a background of shared values* (row 12). Recognition therefore rewards legibility to the existing community, not difference.
2. **Commons are bounded by definition.** Ostrom's principle 1A is "clear and locally understood boundaries between legitimate users and nonusers" (row 14). Any commons excludes; the only questions are who draws the line and on what basis.
3. **Ethos is a governance choice.** Toda's six gardens split between community-engagement and individualistic ethoses (row 18). "Belonging" is not what a garden *is*; it is what a garden's rules and leadership *do*.

Counterweight — the freedom not to participate is documented, not just idealised. Schoneboom's lecturer values the plot because "no-one knows where I'm here, and no-one can come and get me"; some postsuburban gardens are run so that gardening is "an independent activity" per plot (row 18). The case file's own anchor records the coordinator's formulation: everyone is free to join one week and not the next (`lead-anchor.md` §2, second-hand `[C]`). Voluntariness of that kind is a design feature that protects against conformity pressure — and it is also, precisely, the feature that makes durable ties unlikely.

What is `[U]`: whether *this* initiative's sociability was experienced as pressure by anyone. There is no first-person volunteer account in the public record (`lead-anchor.md` §10), so any claim that participation was experienced as obligation at Voedselbos Eindhoven is unsupported.

## 4. Recognition and mutual recognition as a rival reading of visibility

**The reading.** Issue #17 treats visibility mainly as a problem of publicity (found/not found, on the map, in the network). The relational literature suggests a different object: visibility as *mutual recognition among people and initiatives who share a practice* — being seen by peers as someone whose contribution counts, rather than being seen by an audience.

**What the theory actually specifies.** Honneth's third sphere is the relevant one, and it is not publicity: esteem is granted within "networks of solidarity and shared values within which the particular worth of individual members of a community can be acknowledged" `[FT]`. Two implications follow directly. (a) The recogniser must be a participant in the same valuing community; an outside audience cannot, on this account, confer esteem — it can confer attention. (b) The characteristic injury is not invisibility but *misrecognition*: "humiliation, degradation, insult, disenfranchisement" (translator's gloss of *Mißachtung*).

**What the evidence says about recognition as a mechanism.** I found no retrieved empirical study that tests recognition among garden or food-forest initiatives. What exists is (i) a theory with a specified mechanism (Honneth), (ii) a canonical account of what happens when participation is staged for outside consumption rather than shared power (Arnstein), and (iii) care-ethics work in which *responsiveness* — "consideration of the position of others as they see it and recognition of the potential for abuse in care" — is a constitutive element, i.e. being seen on one's own terms is not optional decorum but a condition of care not becoming abuse (row 9).

**Failure modes, with the best available support.**
- *Being shown without consent / being instrumentalised.* Arnstein's manipulation rung: citizens placed on committees "for the express purpose of 'educating' them or engineering their support"; advisory bodies that "functioned mostly as letterheads, trotted forward at appropriate times"; leaders hired "to muzzle them while destroying their credibility in the black community" `[FT]`. This is the closest retrieved analogue of "the poster case", and it is a *power* diagnosis, not an image problem: display without decision rights.
- *Therapy.* Being cared for *as* the problem: programmes that "cure" participants of "pathology" rather than change the conditions producing it (Arnstein). Directly relevant to green care framed as burn-out prevention (the case's own site frames participants as recipients of a health intervention — `lead-anchor.md` §4).
- *Misrecognition by category.* Being visible as a representative of a category (volunteer, lonely elder, migrant) rather than as a practitioner, which is what Honneth's disrespect class describes.
- *Recognition captured by the same few.* The tie-layer evidence (row 7) implies that in a 30-person group, esteem will concentrate on a small number of salient contributors; a rotating "poster" is the predictable equilibrium `[I]`.

**What would falsify recognition-as-visibility.** Stated so it can be tested against interviews or documents:
1. If initiatives report that *publicity* events (press, municipal platforms, awards) produced a felt change in standing *among peers* — i.e. that outside attention did the recognitive work — the rival reading loses its distinctive claim.
2. If participants describe their good experiences in the vocabulary of *being seen by an audience* (visitors, funders, the city) rather than by co-workers, the mechanism is publicity after all.
3. If esteem is granted symmetrically by non-participants (neighbours, officials) and that suffices for people to keep contributing, then the "shared values/shared practice" condition in Honneth is unnecessary.
4. Conversely, the reading gains support if exit, resentment or withdrawal cluster around moments of being displayed (a photo, a visit, a grant application) rather than around workload.
5. If a site sustains mutual recognition well beyond the ~150 tie ceiling (e.g. a city-wide network where people know each other's work), the tie-layer constraint on recognition is wrong or recognition does not require personal ties.

Current status: **provisional rival interpretation, theoretically motivated, empirically untested in this domain** `[I]`. It should not be reported as a finding.

## 5. Commoning and its limits (in brief)

Ostrom's contribution is not that commons work; it is that they work *when* a specific institutional set is present, and that "one-size-fits-all" policy fails `[FT]`. For a volunteer food forest on private ground the interesting columns are: boundaries (who is a member — for this case, undocumented), collective choice (who decides what gets planted — one coordinator in the record), monitoring and graduated sanctions (nothing documented), conflict resolution (nothing documented), and **minimal recognition of rights by government** — the principle that most directly names the case's structural problem, since a twelve-year commons on private land had no recognised right to make its own rules `[I]`. The "tragedy of the uninterested" is the mirror of the free-rider problem: both are handled, when they are handled, by monitoring and low-cost sanctions, not by exhortation.

## 7. What this means for the meaning question (implications, not findings)

1. The strongest defensible relational claim is modest: **shared work produces acquaintance, practical learning and mutual acknowledgement of competence, and sometimes friendship, for people who self-select into it.** Anything stronger — that a place builds community, that encounter produces trust, that care is reciprocal — outruns the retrieved evidence.
2. The sharpest warning against romanticisation is structural rather than attitudinal: **where participation has no decision rights, being shown is manipulation** (Arnstein), and **where care is unpaid and gendered, celebrating it can entrench the injustice** (care-ethics critique; Hall). Both are about power, not sentiment.
3. **Belonging and conformity are the same mechanism seen from two sides** (Honneth's shared-values condition; Ostrom's boundary principle; homogeneity in Kingsley's gardens). A community that makes people feel seen is also a community that makes some people legible and others not.
4. **The freedom not to participate is a substantive good with a relational cost.** It protects against conformity and obligation, and it is exactly what makes close ties improbable (Schoneboom's autonomy; Toda's individualistic ethos; the tie ceiling).
5. **Recognition-as-visibility is a better-motivated rival to publicity-as-visibility than the evidence can yet settle.** It should be carried forward as a hypothesis with the five falsifiers in §4, not as a conclusion.
