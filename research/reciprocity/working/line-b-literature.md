# The literature lens on reciprocity: what it establishes, and where it is only analogy

**Line of investigation:** line B (literature), serving issue #5 — "What makes reciprocity between initiatives durable rather than extractive?"
**Author:** delegated literature researcher. **Retrieved:** 2026-11-26 (UTC). **Status:** working note for the Lead; primary output of this line.

**What this file is.** A claim-by-claim account of seven literature lenses on reciprocity — norm and types (Gouldner, Sahlins), gift exchange (Mauss), social exchange and power-dependence (Emerson, Blau, Homans; critiques), commons governance (Ostrom and its critics), thin vs thick trust, care webs / mutual aid, and coordination labour — with, for each lens, one verbatim quote from a retrieved text and an explicit **transfer assessment**: what the lens licenses for Eindhoven and what it does not.

**What this file is not.** It is not evidence about Eindhoven. Not one source below studies Eindhoven, Dutch civil society, food forests, or relations between formal organisations. Every lens here is an **analogy** whose strength must be argued, not assumed. It is not an admission of evidence to the corpus; that is the reviewer's act under `PROVENANCE.md`. No training knowledge was used: every substantive claim rests on a retrieved text, and where I could not retrieve a text I say so rather than paraphrase it.

**Marking conventions.** **[D]** = what the retrieved text says (documented at the depth stated). **[I]** = my inference, mine and not the author's. **[U]** = not established, or not retrievable. Read depth is stated per source: *full text*, *abstract only*, *record only*. Page numbers appear only where I saw the printed page number in the retrieved representation. Where a quote comes from a **translation**, that is stated — a translation is a derivative witness, not the original.

**OCR rule.** Four of the full texts below are OCR of scans (S1, M1, O1, P1). Where the OCR is plainly corrupt I have normalised it (Ostrom: "co"→"to", "actlvely"→"actively", "cunditions"→"conditions"; Mauss: "magzster"→"master"), and I have changed nothing else. Any quotation that a reviewer intends to publish should be checked against a printed or library copy; I flag the two places where the OCR is materially uncertain (Mauss p. 72; Gouldner, translation).

**Why the transfer question is the point.** The Lead is building a typology of reciprocal practices and design conditions for durable exchange. The risk in this literature is not that it is wrong; it is that it is *borrowed too easily*. Ostrom's principles are the obvious candidate for a design checklist, Sahlins' "generalised reciprocity" is the obvious candidate for naming volunteer generosity, and "thick trust" is the obvious candidate for praising a network. Each of those borrowings fails for a specific, statable reason. Section 4 is the list of which concepts may be used and which may not.

## 2. Lens-by-lens: retrieved claim, quote, transfer assessment

### 2.1 Reciprocity as norm and as pattern — Gouldner 1960

**Level:** individual / interpersonal (a norm held by persons), with a claim about society-wide moral codes. **Depth:** full text, but only in Spanish translation; English original closed.

**[D] What the retrieved text says.** Gouldner's paper is explicitly a "preliminary statement" that (a) distinguishes reciprocity as a *pattern of exchange* from reciprocity as a *norm*, and (b) hypothesises a universal norm with two minimal demands. He also treats the *pattern* as something that can be described without any normative force attaching to it — the distinction the Lead needs, because "we observe exchange" and "the parties hold an obligation" are different claims.

> "En suma, más allá de la reciprocidad como un patrón de intercambio y más allá de las creencias de la reciprocidad como un hecho de vida, existe otro elemento: una norma moral generalizada de reciprocidad que define ciertas acciones y obligaciones como retribución de los beneficios recibidos." — G1, pp. 104–105 (Spanish translation; my rendering: *"In sum, beyond reciprocity as a pattern of exchange and beyond beliefs that reciprocity is a fact of life, there is another element: a generalised moral norm of reciprocity which defines certain actions and obligations as repayment for benefits received."*)

> "Específicamente, sugiero que una norma de reciprocidad, en su forma universal, realiza dos demandas mínimas interrelacionadas: (1) las personas deben ayudar a quienes lo ayudaron, y (2) las personas no deben injuriar a aquellos que la ayudaron." — G1, p. 105 (my rendering: *"(1) people should help those who have helped them, and (2) people should not injure those who have helped them."*)

He also insists the obligation is *contingent*, not unconditional, and that whether repayment must be *equivalent* is an empirical question, not a definitional one [D] (G1 p. 105: "dichas obligaciones de retribución son contingentes al valor imputado por el beneficio recibido"; and on equivalence, "es una cuestión empírica").

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** It licenses one precise diagnostic question for the typology: for any observed Eindhoven exchange, is it a *pattern* (goods/services/labour moved, describable without remainder) or is there evidence that the parties *hold a norm* — i.e. that non-repayment would be sanctioned by someone? That is a question the Lead can answer from documents and interviews, and it is the difference between "co-presence" and "reciprocity" that the case evidence currently turns on.

**What it does not license.** (i) Gouldner offers no evidence at all: the paper is a programme for functional analysis, with no data, no cases, no measurement — so it cannot support any claim that reciprocity norms *produce durability*. (ii) The universality claim is a hypothesis ("se puede hipotetizar"), and it is about *individuals' moral codes*, not about organisations, networks or governance. (iii) The translation problem is real: I have not seen the English wording, so the Lead should not quote English phrasing of Gouldner (e.g. "two interrelated, minimal demands") as if verified — that phrasing is a standard rendering I did *not* retrieve. (iv) Nothing in the text says a norm, once present, is stable or self-enforcing; Gouldner's own framing makes the norm's strength variable across cultures and statuses.

### 2.2 The three types — Sahlins, *Stone Age Economics* ch. 5

**Level:** interpersonal/group, in kin-based, non-monetary societies. **Depth:** full text, Routledge Classics 2017 edition, pp. 173–179.

**[D] The scheme.** Sahlins sets reciprocity on a continuum "based on the 'vice-versa' nature of exchanges", with three named positions (p. 175–177):

> *Generalized reciprocity* "refers to transactions that are putatively altruistic, transactions on the line of assistance given and, if possible and necessary, assistance returned." — S1, p. 175

> "A good pragmatic indication of generalized reciprocity is a sustained one-way flow." — S1, p. 175

> *Balanced reciprocity* "refers to direct exchange. In precise balance, the reciprocation is the customary equivalent of the thing received and is without delay." — S1, p. 176

> "So the pragmatic test of balanced reciprocity becomes an inability to tolerate one-way flows; the relations between people are disrupted by a failure to reciprocate within limited time and equivalence leeways." — S1, p. 176

> *Negative reciprocity* "is the attempt to get something for nothing with impunity, the several forms of appropriation, transactions opened and conducted toward net utilitarian advantage." — S1, p. 177

And the sentence that does the most analytic work for this issue, because it states *which way round* the causal arrow runs in each mode:

> "It is notable of the main run of generalized reciprocities that the material flow is sustained by prevailing social relations; whereas, for the main run of balanced exchange, social relations hinge on the material flow." — S1, p. 176

**[D] The mechanism he proposes for moving between modes** — social distance, not goodwill:

> "The span of social distance between those who exchange conditions the mode of exchange. … Reciprocity is inclined toward the generalized pole by close kinship, toward the negative extreme in proportion to kinship distance." — S1, p. 178

> "Equivalence becomes compulsory in proportion to kinship distance lest relations break off entirely, for with distance there can be little tolerance of gain and loss even as there is little inclination to extend oneself." — S1, p. 178

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** This is the cleanest vocabulary available for the Lead's typology, and it comes with a prediction that can be checked rather than merely applied: *because* the mode tracks social distance, a city-wide network of initiatives that are not kin, not neighbours and not long-acquainted should be expected to default to **balanced** reciprocity — explicit, time-bounded, equivalent — and to degrade to negative when equivalence is not enforceable. Sahlins' p. 176 sentence is the sharpest available statement of why the *same* act (a shared afternoon, a loaned tool, a volunteer crew) means different things at different distances: near, the relation carries the flow; far, the flow must carry the relation. For durable exchange between Eindhoven initiatives, this predicts that durability is bought by *making the equivalence explicit* (what, how much, by when), not by appeals to generosity.

**What it does not license.** (i) The empirical base is small-scale societies studied by mid-century anthropology; there are no formal organisations, no municipal counterparty, no money, no employees, no legal entities in his cases — so the transfer to a 24-hectare staffed estate and a 0.7-hectare volunteer group is analogy at two removes. (ii) "Generalised reciprocity" must **not** be used to redescribe volunteer labour, mutual help or gifts to a food forest. In Sahlins, generalised reciprocity is sustained by *kinship obligation* and produces "a sustained one-way flow" [D p. 175] — a volunteer's unpaid work is not a one-way flow within a kin group, and calling it generalised reciprocity pre-loads the conclusion that it will be sustained. (iii) Nothing in the chapter measures durability, exit, or what happens when a giver stops giving; "durable" is the Lead's question, not Sahlins'. (iv) Sahlins' own caution: empirical exchanges "often fall somewhere along the line, not directly on the extreme and middle points here outlined" [D p. 177–178] — so a typology that assigns cases to three boxes is already a simplification he warned against.

### 2.3 Gift exchange — Mauss, *The Gift*

**Level:** group/clan and inter-group (total prestation between clans, potlatch between chiefs). **Depth:** full text, Cunnison translation (Cohen & West 1954), pp. 11–12 and p. 72.

**[D] The three obligations:**

> "Total prestation not only carries with it the obligation to repay gifts received, but it implies two others equally important: the obligation to give presents and the obligation to receive them." — M1, pp. 11–12

**[D] The asymmetry point** — the one the Lead needs for "extractive rather than durable":

> "To give is to show one's superiority, to show that one is something more and higher, that one is master. To accept without returning or repaying more is to face subordination, to become a client and subservient, to become minister." — M1, p. 72 [OCR reads "magzster" for "master"; "minister" is as printed]

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** It licenses a structural claim that does real work against a "generosity is good" reading of the case: an unrepayable gift does not create equality, it creates **rank**. Applied to Eindhoven, the test is not "who gives?" but "**can the receiver repay, and in what currency?**" A staffed, funded organisation offering help it knows a volunteer group cannot match is, on Mauss' logic, producing clientship, whatever the intention — and that is the mechanism by which well-meant support becomes extractive *without anyone behaving badly*. It also licenses the third obligation, the **obligation to receive**: refusing a gift, or refusing to accept help, is itself a relational act (Mauss: refusal is "the equivalent of a declaration of war" [D p. 11]), which matters for initiatives that decline collaboration.

**What it does not license.** (i) Mauss' cases are Polynesian, Melanesian and Northwest Coast, and his explanatory apparatus includes the *hau*, the spirit of the thing given [D, ch. 2] — an ontological claim no one should import into a Dutch civic network. What transfers is the *obligation structure as an ideal type*, not the spirit. (ii) The gift in Mauss is between groups bound by enduring total relationships; a one-off collaboration between two foundations has none of that. (iii) The book is an essay in comparative sociology, not a study of voluntary association durability; it contains no evidence about what makes an exchange survive beyond the initial cycle. (iv) The translation is Cunnison's; the French *Essai sur le don* was not retrieved, so French terminology should not be quoted as verified.

### 2.4 Social exchange and power-dependence — Emerson (Blau, Homans behind him), and the critique

**Level:** dyad (persons, and by extension corporate actors). **Depth:** E1 full text; E2, C1, F1 abstracts; B1 not read.

**[D] The core proposition** — power as the mirror of dependence:

> "The premise we began with can now be stated as Pab=Dba; the power of A over B is equal to, and based upon, the dependence of B upon A." — E1, p. 33

Emerson's own abstract states the programme as deriving "a reciprocal power-dependence relation" and analysing "properties of balance and 'balancing operations'" [D, E1 p. 31 abstract]. Crucially, dependence in his scheme has two components — motivational investment in the relationship and availability of alternatives [D, E1 p. 33].

**[D] What the field says about itself.** Emerson, looking back: "theory is still growing; it still contains diversity and sparks of controversy" (E2, abstract). Cropanzano & Mitchell, reviewing it: "Despite its usefulness, theoretical ambiguities within SET remain. As a consequence, tests of the model, as well as its applications, tend to rely on an incompletely specified set of ideas" (C1, abstract).

**[D] A competing typology that bounds the exchange frame.** Fiske's four relational models include one in which reciprocity-as-bookkeeping is only one mode: "In communal sharing, people treat all members of a category as equivalent. In authority ranking, people attend to their positions in a linear ordering. **In equality matching, people keep track of the imbalances among them.** In market pricing, people orient to ratio values." (F1, abstract)

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** One thing, and it is the most useful single analytic instrument in this file: an **asymmetry test that does not require imputing motives**. A's power over B = B's dependence on A = B's motivational investment × B's lack of alternatives. Applied to the asymmetries already documented in this issue (a 145-hectare municipal estate with a staffed centre, a €9.8 m programme and €600 k a year, against a 0.7-hectare volunteer food forest on private land for which no municipal instrument exists), the prediction is that the weaker party's dependence is what makes the relation extractive — **structurally, not morally** — and that the remedy is *alternatives*, not goodwill. That reframing is worth carrying into the design conditions.

**What it does not license.** (i) SET assumes actors who calculate benefits and costs; that assumption is precisely what a care-based account denies, and Cropanzano & Mitchell's own complaint is that the model is under-specified rather than that it is wrong [D]. Using SET to explain a food forest's shared care would describe the case in a vocabulary the participants reject — an analogy that explains by redefinition. (ii) Fiske's abstract is evidence that *other* relational models exist and are treated as distinct (equality matching as turn-taking/bookkeeping; communal sharing as equivalence of persons), i.e. that reciprocity is a *mode*, not the ground of sociality [D for Fiske's claim; **I** for the use I make of it]. It is not evidence about which mode Eindhoven actors use. (iii) Blau's *Exchange and Power* — the source of the "reciprocity imbalance → power" argument in its fullest form — **I did not read** (401). Any claim attributed to Blau in the final note would be a claim I cannot support. (iv) Homans was not retrieved at all. (v) SET is a dyadic theory; organisations enter only by analogy, and "corporate actor" is an assumption, not a finding.

### 2.5 Commons governance — Ostrom's design principles and their critics

**Level:** collective of appropriators of a common-pool resource; nested units for larger systems. **Depth:** O1 full text (pp. 88–91); O2 and O3 full text (OA).

**[D] The principles, as printed** (O1, Table 3.1, p. 90) — I quote the five the Lead's brief asks about, because the *wording* matters for the transfer:

> "3. Collective-choice arrangements — Most individuals affected by the operational rules can participate in modifying the operational rules."
> "4. Monitoring — Monitors, who actively audit CPR conditions and appropriator behavior, are accountable to the appropriators or are the appropriators."
> "5. Graduated sanctions — Appropriators who violate operational rules are likely to be assessed graduated sanctions (depending on the seriousness and context of the offense) by other appropriators, by officials accountable to these appropriators, or by both."
> "6. Conflict-resolution mechanisms — Appropriators and their officials have rapid access to low-cost local arenas to resolve conflicts among appropriators or between appropriators and officials."
> "8. Nested enterprises — Appropriation, provision, monitoring, enforcement, conflict resolution, and governance activities are organized in multiple layers of nested enterprises." — O1, p. 90

**[D] Her own hedge, in the same chapter:**

> "This list of design principles is still quite speculative. I am not yet willing to argue that these design principles are necessary conditions for achieving institutional robustness in CPR settings. Further theoretical and empirical work is needed before a strong assertion of necessity can be made." — O1, p. 90

**[D] What the principles were derived from** — the table's own title is the transfer warning: "Design principles illustrated by long-enduring **CPR institutions**" (O1, p. 90), and the units throughout are *appropriators* withdrawing *resource units* from a bounded resource system (principle 1: "Individuals or households who have rights to withdraw resource units from the CPR must be clearly defined, as must be the boundaries of the CPR itself").

**[D] The critics.** Cox et al. reviewed 91 studies: "We found that the principles are well supported empirically and that several important theoretical issues warrant discussion. We provide a reformulation of the design principles, drawing from commonalities found in the studies" (O2, abstract) — i.e. support *and* revision, not a validated checklist. Saunders, more severely:

> "the individual 'rational resource user', encapsulated in the CPR design principles, struggles to provide clear direction for meaningful consideration of local norms, values and interests in commons projects." — O3, abstract

and, in the body, that CPR theory's "focus on efficiency and functionality results in a tendency in commons projects to overlook how local conditions are forged through relations at multiple scales" [D, O3 p. 636 abstract].

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** Three things, all of them *questions rather than answers*: (1) the principle set names the institutional functions any durable arrangement must discharge — who monitors, who may change the rules, where a conflict goes, whether sanctions are graduated — and a network of initiatives that has no answer to "where does a dispute go?" is missing something, whatever its resource base; (2) the *distinction between monitoring by accountable monitors vs external inspection* [D principle 4] is directly usable: a municipal officer counting volunteers is not Ostrom's monitor; (3) "nested enterprises" is the only principle that speaks to a *network* — and it speaks about nesting layers of the same governance activity, not about a network of independent peers.

**What it does not license — and this is the transfer the Lead must not make.** A network of initiatives is **not** a common-pool resource. There are no resource units being withdrawn, no defined appropriator set, no subtractable stock, and the "boundary" is an organisational membership, not an ecological one. Applying the eight principles as a checklist to the Eindhoven network would be exactly the move Saunders criticises [D, O3]: reducing politically complex, multi-scale relations to an institutional design problem for rational resource users. Two further limits: Ostrom's cases are *selected* for being long-enduring — the table's own title says so — and the principles are *post hoc* characterisations of survival, not tested interventions — so they cannot be used to predict that adopting them produces durability; and her own hedge [D, p. 90] removes any claim of necessity. A defensible use is: "Ostrom's principles generate the questions we should ask of any proposed reciprocal arrangement; they do not tell us the answers, and the answers must come from the Eindhoven evidence."

### 2.6 Thin vs thick trust

**Level:** individual (T1's generalised trust is a societal-level attitude held by persons); dyad (T2/T3's trust between parties). **Depth:** T1 full text; T2 abstract; T3 full text but secondary for L1; L1 not retrieved.

**[D] The distinction that is actually operational** — Uslaner separates trust earned from experience from trust learned as outlook:

> "Confidence in your stockbroker is strategic trust, which is based on experience. … Erikson's faith in others is generalized trust, which does not change readily." — T1, ch. 4, first page (running head "Chapter 4-2" marks the next page)

> "Trust must be learned, not earned. Generalized trust reflects our outlook on the world and is stable over time." — T1, ch. 4, running head "Chapter 4-2"

> "Attitudes toward your own group and to outsiders are the core of particularized trust." — T1, ch. 4, running head "Chapter 4-2"

**[D] The conditions and "bandwidth" of trust** — Rousseau et al.:

> "The 'bandwidth' of trust--where trust and distrust are differentiated--can vary over time in the same relationship or coexist at the same time. Bandwidth types are deterrence-based trust, calculus-based trust, relational trust, and institution-based trust. Two conditions of trust are risk and interdependence." — T2, abstract

**[D] The three levels, as summarised by a later paper** (secondary witness — L1 is the origin and I did not read it):

> "Calculus-based trust refers to the acceptance of a certain level of vulnerability based on the calculated costs of maintaining or severing a relationship." … "Knowledge-based trust … develops with time as a result of the track record of interactions that enables both parties to develop generalized expectations about the other's behavior." … "Identification-based trust is grounded in deep knowledge of the partner's desires and intentions." — T3, pp. 176–178

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** It licenses replacing the word "thick" with **four checkable properties**: (a) a track record of *specific* prior interactions with *this* partner (knowledge-based); (b) predictability derived from that record, not from reputation; (c) risk and interdependence actually present — no vulnerability, no trust to speak of (T2); (d) identification: shared values such that each can act on the other's behalf (identification-based). That converts "thick trust" from an adjective into a set of things the Lead can look for in the record — how many times these two parties have actually transacted, whether either could name the other's constraints, whether anything was put at risk. It also licenses a negative: generalised trust is an early-learned, stable outlook [D, T1], so **a network cannot manufacture it**; what a network can build is particularised, partner-specific trust — which is a different and smaller claim than "build trust in the ecosystem".

**What it does not license.** (i) Measuring trust from warm language in newsletters or interviews: the operationalisations above are survey instruments and dyadic scales, none validated for Dutch civic organisations, and none of them can be applied retrospectively to documents. (ii) Treating "thick trust" as a *network-level* property: every source here locates trust in persons or dyads, and T1 explicitly treats generalised trust as an individual disposition. (iii) Citing Lewicki & Bunker directly: I did not read the chapter, and T3 both summarises it *and* redefines "calculative trust" for its own buyer–seller model — so the three-level scheme should be attributed to L&B via a repeater, or dropped. (iv) Inferring durability from trust: none of these texts shows that high-trust relations last longer; that is the Lead's hypothesis, not a finding.

### 2.7 Care webs and mutual aid — Piepzna-Samarasinha, Spade

**Level:** small, close-knit networks of individuals; movement groups. **Depth:** P1 and P2 full text. **Genre:** normative and practical theory, disability-justice and anarchist traditions. **Not measurement.**

**[D] The durability claim in the care-web literature is about *specific relationships* and *asymmetric, consented* reciprocity:**

> "Fair trade femme disabled care webs are reciprocal." — P1 (chapter on fair-trade care; the passage goes on to describe asking for food, services, pet care or cash in exchange for emotional labour)

> "If these care webs are going to keep working, a paradigm shift needs to occur in those friends' heads." — P1 (on emergency-response care webs)

**[D] And the failure mode is stated plainly:**

> "The urgent care calendar care web lasts for a few weeks, a month. And then ... people trickle off." — P1

> "These emergency-response care webs often really fall apart when and if the person they're for becomes disabled in a long-term way" — P1

**[D] Spade's version of the same durability problem, with the boundary drawn against charity:**

> "We should be very clear: mutual aid is not charity. Charity, aid, relief, and social services are terms that usually refer to rich people or the government making decisions about the provision of some kind of support to poor people—that is, rich people or the government deciding who gets the help, what the limits are to that help, and what strings are attached." — P2, "Solidarity Not Charity"

> "Burnout is a reason people often give for why they leave mutual aid groups. Burnout is more than just exhaustion that comes from working too hard. … Burnout is the combination of resentment, exhaustion, shame, and frustration that make us lose connection to pleasure and passion in the work…" — P2, "Burnout"

> "Burnout is created or worsened when we feel disconnected from others, mistreated, misunderstood, ashamed, overburdened, obsessed with outcomes, perfectionist, or controlling. Burnout is prevented or lessened when we feel connected to others, when there is transparency in how we work together, when we can rest as needed, when we feel appreciated by the group, and when we have skills for giving and receiving feedback." — P2, "Burnout"

**[D] Institutional co-optation is named as a mechanism, not a mood:** Spade argues that "the nonprofit system creates a tax shelter for rich people" (P2) and that poverty-focused nonprofits "are essentially encouraged to merely manage poor people" (P2); elsewhere, groups are "urged to be single-issue oriented" (P2) [D]; Indigenous practices are undermined "by settler nonprofits" [D].

**[D] And the invisible-care-labour link, in Spade's own words** — a bridge to lens 2.8:

> "we are used to being part of groups that ignore ordinary caring labor, much of which is seen as women's work, like cooking and cleaning and conflict mediation, while celebrating only the final, outward-looking evidence of production: the big protest march, the finalized legislation, the release of someone from prison, the media coverage." — P2, part II, "Some dangers and pitfalls of mutual aid"

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** It licenses a *design condition* with a mechanism attached: durable care is **negotiated and bounded**, not unlimited — the care-web literature's practical rules (ask before offering, "no is okay", the receiver defines what counts as care, the giver defines what they can give) are rules for keeping an asymmetric relation from becoming extraction. And it licenses the strongest available warning about the case's own failure mode: burnout is produced by *conflict, invisibility and disconnection*, not by hours alone [D, P2] — which matches the documented organiser burnout in community gardens better than a "too much work" story does. Third, it licenses scepticism about institutionalisation: Spade's account of nonprofit funding shaping what groups may do is a mechanism by which a funded version of reciprocity stops being reciprocal.

**What it does not license.** (i) Any empirical claim: these are normative texts with no samples, no comparison and no outcome measurement; "care webs endure because…" is an argument, not a finding. (ii) Transfer of *scale*: the care web is a handful of intimates; the Eindhoven question is about relations between organisations with staff, boards and premises. (iii) Transfer of *context*: US disability-justice and anarchist milieus, with the state and the nonprofit sector as antagonists — a stance the Lead cannot assume for a Dutch municipality that is also a funder. (iv) The claim that "reciprocity that is not symmetrical" is a *solution*: read carefully, P1 and P2 are describing how to keep asymmetric care from becoming domination, which is a constraint on reciprocity, not an alternative to it. (v) P1's provenance: my copy is a community scan of a commercial book, so quotations should be checked against a library copy before publication.

### 2.8 Coordination labour / invisible work — Star & Strauss (abstract only)

**Level:** work practice, organisational. **Depth: abstract only.** The full text is paywalled at Springer; I did not bypass it.

**[D] What the retrieved abstract says** (and this is all I can support):

> "No work is inherently either visible or invisible. We always 'see' work through a selection of indicators: straining muscles, finished artifacts, a changed state of affairs. The indicators change with context, and that context becomes a negotiation about the relationship between visible and invisible work." — X1, abstract

The abstract also promises a framework sampling "a variety of kinds of work", with processes including "creating a 'non-person' in domestic work; disembedding background work; and going backstage" [D].

**[I] Transfer assessment — what it licenses.** Only a vocabulary and a question, not a finding. The abstract supports: visibility is *relational and negotiated*, not intrinsic; what counts as "the work" is selected by indicators that vary by context. That is enough to justify the Lead's lens — asking *which* labour in the Eindhoven network is counted (events, harvests, openings) and which is not (bookkeeping, permits, volunteer coordination, conflict mediation) — and to insist that invisibility is produced by *whose indicators are used*, not by the work's nature. What it does **not** license: the empirical claim that coordination work is disproportionately borne by one party in *this* case. I did not retrieve the article's body, so I cannot report its cases, its mechanisms, or its account of articulation work. If the final note needs that claim, it must rest on the case evidence (the documented administrative burden) and cite Star & Strauss only for the framing concept.

**Retrieval failure, recorded.** Springer article page returned 200 with the abstract and a subscription gate for the full text; the ACM DL result for the same DOI carried a Cloudflare challenge token (`__cf_chl_rt_tk`) in its URL, so I did not request it; Semantic Scholar's record is CLOSED with the abstract elided by the publisher; OpenAlex holds no abstract. An open copy was not found: the CiteSeerX hit resolved to a *different* Star paper (a 2002 LSE workshop paper on infrastructure), and I did not use it as a substitute. **[U]** The article's substantive content.

## 4. Concepts that may safely be used — and ones that may not

**Safe to use, with the stated hedge.**
1. *Reciprocity as pattern vs as norm* (G1) — as an analytic distinction, attributed, with the note that Gouldner's paper is a programme, not a study.
2. *Generalised / balanced / negative* (S1) — as a **spectrum keyed to social distance**, not as three boxes; explicitly flagged as drawn from kin-based, non-monetary societies.
3. *The obligation to give, to receive and to repay* (M1) — as ideal-typical structure; and *unrepayable gift → subordination* as the mechanism of unintended extraction.
4. *Power = dependence; dependence = investment × lack of alternatives* (E1) — as the asymmetry test, with alternatives named as the remedy.
5. *Ostrom's principles as diagnostic questions* (O1), together with the critics' warning that the principles presuppose rational appropriators of a bounded resource (O2, O3).
6. *Thin/thick trust operationalised* as calculus-based vs knowledge-based vs identification-based, with risk and interdependence as conditions (T1, T2, T3-via-L1).
7. *Care as negotiated and bounded* — "no is okay", the receiver defines the care, the giver defines capacity (P1); *burnout as conflict- and invisibility-driven* (P2).
8. *Visibility is relational and negotiated* (X1, abstract-level only).

**Not safe — do not let these into the note.**
1. "Ostrom's design principles explain/predict durable commons governance" — she disclaims necessity herself (O1 p. 90), and the CPR transfer is contested (O3).
2. "The network is a commons" — it has no resource units, no appropriator boundary; this is a metaphor masquerading as a finding.
3. "Generalised reciprocity" as a description of volunteer giving or mutual help in Eindhoven.
4. "Thick trust" as an unanalysed compliment, or as something a network can be said to *have*.
5. "Social exchange explains shared care" — the calculative assumption is the thing the case's own language denies, and the theory's own reviewers call it under-specified (C1).
6. Any citation of Blau (1964), Homans, Lewicki & Bunker (1996), or the body of Star & Strauss (1999): **not read**. Cite them only as "not retrieved" or through the repeater named in the table.
7. Any English-language quotation of Gouldner: I saw only a Spanish translation.
8. Any figure, prevalence or effect size from any source in this file: the only quantitative statements retrieved are (a) Cox et al.'s "91 such studies" (O2) and (b) Ostrom's table of principles. There is no measurement of reciprocity, durability or trust anywhere in these seven lenses.

## 5. What the literature cannot do for this question (stated as a limit, not a gap to fill)

1. **No source studies reciprocity between formal organisations in a Dutch city.** The nearest is Ostrom on CPR appropriators — a different unit — and SET on dyads. The typology will therefore be built by *analogy plus case evidence*, and the analogy's load-bearing assumptions (comparable units, repeated interaction, an exit option) must be argued for Eindhoven, not imported.
2. **No source measures durability.** Sahlins describes modes, not survival; Ostrom characterises long-enduring cases *after the fact*; Mauss describes cycles; the care-web texts argue normatively. "Durable" remains the Lead's construct, and the only evidence about it in this issue is the case record (twelve years, then closure).
3. **The "who bears the coordination cost" lens is the weakest link in this file.** The concept is retrievable only at abstract level, and the practical tradition (P1, P2) supplies the *normative* version — invisible care labour, burnout — not a measured distribution. If the note asserts that coordination cost is unequally borne in the Eindhoven network, that assertion must rest on the case documents.
4. **Two of the seven lenses rest on one text each, read in translation or in summary.** Any reviewer testing this file should test G1 (translation) and X1 (abstract) first.
